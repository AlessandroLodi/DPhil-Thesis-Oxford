\section{Introduction}\label{gnrset:sec:intro}

The electron tunnelling in a molecular or solid-state junction depends on both the bias applied and the temperature. Tipically, two regimes are distinguished. At low temperatures the electrons tunnell across the junction independently of the temperature; thus the conductance does not depend on the temperature. If the temperature is increase, the electrons will be preferentially udnergo through a thermally-activated hopping mechanism, resulting in a dependence of the conductance with the temperature. The origin of thermal excitations and their respective role played on the tunnelling proccess remaines largely unexplored in graphene nanostructures.

Recent experiments on graphene have shown the possibility of using graphene graphenoids (also knows as radicaloids) for quantum information processing~\cite{lombardi2019quantum, lombardi2021synthetic}. The advantage of these systems over molecular counterparts are multiple. Being delocalised molecular structures uniquely based on carbon and hydrogen atoms, the spin-orbit coupling is typically low and isotropic, in the order of a few microvolts. This is one of the factors which determines coherence times in the order of a few tens of microseconds even at temperatures below 100 K. Another robustness factor is that the mechanisms governing the dechorence for the lattice relaxation times are relatively well understood when these molecules are placed in solutions.

A great deal of experimental and theoreitcal efforts have been put into understanding electronic transport in single molecules. The advent of quantum technologies have posed a few guidelines on what the future electronics devices should look like. The setup should be such to work at temperatures beyond the cryogenics. The scalability of the qubits is required also to be independent to the number of nearest neighbours a single qubit is coupled to. Thirdly, the coherence times should exceed the maximum required time for the slowest fundamental quantum operations.

The advent of increasingly advanced lithographic technologies since the 1990s and the substantial theoretical drive supporting experimental fervours have made these single-electron transistors -- and, more generally, semiconductor charge and spin qubits -- potential criteria-meeting candidates \cite{hanson2007spins, chatterjee2021semiconductor}. While it is far from be clear what could potentially be the most suitable material, several proposals -- first theoretical at the dawn of the 90s, and then experimental after the discovery of graphene in 2004 -- have envisaged graphene nanoribbons.

In the present chapter, I report, to the best of my knowledge, the first realisation of room-temperature single-electron transistor made with \gls{MGNR}. I use a range of source-drain bias and gate voltages in a two-terminal back-gated device to study the temperature dependence from cryogenic up to room temperatures of three conduction regimes. The measurements have been carried out in the setup detailed in Ch.~\ref{ch:theory} where an acquisition scheme has been developed to work with a Oxford Instrument dilution refrigerator.

\section{Sample Preparation and Characterisation}\label{gnrset:sec:chara}

\Glspl{GNR} were received from the Feng's groups located in Dresden in powders in a sealed vial. Graphene nanostructures powders are notoriously electrostatic and tend to stick along the vials walls. An approximately $1.0 \pm 0.1$ mg of powder was scooped and transffered into a vial with the help of an electrostic gun to ease the remove of residul material on the scoop.

The powder was then dissolved in $10.0 \pm 0.2$ mL of toluene. Initially, it was attempted to solubilise \glspl{GNR} by entirely avoiding sonications. In the literature it has been reported that heavy sonication treatements might damage the tube integratity in carbon nanotubes, resulting in poor electrical conductivity and optical properties \cite{park2002dispersion, huang2012dispersion, rossell2013impact}. Although similar effects can take place in graphene nanoribbons, the following qualitative arguments were taken into account for the sample preparation.
After having mechanically agitated the solution over a week, black aggregates identifies as \glspl{GNR} oligomers suspended in solution were visible. In order to overcome stacking energies, a mild sonication treatmenet was evalueated to be the best alternative. In talks with our collaborators, it was discussed that a $\sim$1h is required for graphene nanoribbons decorated with aliphatic alkyl chains along the edges. Surprisingly, \glspl{GNR} decorated with maleimide take approximately 10 minutes at 50 \% of relative sonication power to produce an almost homogenous dispersion visible to the naked eye. These enhanced solubility properties are further analysed and discussed over the next chapters.

In order to reduce further the chance of having medium and small oligomers, the mother solution was diluted at different concentrations. Since weigth-average molecular weight $M_\textrm{W}$ and polydispersity index (PDI) were not known upon arrival, the dilution protocol was as followed. A mother solution volume of $1.00 \pm 0.01$ mL was transferred on a second vial, were $10 \pm 0.02$ mL of toluene were added, followed by a 5 minutes sonication treatement at 50 \% of relative power (different uncertainties are associated with different syringes used). Sonicator bath temperature was set at 330 K. The dilution processed was repeated four times, obtaining a range of concentrations from 1:10 up to 1:10000. While the mother solution and the 1:10 diluted one display a Pantone 191 colour, the most diluted ones are completely transparent. The coloration of the solutions obtained is in line with what reported by the collaborators, confirming, therefore, a good preservation of the optical and photochemical properties of the ribbons.
A more detailed quantitative discussion is provided in the next chapter.

Molecular devices were fabricated using well-know fabrication techniques in molecular electronics and as detailed already in Ch.~\ref{ch:fabrication}. Molecular \glspl{GNR} are added on top of graphene electrode where tunnel junctions have been formed by feedback electroburning means by submerging the whole chip in the 1:10000 solution for 30 minutes.

The devices are then measured at room temperature with an automated probe station to check which devices display possible molecular features. Given the high variability of molecular devices and the absence of experimental data on these kind of ribbon, the criteria I have used to decide which devices may exhibit electric characterstics likely attributable to those of graphene nanoribbons can be found in the literature \cite{limburg2018anchor, lortscher2007statistical, reus2012statistical}.

Across several depositions and post deposition measurements, it is possible to drawn the following observations.
Firstly, when the feedback electroburning program fails to burn correctly a junctions, the $I-V_\textrm{sd}$ characterstics are ohmic with resistance values in the few k$\Omega$ range. Conversely, unsuccessful electroburning runs will produce gaps within the hundrends of G$\Omega$. Consequently, these devices are discarded from further investigations.
Secondly, for a completely empty junction the source-drain conductance $G_\textrm{sd}$ is generally almost undetectable in the room temperature setup; when a MGNR bridges the electrodes, in any configuration, the conductance values range within tens and hundrends of nS range. Although with this procedure it is not possible to control the orientation of the ribbons on the substrate, we believe that a preference for anchoring may be promoted.
In particular, intermolecular Van der Waals forces play a crucial role in building strong $\pi^*-\pi$ interactions between the $p_z$ delocalised orbitals of graphene and MGNRs. For having a flat bridging of the nanoribbons on top of the graphene leads these interactions $\Delta E_{\pi-\pi}$ should first be greater than the buckle up energy $\Delta E_\textrm{bundling}$. In the present discussion $\Delta E$ are to be understood as Gibbs free energy: the change in notation respect to the thermodynamics literature is adopted in order to not confuse Gibbs free energies with the electrical conductance $G$.
From a chemical point of view, the adsorption of \glspl{GNR} on the substrate can be seen as a chemical reaction between the ribbon dispersed in mobile (liquid) phase absorbed on a substrate:

% taken from 
% https://tex.stackexchange.com/questions/10166/how-do-i-position-pairs-of-arrows-in-tikz-to-make-harpoons
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:absorption}
  \textrm{MGNR}_\textrm{(sol)} \xrightleftharpoons[k_{-1}]{k_{+1}} \textrm{MGNR}_\textrm{(ads)}
\end{equation}

Here $k_1$ and $k_{-1}$ are the adsorption and desorption rate constants, respectively, and, in case of a one-step process, have the same magnitude at the equilibrium. In cases where the adsorption equilibrium is reached very quickly, i.e. $k_1 \gg k_{-1}$, the rate of formation of the adsorbed layer is controlled by mass transfer. Therefore, the process is subject to the conditions applying to mass transfer.

Mass transfer can occur through mainly three mechanism: migration, diffusion, and convection. Migration is the movement of a charged body subject to an electric field (a gradient of electrical potential); in what follow we exclude \glspl{GNR} hosting a net charge. Diffusion drives the movement of the ribbon in presence of a concentration gradient. By assuming an homogeneous dispersion resulted from high-steric-hindrance-kind edge decorations, this contribution can be regarded as small compared to convection. Natural (density gradients) and forced convections are characterised by stagnant regions, laminar and turbulent flows. Therefore, we argue that deposition of graphene nanoribbons is mainly driven by mass transfer convection mechanism towards the chip surface.

To validate these claims, a simple and obvious experiment consists of varying the solvent in which the graphene nanoribbons are dispersed, using increasingly high-boiling solvents and qualitatively observing changes in the spatial distribution of the ribbon on the substrate. Although it has not been possible to perform a more quantitative characterisation to test this hypothesis -- such as an AFM study on the chip -- if the solvent evaporates quickly, the flux of ribbons dispersed will rapidly migrate will result in a less homogeneous dispersion, favoruing buclking up and the formation of big GNR oligomers \cite{limburg2018anchor}.

\section{Electrical Transport Measurements}\label{gnrset:sec:measure}
Dilution refrigerators work very well in the cryogenic temperature range and they enable temperature control up to a certain temperatures defined by fridge heaters. In order to scan the entire temperature spectrum range up to room temperature, however, one must ensures that the temperature drift is ideally slower than the time taken to acquire experimental measurements. Since there is no well-defined experimental procedure for doing so, a workaround which leverages Triton system was found and used in this thesis.

Triton heaters are capable to control the temperature precisely from base temperature ($\sim$ 25 mK) up to 30 K. In order to stabilise the temperature above this range, one has to generate a thermal link between the sample and the environment. First the heaters (mixing chamber and still, see Ch.~\ref{ch:theory}) are switched off: this enables main heat sources to be excluded from exchanging thermal energy with the sample inside the mixing chamber. Being the mixing chamber very well thermally isolated, the temperature will start raising very slowly: to give a reference number, $\sim$24h after the heaters were switched off the temperature rose by only 2 K. The pre-cooling loop is then filled with the He3/He4 mixture, creating a thermal link between the various stages within the dilution refrigerator and the mixing chamber. This results in a faster heating rate, but slow enough to allow to take quick measurements such as $I-V_\textrm{sd}$ and $I-V_\textrm{g}$ traces.

While these kind of measurements measure the current flowing through the device (after the voltage to current conversion that occurs inside the Delft box, see Ch.~\ref{ch:theory}), it is generally reccommended to acquire also the conductance. In absence of a lock-in apparatus, a possible workaround is to measure the current at various bias voltages and different gate voltage and differentiate numerically the current to obtain the conductance. In order to stabilise the temperature and remove the thermal link between the mixing chamber and the various refrigerator stages, the pre-cooling loop in then emptied, leaving the sample thermally isolated. The whole procedure was then automated in a python python script measurements, which algorithm is detailed in Alg.~\ref{alg:cap}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Triton High Temperature Measurement}\label{alg:cap}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require Initialise Alazar, Triton and IVVI
    \Ensure Alazar, Triton, and IVVI are global
    \State Initialise Final Temperature
    \State Initialise Bias Array
    \State Initialise Target Temperatures Array
    \State Current Temperature $\gets$ Read Temperature
    \State Temperature Step $\gets$ 3
    \State Temperature Channel $\gets$ 8
    \State Precooled $\gets$ \texttt{False}
    \While{Current Temperature $<$ Final Temperature}
    \State Precooled $\gets$ \texttt{True}
    \State T $\gets$ Read Temperature
    \If{T $> 1.5$ }
    \State Temperature Channel $\gets$ 5
    \EndIf
    \For{length Bias Array}
    \State Run Gate Trace Measurement
    \EndFor
    \State T $\gets$ Read Temperature
    \If{T is in Target Temperatures Array}
    \State Precooled $\gets$ \texttt{False}
    \State Run Stability Diagram
    % \State $N \gets \frac{N}{2}$  \Comment{This is a comment}
    \EndIf
    \EndWhile
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The Alazar card, Triton, and IVVI (Delft box modules) drivers are first initialised and assigned to global variables which hold their state throughout the program execution. Then several variables are initialised. The precooled variables is a routine implemented directly on top of Triton drivers which allows to set and get command directly on the instruments. While below 1 K the RuO$_2$ thermoter is used, to reliably read the temperature above this temperature one has to switch to the Cernox thermometer, hence we are setting the temperature channel variable to channel 5 within the while loop in case this conditions applies. The program then acquires gate trace and stability diagram measurements. These are functions which have been implemented and improved from scripts written by a former member of the group, Dr.\ Nicola Dotti.

The results of the measurement protocol are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:all_sd}. The stability diagram map reveals six Coulomb diamonds regions clearly distinguishable up to, at least, 180 K within the experimental gate voltage range. At sufficiently low energies or temperatures, then, a finite graphene nanoribbons behaves like a quantum dot, similar to what has been observed for carbon nanotubes \cite{bockrath1997single, nygaard2000kondo,buitelaar2002multiwall, postma2001carbon, liang2002shell, guinea2005many}.

Gate voltage outside the shown region resulted in strong leakage currents, likely due to substrate breakdown, possibly caused after the wirebonding process. Therefore, we restricted the measurement range between $\SI{-20}{\V}\le V_\textrm{g}\le\SI{+20}{\V}$. Since outside this range the current will be fully dominated by charges across dioxide substrate, and while formally further charge states could be visible outside the experimental window, it is still correct to assign the region at the left of the leftmost diamond as associated with the neutral state of the graphene nanoribbon; from this, it follows that the diamond located at $V_\textrm{g}\approx\SI{-19}{\V}$ is associated with the charge transition $N \rightarrow N+1$ and the rightmost diamond is associated with the $N+6 \rightarrow N+7$ transition.

From these \glspl{SD} it is not possible to distinguish whether the the charge states are well defined or not, given below 150 K they seem not closing between $\SI{-0.1}{\V}\le V_\textrm{sd}\le\SI{+0.1}{\V}$. High-resolution temperature data taken at 25 mK applying higher bias voltages $\SI{-0.2}{\V}\le V_\textrm{sd}\le\SI{+0.2}{\V}$, however, confirms that diamonds are closing. Therefore, as a result, charge states host well defined charges which can be assigned, as reported in similar studies, to graphene nanoribbons quantum dots \cite{el2020controlled, sols2007coulomb, todd2009quantum}. From 150 K up to room temperature, height and width of current suppressions regions becomes narrower and shorter while preserving the Coulomb blockade regime: this can be explained as a result of the leads' Fermi-Dirac distribution smoothing, which consequently leads to a high number of electrons jumping inside the junction region due to the thermal energy.


Fig.~\ref{fig:waterfall} depicts a set of $I-V_\textrm{g}$ curves measured on the same sample at different temperatures at different bias voltages. From low temperature data, it possible to estimate the lever arms associated with the various charge states: these quantify by how much in units of meV it is possible to change the chemical potential of the various molecular/quantum dot levels of  by varying the gate potential by a quantity in units of V, $\beta|e| V_\textrm{g}=$ $|e| V_\textrm{sd}$, with $\beta$ being the lever arm as defined $\beta=1 /\left(\left|\alpha_\textrm{+}\right|^{-1}+\left|\alpha_\textrm{-}\right|^{-1}\right)$, from the slopes of the diamond edges \cite{cuevas2010molecular}; $\alpha_\textrm{-}=-|e| C_\textrm{g} /\left(C-C_\textrm{s}\right)$ and $\alpha_\textrm{+}=|e| C_\textrm{g} / C_\textrm{s}$ are the negative and positive slopes of each diamonds, respectively, and $C_\textrm{s}$ is the total capacitance calcualted according the constant interaction model (see Ch.~\ref{ch:theory}).

The height of the diamonds reads the bias voltage necessary to add an electron to the ribbon, which defines an addition energy $E_\textrm{add} = e^2/C_\textrm{s} + \Delta E$, where $\Delta E$ is the energy difference between the energy levels. Energy levels are here associated with the quantum states of the ribbon in its quantum dot configuration. Although the length of a \gls{MGNR} is molecularly defined, its quantum dot configuration depends strongly on the coupling with the leads. Therefore, the $\Delta E$ read from a first-principle calculation and the one measured experimentally may differ significantly. An average $\overline{E_\textrm{add}} =  156.7 \pm 2.6$ meV was extracted from the heights of all the six suppressed current regions, which is higher than previosuly reported for room-temperature operating single-electron quantum dots in semiconductors \cite{zhuang1998silicon} and carbon nanotubes \cite{postma2001carbon}.

Low temperature data allows also to quantify the coupling between the finite \gls{GNR} and the leads. The $\SI{0.025}{K}$ zero-bias gate trace are fitted with a Breit-Wigner model which accounts that the \gls{GNR} resonant states are broadened as exclusively by lifetime broadening \cite{beenakker1991theory}:
\begin{equation}
  G = \frac{2e^2}{h}\qty(\frac{1}{\Gamma^\textrm{S}} + \frac{1}{\Gamma^\textrm{D}})^{-1}\frac{\Gamma/2}{(h\Gamma)^2 + \beta^2(V_\textrm{g}^\textrm{max} - V_\textrm{g})^2}
\end{equation}

Since the \gls{FWHM} is $\Gamma$ and by approximating $\Gamma\approx\Gamma^\textrm{S}=\Gamma^\textrm{D}$, we find the experimental value for the tunnelling coupling being $\overline{\Gamma}\sim 128 \pm \SI{8}{\micro\eV}$, where the average was calculated across all the distinct charge transitions.

Given the graphene electrodes have low electrostatic screening, the main contribution to the total capacitance is given by the capacitive coupling of the \gls{MGNR} to the back gate, so we can approximate $C_\textrm{s}\approx C_\textrm{g}$. By using reported values in the literature \cite{han2010electron} $C_\textrm{g}\approx \SI{700}{\atto\farad}/\mu\textrm{m}^2$, we estimate \mccorrect{$\Delta E < \SI{100}{\meV}$}.

The conductance shows a power-law dependence on $T$, as displayed in Fig.~\ref{fig:power_fit_10mv} where $G_\textrm{max}(T)$ is plotted for the distinct transistion charges. The quantities have been transformed into the respective logarithms, such that the parameter estimation for the power-law is transformed into a much simpler linear regression problem.

As the limited sample size restricts the validity of law of large numbers, it is incorrect to estimate the prediction limits and the confidence interval as if we were drawing the experimental data from a Gaussian distribution. Thus, prediction limits are calculated from the model estimates as reported in \cite{friedman2001elements, barlow1993statistics}:
\begin{equation}
  S^2_\textrm{pred}(G_\textrm{max}) = \hat\sigma^2\frac{n}{n-2}\qty(1 + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(G_\textrm{max} - \overline{G_\textrm{max}})}{n~S_{G_\textrm{max}}^2}),
\end{equation}
In general the prediction interval will be narrower when the sample size $n$ is large and the value predicted by the model $G_\textrm{max}$ is near the sample mean $\overline{G_\textrm{max}}$.
The variance here has two main contribution: the true noise variance about the
regression line ($\sigma^2$), plus the term coming from the uncertainty in where the regression line lies $S_{G_\textrm{max}}^2$.

In Tab.~\ref{tab:integrated_conductance_exponents_10K_to_72K} we compared the estimate for the integrated conductance $G^*$ from a standard (weak) linear regression method against the Siegel Repeated median estimator \cite{siegel1982robust}. The reason why we believe is worth comparing both regression method is doublefold.

The limited number of datapoints and the technical challenges associated with thermalising the mixing chamber at high temperature introduce noise and source of errors. An experimental workaround would involve the usage of an instrumentation apparatus with higher signal-to-noise ratio, such as a lock-in amplifier. At the stage when these measurements were perfomed, our custom-made electronics setup had several technical challenges to overcome in order to correctly read the lock-in signal. Therefore, the best DC technique available was to acquire the numerical differential conductance $G_\textrm{sd}$ by measuring the current for a range of bias and gate voltages.

The second motivation is that, for a tunnelling experimental in graphene nanondevices, experimental evidences are strong that conductance values measured are -- statistically speaking -- not drawn from a simple Normal distribution, but are likely to be drawn from a Dirac Billiard type of statistics \cite{da2019klein, ponomarenko2008chaotic}. In particular, the Hamiltonian of the system is a member of the Wigner-Dyson Ensemble with a Gaussian probability distribution, which must also satisfy the fundamental anti-commutation rules imposed by the chiral symmetry of Dirac quasiparticles (Chiral Ensemble). These considerations have been proved experimentally and have assigned a Dyson index $\beta = 1$\footnote[2]{The Dyson index is, technically speaking, the number of real components for each matrix elements: $\beta = 1$ for real and corresponds to the Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble, $\beta = 2$ for complex and corresponds to the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble, and $\beta = 4$ for quaternions and corresponds to the Gaussian Simpletic Ensemble.} for tunnelling experimental in graphene quantum dots.

The height of the diamonds reads the bias voltage necessary to add an electron to the ribbon, which defines an addition energy $E_\textrm{add} = e^2/C_\textrm{s} + \Delta E$, where $\Delta E$ is the energy difference between the energy levels. Energy levels are here associated with the quantum states of the ribbon in its quantum dot configuration. Although the length of a \gls{MGNR} is molecularly defined, its quantum dot configuration depends strongly on the coupling with the leads. Therefore, the $\Delta E$ read from a first-principle calculation and the one measured experimentally may differ significantly. An average $\overline{E_\textrm{add}} =  157.43\pm 5.65$ meV was extracted from the heights of all the six suppressed current regions, which is higher than previosuly reported for room-temperature operating single-electron quantum dots in semiconductors \cite{zhuang1998silicon} and carbon nanotubes \cite{postma2001carbon}.
