\section{Graphene}\label{intro:sec:graphene}

\subsection{crystal structure}
Graphene has a hexagonal structure where the carbon atoms are hybridised $sp^2$, as detailed by the Pauling theory on chemical bonds. Based on the experimental evidence collected in the last 20 years, the model
that seems to best describe the proprierties of graphene is the band-structure Wallace model, which dates back in 1947, and describes graphene as a semimetal with a linear dispersion of the electronic excitations.
In the International Table of Crystallography, graphene is included in the symmorphic space group \#191, with symmetry $D^1_6h$ ($P6/mmm$).
The triangular lattice is obtained by considering two atoms per unit cell having the respective lattice vectors:

The carbon atoms in graphene form a two-dimensional hexagonal crystal with two atoms per unit cell, as shown \mccorrect{in figure}. 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_lattice_vectors}
    \vect{a}{_1} = \frac{a}{2}(3, \sqrt{3}),~ 
    \vect{a}{_2} = \frac{a}{2}(3, -\sqrt{3}),
\end{equation}

with $a\approx1.42$ \si{\angstrom} be the carbon-carbon bond length. 
Each atom belonging to one sublattice, called sublattice A, has three first neighbours belonging to the other sublattice, called sublattice B. With respect to one atom chosen from sublattice A as origin, 
the position of these first three neighbours in real space are given by: 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_first_neighbours}
    \vect{\delta}{_1} = \frac{a}{2}(1, \sqrt{3}),~ 
    \vect{\delta}{_2} = \frac{a}{2}(1, -\sqrt{3}),~
    \vect{\delta}{_3} = -a(1,0),
\end{equation}

The reciprocal-lattice vectors are then given

\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_reciprocal_vectors}
    \vect{b}{_1} = \frac{2\pi}{3a}(1, \sqrt{3}),~ 
    \vect{b}{_2} = \frac{2\pi}{3a}(1, -\sqrt{3}),
\end{equation}

In order to define the vertices of the Brillouin zone one has to apply the Bragg condition:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Bragg}
    2\vect{k}\cdot\vect{G} = 0
\end{equation}

where 

\begin{equation}
    \vect{G} = n\vect{b}_1 + m\vect{b}_2,
\end{equation}

The first amongst these are:

\begin{align*}
    n &= \pm 1,  & m&=0,      & \vect{G}&=\pm\vect{b}_1, \\
    n &= 0,      & m&=\pm 1,  & \vect{G}&=\pm\vect{b}_2, \\
    n &= \pm 1,  & m&=\pm 1,  & \vect{G}&=\pm(\vect{b}_1+\vect{b}_2), 
\end{align*}

and all these vectors have the same modulus $\abs{\vect{G}} = \frac{4\pi}{3a}$. By applying Bragg's condition in the first of the three cases we get:

\begin{equation}
    \sqrt{3}k_{x} + k_{y} = \mp~\frac{4\pi}{3a},
\end{equation}

which gets satisfied by the points:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:K_point_components}
    K \equiv\mp~\frac{2\pi}{3a}\qty(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, 1)
\end{equation}

For the second case one gets:

\begin{equation}
    \mp(\sqrt{3}k_{x} - k_{y}) = \frac{4\pi}{3a},
\end{equation}

which gives the points:

\begin{equation}
    K' \equiv\pm~\frac{2\pi}{3a}\qty(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, -1)
\end{equation}

The third and last case gives the point:

\begin{equation}
    M \equiv\pm\qty(0, \pm~\frac{2\pi}{3a})
\end{equation}


\subsection{band dispersion}
The electronic structure of graphene can be model with a tigh-binding hamiltonian. 
Following the second quantization formalism ($\hbar = 1$ units), the hamiltonian can be written as:

\begin{equation}
    H = -t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,~\sigma}\qty(a^\dagger_{\sigma,i}b_{\sigma,j} + \text{H.c.}) - t' \sum_{\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle,\sigma}\qty(a^\dagger_{\sigma,i}a_{\sigma,j}+b^\dagger_{\sigma,i}b_{\sigma,j}+ \text{H.c.}),
\end{equation}
The notation $\langle ij\rangle$ means that the sum runs over pairs of electrons which are nearest neighbour; similarly, the notation $\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle$ specify a summation over the next nearest neighbours; 
the operators $a^\dagger_{i,\sigma}$ ($a_{i,\sigma}$) and $b^\dagger_{i,\sigma}$ ($b_{i,\sigma}$) creates (annihilates) an electron with spin $\sigma$ ($\sigma=~\uparrow,\downarrow$) for atoms on sublattice A and B, respectively. 
The value of $t$ which reproduces best experimental result is $t\approx2.8$ \si{\electronvolt} and quantify the nearest-neighbour hopping energy; similarly, $t'$ denotes the next nearest-neighbour hopping energy. The dispersion energy computed from this model reads:

\begin{equation}\label{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands}
    E_{\pm}(\vect{k}) = \epsilon_\text{p} - t'f(\vect{k}) \pm t\sqrt{3 + f(\vect{k})},
\end{equation}

with $f(\vect{k})$ defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands_fk}
    f(\vect{k})=2\cos\qty(\sqrt{3}k_ya) + 4\cos\qty(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}k_ya)\cos\qty(\frac{3}{2}k_xa).
\end{equation}

Therefore, it is possible to identify an upper ($\pi*$) band associated with the plus sign and a lower ($\pi$) band described by the minus sign; these are symmetric with respect to the single-particle zero energy.
The experimental estimate reported for next nearest-neighbour hopping energy is $t'\approx0.1$\si{\electronvolt} thus the asymmetry introduced is small. It is worthwhile to notice that the function $3 + f(\vect{k})$

If the system is not doped, there is one electron per carbon atom, so two electrons per cell: it follows that the lower band is full and the upper band is empty the upper one is empty. The system is therefore a semi-metal.
The peculiarities of the dispersion in graphene become apparent when analysing their trend near the point \vect{K} and \vect{K'}. 
By taking the positive components for the \vect{K} as define in Eq.~\ref{eq:K_point_components} and expanding the 
Eq.~\ref{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands} as $\vect{k} = \vect{K} + \vect{q}$, with $\abs{(\vect{q})}\ll\abs{(\vect{K})}$, one gets:
\begin{equation}
    E_{\pm}(\vect{q}) = \epsilon_p \pm \frac{3at}{2}\abs{\vect{q}}
\end{equation}
which is linear in \vect{q}. For intrinsic graphene, at $T = 0 $K $E_F = \epsilon_p$. The Fermi velocity is defined by the usuale relation $v_F = \frac{1}{\hbar}\qty(\dv{E}{\vect{k}})$, 
and in graphene $v_F\simeq1\times 10^6$\si{\metre\per\second}.  

A difference arises in the density of the states, when we limit ourselves to consider those near the Fermi energy. 
In the case of a two-dimensional electron gas, it is constant. On the contrary for graphene, the density of states per unite cell is:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:grafene:density_of_states}
    \rho(E)=\frac{2A_\text{cell}}{\pi}\frac{\abs{E}}{v_F^2}
\end{equation}

If the graphene is charge-neautral, the DOS has two Van Hove singularities at $\omega_\text{VH} = \pm~1$\si{\electronvolt} \cite{bena2005quasiparticle}. This behaviour is in stark constrat with the DOS 
of carbon nanotubes and clean graphene nanoribbons, as they show $1/\sqrt{E}$ singularities due to the 1D nature, which imposes a quantization perpendicular to the ribbon or tube longitudinal direction.

\section{Graphene Nanoribbons}

\co{gnr were first studied by physicist, lather by chemist}
The scientific interest for graphene nanoribbons started in the physics community. Although this may appear irrelevant at the first glance, we will see that this actually had an impact over the last twenty years of progress in the field.
The physics community was looking for a way to open a bandgap in graphene. As mentioned in Sec.~\ref{intro:sec:motivation}, graphene is a semi-metal, therefore unsuitable for all those semiconducting applications where the conduction paths for the charge carriers are well defined. 

The first class of methods for synthesising graphene nanoribbons are methods that involve cutting or producing rectangular shapes from the graphene lattice through macroscopic methods. 
For this reason, they are also known as \textit{top-down} approaches. Although they have seen an improvements in recent years, common drawbacks for this class of methods are a random distribution in the widths, a non-atomical control of the edge structures, and non-negligible 
impurity concentrations left after the process.

\co{ebl and etched gnrs present randomly distributed widths}
Kim and co-workers synthesized GNRs by employing the electron-beam lithography and etching technique~\cite{RN345}.
The widths of GNRs obtained by this technique were scattered over a broad range, and most of the edges were extremely rough.
An 8 nm GNR FET showed a dramatically improved on/off ratio of about 160.


\co{cutting graphene with nanoparticles leads to a random distribution of the widths}

Nanocutting of graphene is another promising method of GNR synthesis. In 2008, Strachan et al. used thermally activated Fe nanoparticles as chemical scissors to cut graphene sheet along a specific crystallographic direction~\cite{RN345,RN346}. Later, Ni and other catalysts were employed as well 
The carbon-carbon bond breaking is driven by the combustion reaction \ce{Ni + C_{\text{graphene}} + 2H2 ^ -> Ni/Fe + CH4 ^} catalysed by the nanoparticles, thus producing hydrocarbon compounds. One of the reason that have discouraged these synthetic way is the random width distribution obtained: this is likely 
to be a consequence of the change in direction when nanoparticles encounter defects or edges.
