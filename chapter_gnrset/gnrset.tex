\minitoc

\section{Introduction}\label{ch:gnrset:sec:intro}

Manipulating individual charges in nanostructured devices is of paramount importance for quantum technologies, and a fundamental step towards improved logic processing units and sensors. One of the possible fundamental building blocks are \glspl{SET} (Ch.~\ref{ch:theory}), in which the current can be tuned by adding electrons one-by-one to a nanoscopic element. Electrons would then flow only when quantized charged levels are shifted into the energy window of the applied bias $V_\textrm{SD}$, for example by applying a gate voltage $V_\textrm{G}$, and this results in periodic regions of blocked conductance (Fig.~\ref{fig:device_gnr}a,b)~\cite{stampfer2008tunable}.

One of the major hurdles of the \gls{SET} technology is that its operability is often limited to cryogenic temperatures, while quantum transport at \gls{RT} is necessary for many practical applications. To operate at RT, \glspl{SET} need to simultaneously display a very low self-capacitance, $C$, charging energies ($E_C$) larger than the thermal energy ($k_B T$), and high tunneling barriers that allow confining electrons on the nanoscale element.

Carbon materials are a particularly appealing platform that have seen multiple scientific and technological breakthroughs, such as superconductivity~\cite{cao2018unconventional}, spin localization~\cite{lombardi2019quantum} and manipulation~\cite{tong2022pauli}. Furthermore, \glspl{CNT} can display \gls{SET} behaviour up to \gls{RT} and have already been used to create low-power processing units~\cite{shulaker2013carbon}. On the other hand, their poly-dispersity still constitutes a major barrier to their large-scale integration.

\Glspl{MGNR}~\cite{jiao2010facile,li2008chemically, wang2011graphene, narita2015bottom} provide in this scenario unique opportunities since they can be chemically synthesised with a degree of edge and topological control far superior to what has been achieved with top-down nanofabrication~\cite{jarillo2004electron, cai2010atomically, narita2014synthesis, jiao2009narrow}.
Most importantly, they display topologically non-trivial electronic quantum phases~\cite{groning2018engineering, rizzo2018topological}, and offer edge magnetism~\cite{slota2018magnetic} and long spin-coherence times at RT~\cite{lombardi2021synthetic}. The missing ingredient to electrically control and read these topological and coherent spin states at high temperatures, if possible, is the observation of high-temperature \gls{SET} behaviour in \glspl{MGNR}.

This possibility seems within reach. Liquid-processable chemical strategies have recently opened up the path to ultra-clean single-MGNR devices directly created from solution~\cite{huang2018intrinsic,niu2019synthetic} (Ch.~\ref{ch:solubility}). Single-\gls{MGNR} devices, because of their extremely-high length/width ratio, should display self-capacitances of $\simeq\SI{1e-18}{\farad}$\footnote[2]{Because graphene electrodes have little electrostatic screening, the capacitive coupling of the ribbom to the back gate contributes the most to the overall capacitance.}, thus being operable at bias voltages $V_\textrm{SD}<\SI{100}{\milli\volt}$, while the extreme lateral electronic confinement should afford $E_C \gg k_B T$. \Glspl{MGNR} would thus offer excellent persective platforms for \glspl{SET} at \gls{RT}, but the evolution of their electronic behaviour with $T$, and the underlying phsyics, is still unknown.

This chapter describes, to the best of my knowledge, the first realisation of a \gls{SET} built with \glspl{MGNR} at \gls{RT}. We shall explore the conduction regimes as $T$ is increased from cryogenic to \gls{RT} by using a variety of source--drain and gate voltages in a two-terminal back-gated device. The measurements were conducted using the apparatus described in Ch.~\ref{ch:fabrication} and Ch.~\ref{ch:instrumentation_development}.

\section{Single-Electron Transistor Architecture}\label{ch:gnrset:sec:device_architecture}

Ultra-clean devices are obtained from \glspl{MGNR} designed with bulky 3D side-groups that enhance solubility (Fig.~\ref{fig:device_gnr}c) in organic solvents, synthesised from diene and dienophile in three steps as previously reported~\cite{huang2018intrinsic}. These groups have here an additional function: by suppressing $\pi$-stacking interactions between the GNR and graphene electrodes, they contribute crucially to the presence of high tunneling barriers, as necessary for high-$T$ performance. Furthermore, the edges carry also optically-active anthracene groups which may be used to monitor the dispersion quality with fluorescent spectroscopy.

The aromatic backbone has width of precisely $w=\SI{1.1}{\nano\metre}$, leading to a bandgap of $\pi \hbar v_F^* w^{-1}_\textrm{GNR} \simeq \SI{1.8}{\electronvolt}$~\cite{wakabayashi2010electronic} which should be associated with a reduced effective mass, thus reducing the contribution to the overall conductance from \gls{SB}~\cite{tung2014physics}, and an improvement of the band alignement with the graphene leads~\cite{Bennett2013}.

The nanodevices were fabricated as described in Ch.~\ref{ch:fabrication} (Fig.~\ref{fig:device_gnr}d,e). The junction energy barrier between the \glspl{MGNR} and graphene is estimated to be $\phi\simeq\SI{0.5}{\electronvolt}$ from the Simmons fit (Sec.~\ref{ch:fabrication:graphene_tunnel_junctions}) of the tunneling current prior to depositing the \glspl{MGNR}, which guarantees enough transparency to observe single-electron tunneling events.

\section{Temperature-Dependent Coulomb-Blockade Spectroscopy}\label{ch:gnrset:sec:coulomb_spectroscopy}

We explore the electronic behaviour by measuring the two-terminal conductance $G$ as a function of $V_\mathrm{SD}$ and $V_{\mathrm{G}}$, from \SI{25}{\milli\kelvin} to \gls{RT} (Fig.~\ref{fig:sd_base_30K} and \ref{fig:sd_60K_270K}). Seven charge transitions are visible within the experimentally accessible $V_\mathrm{G}$ range  (Fig.~\ref{fig:sd_base_30K} and \ref{fig:sd_60K_270K}), and they remain clearly distinguishable upon increasing $T$, with a periodic peak spacing of $\Delta V_{\mathrm{G}} \simeq\SI{4}{\volt}$. We assign the leftmost diamond having $N$ electrons in the ground state with the charge transition $N \leftrightarrow N+1$, and the rightmost diamond as the $N+6 \leftrightarrow N+7$ transition, so we assume the ribbon is n-doped.

The periodicity and cleanliness of the Coulomb oscillations excludes that electron transport occurs through multiple quantum dots in series, formed by intraribbon defects, such as edge roughness~\cite{mucciolo2009conductance, stampfer2009energy} and aromatic-core defects~\cite{lombardi2019quantum}. For each transition, it is possible to extract the gate coupling $\beta$ (also knwon as \textit{lever arm}) and the addition energy $E_\textrm{add}$ from the Coulomb diamond heights (Tab.~\ref{tab:lever_arm_Eadd}).

We observe a nonlinear increase in $\beta$ for diamonds at negative gate voltages, peaking at the greatest values for diamonds at zero gate voltage and then decreasing practically linearly as we move towards more positive gate voltages, with a mean value of $\overline{\beta}=\SI[separate-uncertainty = true]{25(4)}{\milli\electronvolt\per\volt}$. This symmetric and non-linear gate dependence could be caused by electrostatic forces in the dioxide substrate~\cite{svensson2013nonlinear}.

Similarly, the charge-state addition enegies lie between $149$ and \SI{168}{\milli\electronvolt}, with an average value of about $\overline{E_\textrm{add}}=\SI[separate-uncertainty = true]{157(7)}{\milli\electronvolt}$, which is comparable to other GNRs~\cite{el2020controlled} and CNT-embedded metal nanoparticles~\cite{zhang2018room}, and approximately five times larger than previously-reported values for CNT-based~\cite{postma2001carbon} or CMOS-integrable~\cite{ray2008cmos} \glspl{SET}.

Remarkably, Coulomb peaks show comparable heights indicating an equal participation of the same number of levels within the bias window (Fig.~\ref{fig:waterfall}). In a \gls{MGNR} of length $l_\textrm{GNR}$, confinement produces an energy spacing given by $\delta_n=\pi \hbar v_F^* l^{-1}_\textrm{GNR}$~\cite{wakabayashi2010electronic}, while, for a rectangular-shaped \gls{MGNR} $E_C=\frac{e^2}{C}=\frac{e^2 \ln (4 d / w)}{2 \pi \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r l_\textrm{GNR}}$, where $e$ is the electron charge, $d$ is the ribbon--gate distance, $w$ is the ribbon width, and $\varepsilon_0$ and $\varepsilon_r$ are the dielectric permittivities of vacuum and \ce{SiO2}, respectively~\cite{shylau2009capacitance}.

From $E_\mathrm{add}=\delta_n+2E_C$, we thus extract $l_\textrm{GNR}=58 \pm 6 \mathrm{~nm}$\footnote[2]{For a ribbon with $w_\textrm{GNR}\simeq\SI{1.12}{\nano\metre}$ and $l_\textrm{GNR}\simeq\SI{56}{\nano\metre}$ the $E_{0,1}/k_B\simeq\SI{37}{\milli\electronvolt}$, hence we include the single-particle level spacing contribution in $E_\textrm{add}$}.

\section{Analysis of the Low-Bias Conductance}

At $T=\SI{25}{\milli\kelvin}$ analysis of the low-bias ($V_\textrm{SD}=\SI{1}{\milli\volt}$) conductance peaks allows us to extract the graphene--MGNR coupling  $\Gamma=\SI{180}{\micro\electronvolt}$ by fitting with a Breit--Wigner model $G(\mu)=\frac{\Gamma / 2 \pi}{\left(\mu-\mu_N\right)^2+(\Gamma / 2)^2}$ the resonances. In the model, $\mu$ is the chemical potential and $\mu_N$ is the chemical potential of the $N^{\text {th}}$-\gls{MGNR} state. This energy scale identifies a first conduction regime at $T_C<\Gamma / k_B \sim 2 \mathrm{~K}$, and plot of the conductance peak maximum $G_{\max}$ against $T$ on a double logarithmic scale indeed shows that the behaviour changes drastically around $T_C$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:zero-bias_conductance_fit_power_law}c).

\begin{equation}
    G(T)\sim \frac{G_\text{max}(T)}{\cosh^2[\frac{2\text{arccosh}(\sqrt2)V_\textrm{G}}{w(T)}]},
\end{equation}

Fit results for the peak height are reported in Tab.~\ref{tab:conductance_exponents_temp}. Moreover, the thermal broadening of the peaks follows a linear $T$-dependence (Fig.~\ref{fig:peak_width_p5}), and we extract from the best-fit model $w_\textrm{peak}=\SI[separate-uncertainty = true]{1.7(0.3)e-1}{} + T\SI[separate-uncertainty = true]{1.1(0.1)e-2}{}$.

For $T<T_C$, the only transport mechanism is pure Coulomb blockade, modulated by the strong spin-phonon coupling present in \glspl{MGNR}, which leads to Franck--Condon blockade (Ch.~\ref{ch:solubility}). In this region, $G$ is essentially constant, and this indicates that the \gls{MGNR} states are independent on $\phi$, as thermally-activated transport with law $G(T) \propto e^{-\phi / k_B T}$ would otherwise be observed~\cite{sols2007coulomb}.

For $T>T_C$, up to \SI{180}{\kelvin}, the conductance follows a power-law behaviour $G_\textrm{max} \sim T^{\alpha}$, and the extracted exponents show values between $\alpha=0.50 \pm 0.06$ and $\alpha=0.83 \pm 0.06$ for all $N$ states (Fig.~\ref{fig:zero-bias_conductance_fit_power_law}c,d and Tab.~\ref{tab:conductance_exponents_temp}). This power-law behaviour of the \gls{TDOS} might be associated with the formation of \gls{TLL}~\cite{egger1997effective,egger1999luttinger, kane1997coulomb} in MGNRs, showing that correlated one-dimensional electronic states may be produced by electron--electron interactions.

Comparison of exponent values for \glspl{MGNR} with those of single nanotube~\cite{yao1999carbon} and ropes of bundled \glspl{CNT}~\cite{bockrath1999luttinger} shows excellent agreement only with \glspl{SET} based on single \glspl{CNT}. The extracted average $\alpha=0.63 \pm 0.08$ is in excellent agreement with the theoretical value for electron tunnels coherently at the ends of a \gls{TLL} thorugh correlated sequential tunneling~\cite{postma2001carbon,thorwart2002correlated}, $\alpha=0.68$, and not into its bulk ($\alpha=0.23$), as expected from the device geometry.

The \gls{TLL} phase allows exploring electron--electron interaction strength through the Luttinger parameter $g$, which is $g=1$ for the non-interacting case and $\ll 1$ for strong repulsive interactions (Sec.~\ref{ch2:derivation_tunneling_DOS}). Given the four-fold degeneracy of the graphene band structure, it is possible to recover the expression for the tunneling exponent of four conducting modes at Fermi energy $E_F$~\cite{bockrath1999luttinger}, as $g=(4 \alpha+1)^{-1}=0.28 \pm 0.04$, or from the expression $g=\left(1+2 E_C / \delta\right)^{-1 / 2}=0.41 \pm 0.08$~\cite{egger1997effective, egger1999luttinger}.

These values are well below $g=1$, confirming the importance of finite long-range Coulomb interactions in \glspl{MGNR}, but are interestingly weaker than those active in \glspl{CNT}. This might be explained with the hypothesis that electron--electron interactions depend significantly on the curvature radius of the conjugated carbon sheet~\cite{laird2015quantum,kane1997coulomb}.

Because peak broadening can obscure features and alter the power-law dependence, we also analyzed the integrated conductance, $G^*$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:integrated_conductance}). A power law, $G^* \propto T^{\xi}$ is observed, where $\xi=\alpha+1$ in the case of end-tunneling~\cite{thorwart2002correlated,postma2001carbon}. The extracted $\xi=1.5 \pm 0.1$ is in overall agreement with the values extracted from originating from $G_\textrm{max}$, which also confirms a more screened interaction picture for \glspl{MGNR}~\cite{sols2007coulomb}.

\Glspl{MGNR} could display \gls{TLL} only as long as electrons can tunnel coherently from one end to another via a single MGNR state, so that the whole MGNR acts as a single electronic structure. This condition is fulfilled when the electron thermal length $L_T = v_F/k_BT$ is larger than the MGNR geometric length $l_\textrm{GNR}$, with deviations from the TLL behaviour expected when $L_T \ll l_\textrm{GNR}$. This regime crossover is indeed observed above $T=\SI{180}{\kelvin}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:zero-bias_conductance_fit_power_law}c), for which $L_T<\SI{43}{\nano\metre}<l_\textrm{GNR}$, using $v_F\simeq\SI{1.6e5}{\metre\per\second}$ for the group velocity.

Beyond \SI{180}{\kelvin}, flexural phonons dominate the electronic behaviour by introducing phonon scattering~\cite{mariani2008flexural, lindsay2010flexural}, and are responsible for a rapid increase of $g$, which approaches the non-interacting limit above \SI{240}{\kelvin}.

\co{Outlook and Perspectives}
The results exposed in this chapter confirm the possibility of using molecular graphene nanoribbons as a technological platform for low-power and quantum applications even at temperatures higher than the cryogenic range. A couple of clarifications are here necessary.

To begin, the \gls{TDOS} of graphene nanoribbons may be different when these are put across a working device instead of being measured through scanning-tunneling techniques. Secondly, for this particular device the electron seems to undergo a correlated sequential tunneling mechanism instead of a standard sequential tunneling expected to weakly coupled systems (see also Ch.~\ref{ch:theory}). In this regard, the impurity model developed~\cite{postma2001carbon,thorwart2002correlated} reproduces very well the theoretical predictions by capturing the electron tunneling within the end--end framework.
