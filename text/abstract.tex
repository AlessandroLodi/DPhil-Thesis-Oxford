Significant efforts have been expended in integrating carbon materials into electronics circuits, but the lack of bandgap in graphene and the high tendency of carbon nanotubes (CNTs) to aggregate have incentivise the study of alternative carbon nanostructures.

In this thesis, I investigate molecularly-defined graphene nanostructures as potential candidate to this end. Intrinsically semiconducting, they offer atomically-precise topology which guarantees, in principle, complete control over their structure. I start by describing the instrumentation and device architectures used to measure the electronic transport properties of molecular graphene nanoribbons (MGNRs). I then demonstrate that they can afford as a molecular quantum single-electron transistor at high temperatures. The findings I present provide the first observation of quantum operation of an electronic device based on a full-carbon, all-around molecular structure operating at room-temperature.

I then provide evidence that MGNRs constitutes a new class of mono-dispersed semiconducting materials, solving long-standing carbon-nanotube technology issues. MGNR-based devices performance, as well as all carbon nanomaterials, can be compromised by intermolecular forces which push MGNRs to aggregate. I demonstrate how this problem can be overcome by chemical approach, allowing us to produce the cleanest features of single-electron ever obtained with this class of materials. The clarity of the transport features is comparable of what has been obtained for carbon nanotubes after more than 10 years of efforts, and allow addressing fundamental problems such as the electron--vibration coupling

I then examine the effect of adding dopant groups to the ribbon edges. The energy shift induced in the MGNR by the dopant is, however, weak and impractical at the device level. I then present the first field-effect transistor measurements of a unique class of materials where the dopant and the metal atoms are inserted directly into the honeycomb lattice, and propose possible improvements.

The research conduct is a first step towards the realisation of practical MGNR-based electronics. MGNRs host a multitude of physical phenomena that should be investigated thoroughly using STM, device-level, and electron-spin resonance techniques. The synthesis of micrometre-long MGNRs while maintaining ultra-small widths and high de-bundling degree will be crucial to the development of the first MGNR-based microprocessor.
