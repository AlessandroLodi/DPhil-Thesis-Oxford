\section{Graphene}\label{intro:sec:graphene}

\subsection{Crystal and Band Structure}
Graphene has a hexagonal structure, where the carbon atoms are hybridised as $sp^2$. In the International Table of Crystallography, graphene is included in the symmorphic space group \#191, with symmetry $D_{6h}$ ($P6/mmm$)~\cite{dresselhaus2007group}.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:graphene_lattice}a--b, the carbon atoms in graphene form a two-dimensional hexagonal lattice with two non-equivalent atoms per unit cell:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_lattice_vectors}
    \vect{a}{_1} = \frac{a}{2}(3, \sqrt{3}),~
    \vect{a}{_2} = \frac{a}{2}(3, -\sqrt{3}),
\end{equation}
%
with $a\simeq\SI{1.42}{\angstrom}$ the carbon--carbon bond length.
Each atom belonging to one sublattice, called sublattice A, has three nearest neighbours belonging to the other sublattice, called sublattice B. With respect to one atom chosen from sublattice A as origin, the position of these first three neighbours in real space are given by:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_first_neighbours}
    \vect{\updelta}{_1} = \frac{a}{2}(1, \sqrt{3}),~
    \vect{\updelta}{_2} = \frac{a}{2}(1, -\sqrt{3}),~
    \vect{\updelta}{_3} = -a(1,0),
\end{equation}
%
while the reciprocal-lattice vectors may be written as:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_reciprocal_vectors}
    \vect{b}{_1} = \frac{2\pi}{3a}(1, \sqrt{3}),~
    \vect{b}{_2} = \frac{2\pi}{3a}(1, -\sqrt{3}).
\end{equation}
%
whereas the Brillouin point are:
%
\begin{align}\label{eq:K_point_components}
    \vect{K}  & \equiv\mp~\frac{2\pi}{3a}\qty(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, 1),  \\
    \vect{K'} & \equiv\pm~\frac{2\pi}{3a}\qty(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, -1), \\
    \vect{M}  & \equiv\pm\qty(0, \pm~\frac{2\pi}{3a}).
\end{align}
%
Based on the experimental evidence collected in the last 20 years, the model that best describe the properties of graphene is the Wallace model of the band structure, which dates back to 1947, and qualifies graphene as a semimetal with a linear dispersion of the electronic excitations close to the Fermi energy~\cite{wallace1947band}.

Within the second-quantization formalism (with $\hbar = 1$), the tight-binding Hamiltonian may be written as
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_hamiltonian}
    \mathcal{H}_\textrm{TB} = -t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,\sigma}\qty(a^\dagger_{\sigma,i}b_{\sigma,j} + \textrm{h.c.}) - t' \sum_{\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle,\sigma}\qty(a^\dagger_{\sigma,i}a_{\sigma,j}+b^\dagger_{\sigma,i}b_{\sigma,j}+ \textrm{h.c.}),
\end{equation}
%
The notation $\langle ij\rangle$ means the sum runs over pairs of electrons that are nearest neighbours; similarly, the notation $\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle$ specifies a summation over the next-nearest neighbours. The two operators $a^\dagger_{i,\sigma}$ ($a_{i,\sigma}$) and $b^\dagger_{i,\sigma}$ ($b_{i,\sigma}$) create (annihilate) an electron with spin $\sigma=~\uparrow,\downarrow$ for atoms on sublattices A and B, respectively. The value of $t$ that best reproduces the experimental evidence is $t\simeq2.8$ \si{\electronvolt}~\cite{castroneto2009}, and quantifies the nearest-neighbour hopping energy; similarly, $t'$ denotes the next-nearest-neighbour hopping energy, and the experimental estimate is $t'\simeq\SI{0.1}{\electronvolt}$~\cite{deacon2007cyclotron}.

By diagonalising the Hamiltonian in Eq.~\ref{eq:graphene_hamiltonian}, it is possible to work out the energy dispersion of graphene,
%
\begin{equation}\label{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands}
    E_{\pm}(\vect{k}) = \epsilon_p - t'f(\vect{k}) \pm t\sqrt{3 + f(\vect{k})},
\end{equation}
%
with $f(\vect{k})$ defined as:
%
\begin{equation}\label{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands_fk}
    f(\vect{k})=2\cos{\left(\sqrt{3}k_ya\right)} + 4\cos{\qty(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}k_ya)}\cos{\qty(\frac{3}{2}k_xa)}.
\end{equation}
%
Eq.~\ref{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands} identifies an upper band ($\pi^*$) associated with the plus sign and a lower band ($\pi$) associated with the minus sign, and these bands are symmetric with respect to zero energy\footnote[3]{The fact that the energy dispersion is symmetric to zero energy is a result of the particle--hole symmetry of the system.}. If the system is undoped, there will be one electron per carbon atom, resulting in two electrons per cell: the lower band will be full, while the top band will be empty. Therefore, the system is semi-metallic.

When examining the trend close to the \vect{K} and \vect{K$'$} points, the peculiarities of the energy-band dispersion can be appreciated. By taking the positive components for the \vect{K} point, as defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:K_point_components}, and expanding Eq.~\ref{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands} as $\vect{k} = \vect{K} + \vect{q}$, with $\abs{(\vect{q})}\ll\abs{(\vect{K})}$, one obtains:
%
\begin{equation}
    E_{\pm}(\vect{q}) = \epsilon_p \pm \frac{3at}{2}\abs{\vect{q}},
\end{equation}
%
This remarkable result tells us that the dispersion close to the Fermi level is linear in \vect{q}. The Fermi velocity is defined by the usual relation $v_F = \frac{1}{\hbar}\qty(\dv{E}{\vect{k}})$, and in graphene $v_F\simeq1\times 10^6~\textrm{ms}^{-1}$~\cite{wallace1947band}. An important consequence of this result is that while for a two-dimensional electron gas the \gls{DOS} is constant, the \gls{DOS} in graphene close to the Fermi level is:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grafene:density_of_states}
    \rho(E)=\frac{2A_\textrm{cell}}{\pi}\frac{\abs{E}}{v_F^2}.
\end{equation}
%
This linear dependency of the electronic states density on the absolute value of the energy has important consequences on the particle velocity $c^* = v_F = 3ta/2\hbar$, which is constant and similar to the speed of light $E = pc = c^*\hbar\abs{\vect{k} - \vect{K}}$~\cite{wolf2014graphene}. This similarity has led to the definition of \textit{massless fermions} for electrons near the Fermi energy of graphene~\cite{novoselov2004electric}. Furthermore, for undoped graphene, the \gls{DOS} displays two Van Hove singularities at $\rho(\omega_\textrm{VH}) = \pm\SI{1}{\electronvolt}$~\cite{bena2005quasiparticle}. This behaviour is rather different to the conventional case of electrons in 2D metals, where they have energy dispersion $\epsilon=\hbar^2 q^2 / 2 m^*$ in terms of the band mass $m^*$, and where one obtains a constant density of states $\rho^\textrm{2D}(\epsilon)=g m^* / 2 \pi \hbar^2$.

Finally, the graphene conductivity may be computed semiclassically using the Einstein--Smoluchowski relation\footnote[2]{$D = k_\textrm{B}T/\eta = \mu k_\textrm{B}T$, where $k_\textrm{B}T$ is the thermal energy of a flow of semiclassical charge carriers that experience an average friction coefficient $\eta$ when diffusing in a medium with coefficient $D$.} and the \gls{DOS}~\cite{bolotin2008temperature}:
%
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:semiclassical_conductivity}
    \sigma_{\textrm{sc}}=e^{2} \rho v \ell_{\mathrm{el}},
\end{equation}
%
where $v$ is the carrier velocity, $\ell_{\mathrm{el}}$ the elastic mean free path (see Ch.~\ref{ch:theory:eq:capacitance_interaction_model}), and $e$ the elementary charge. From Eq.~\ref{ch1:eq:semiclassical_conductivity} and by knowing the charge density $n$, one can calculate the charge mobility, which at $T=\SI{0 }{\kelvin}$ reads:
%
\begin{equation}
    \mu(E)=\frac{\sigma_\mathrm{sc}}{e n}.
\end{equation}
%
Close to the Dirac points, the experimental conductivities lie within $\sigma\simeq 2-5~e^{2}/ h$~\cite{chen2008charged}, although several experiments have demonstrated a variation of more than an order of magnitude because of doping from the electrical contancts or the substrate~\cite{jiang2007quantum, ozyilmaz2007electronic}.

Initial interest for using graphene as a channel material in \gls{CMOS} logic may have been fueled by its remarkable mobility~\cite{schwierz2010nanometer}. In a standard CMOS gate, carriers are driven by a longitudinal electric field $\CMcal{E}_\textrm{SD}$ from the the source to the drain, and their motion is often limited by various scattering sources, such as collisions between electrons and phonons of the crystal lattice from which the gate is made. In their first graphene-based \gls{FET} prototype, Novoselov and colleagues measured an impressive intrinsic mobility $\mu_e^{\textrm{in}}$ at \SI{290}{\kelvin} close to $\mu_e^\textrm{in}\simeq10,000$ cm$^{2}$V$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$~\cite{novoselov2004electric} using a single layer of graphene on a \ce{Si}/\ce{SiO2} substrate serving as a back gate.

These results were explained by: (1) an assumption that graphene has electrons with negligible effective mass close to the Fermi energy, and (2) a lack of direct carrier backscattering because of graphene's lattice symmetry~\cite{wolf2014graphene}. Although some believed scatterings due to charged impurities~\cite{hwang2007carrier} or microscopic ripples~\cite{schedin2007detection} to be predominant at low temperatures, weak electron--phonon scattering even at room temperature is considered to be responsible for the extrordinary mobility $\mu^{\text{in}}\simeq\SI{200000}{\centi\metre\squared\per\volt\per\second}$ reported from graphene sandwiched between two \gls{hBN} layers~\cite{morozov2008giant} (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:graphene_device}). Having a slight lattice mismatch (about 1.5\%~\cite{giovannetti2007substrate}) relative to graphene, $\varepsilon_{\text{hBN}}\simeq$\numrange{3}{4}, and significant bandgap (\SI{5.9}{\electronvolt}), \gls{hBN} is considered the best insulating to encapsulate graphene, and indeed hBN-sandwiched graphene devices have been recently used for groundbreaking results such as unconventional superconductivity in bilayer graphene~\cite{cao2018unconventional} and Stoner ferromagnetism~\cite{seiler2021quantum}.

The high mobility alone is, however, insufficient. As we have mentioned earlier, one of the main motivations for engineering graphene-based transistors is that, because of the absence of electron backscattering, they should be robust against increases in off-state current when the \gls{CMOS} channel is scaled down~\cite{schwierz2013graphene,young2009quantum,katsnelson2006chiral}. Furthermore, the device conductance must be regulated by the gate voltage $V_textrm{G}$, and has to be robust against any voltage fluctuations. The figure of merit associated with last requirement is the so-called $I_\text{on}/I_\text{off}$. The on--off ratio quantifies the ability of any circuit component in electrical contact with the transistor to distinguish between the transistor's on and off-states. A high on--off ratio corresponds, typically, to a low leakage current.

It is possible to estimate the on--off ratio of a device using the thermoionic-emission model for the drain current (see pictorial representation in Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:graphene_device}). When graphene is in contact with a semiconductor, the two work functions $\phi_\textrm{Si}$ and $\phi_\textrm{g}$ align accordingly based on the doping and the carrier concentrations. If $\phi_\textrm{Si} = \phi_\textrm{g}$, the \gls{SB} formed at the Si--graphene junction may be written as $\phi_B=E_g/2$, where $E_g$ is the semiconductor bandgap, and the on--off ration is~\cite{kim2011role}:
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bandgap_current_ratio}
    \frac{I_\textrm{on}}{I_\textrm{off}}\propto\exp{\frac{E_g}{2k_\textrm{B}T}},
\end{equation}
%
where $k_\textrm{B}T$ is the thermal energy. Some researchers suggest that bandgap values of \SIrange{0.3}{0.5}{\electronvolt} are ideal for \gls{CMOS} applications~\cite{schwierz2010graphene, reddy2011graphene}.

Despite considerable efforts, researchers have been hindered by the metallic nature of graphene from producing \gls{CMOS} architectures with high-resistance off-states, and have therefore deemed it unsuitable for logic applications. In contrast, graphene-based electronics are broadly useful in applications requiring high frequencies. For analogue applications, properties such as ballistic transport, linear current--voltage characteristics, high transconductance, and exceptionally high sustained currents ($> 10^8$ \si{\ampere\per\square\centi\metre}) have proven to be ideal~\cite{meric2008rf, meric2008current}.

\subsection{High-Frequency Applications}

The frequency device performance are characterised in terms of two quantities, namely cutoff $f_\textrm{T}$ and maximum of oscillations $f_\textrm{max}$ frequencies. The former indicates how quickly a signal may travel from the gate to the drain of the device and is often measured when the small-signal current gain approaches unity, while the latter is the highest possible frequency at which there is a non-vanishing power gain~\cite{schwierz2010graphene}.
The frequency response is inversely related to the length of the gate and proportional to the transconductance, which is approximately proportional to mobility\footnote[2]{This is strictly valid if the mobilities are in the diffusive regime. If carriers approach ballistic values, then the transconductance is constant.}. Therefore, to achieve high cut-off frequencies in a transistor, researchers must maintain high carrier mobility when the channel length is scaled down~\cite{schwierz2013graphene}. In this regard, numerous research groups have demonstrated \gls{RF} applications for graphene~\cite{colmiais2022towards, liao2012graphene, lin2009operation}.

Late in 2008, it was claimed that the first device with a cutoff frequency in the gigahertz region was fabricated using exfoliated graphene placed on a high-resistivity silicon substrate, with a \SI{30}{\nano\metre}-thick top-gate dielectric made of \ce{HfO2} and a \SI{500}{\nano\metre}-long gate~\cite{meric2008rf}. Despite a reported cutoff frequency of about $f_\textrm{T}\simeq\SI{14.7}{\giga\hertz}$ it was stated that excessive contact resistances and gate lengths were the primary causes of low ($f_\textrm{max}<\SI{1}{\giga\hertz}$) maximum frequency performance. The following year, Pd/Au contacts and a \SI{12}{\nano\metre}-thick aluminum oxide were implemented in a \SI{350}{\nano\metre}-long graphene gate, and although the cutoff frequency was increased to \SI{50}{\giga\hertz} the maximum frequency was increased only to \SI{2}{\giga\hertz}~\cite{lin2009development}. This poor performance was attributed to the organic contaminations on the graphene surface resulting from the use of poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) as a supporting layer during the transfer process, as well as poor development of gate dielectrics~\cite{jang2014improved}.

After two years, a one-order-of-magnitude rise in the cutoff and the highest frequency of oscillation was demonstrated~\cite{lin2010100}. The devices are realised with an epitaxially grown silicon-carbide--graphene \glspl{MOSFET} with a \SI{240}{\nano\metre} gate length, Ti/Pd/Au contacts, and \ce{HfO2} \SI{10}{\nano\metre}-thick as top gate. Local gating is frequently preferred as gate-source, and gate-drain capacitances are inversely proportional to the channel length and minimised in this geometric configuration, hence improving the cut-off frequency that can be reached~\cite{liao2012graphene}. In addition to $f_\textrm{T}\simeq\SI{100}{\giga\hertz}$, the highest maximum frequency ever measured was $f_\textrm{max}\simeq\SI{100}{\giga\hertz}$, beyond the capabilities of any technology at the time. \citeauthor{xu2016contacts} noticed that, since the maximum oscillation frequency significantly depends on  device layout and parasitic resistances arising from the metallic contacts, partial passivation of the metal contacts and a multi-finger gate layout would be viable strategies to increase $f_\textrm{max}$~\cite{xu2016contacts}.

Later the same year, a \SI{144}{\nano\metre}-long gate device with $f_\textrm{T}\simeq\SI{300}{\giga\hertz}$ was developed using mechanically exfoliated graphene and \ce{Co2Si}–\ce{Al2O3} nanowire as self-aligned multi top-gates~\cite{liao2010high}. This result was also confirmed by comparison to the predicted value based on the relationship\footnote[2]{$f_\textrm{T} = g_\textrm{m}/(2\pi C_\textrm{g})$~\cite{sze2021physics}} between dc transconductance, gate capacitance, and the cutoff frequency in the case where total input parasitic capacitance is negligible. The authors did not report the power $G^{1/2}_\textrm{max}$, only the current gain $\abs{h_{21}}$, making it difficult for others to assess the $f_\textrm{max}$ of the device. A rough estimate might be extracted by looking at the results reported by Cheng and colleagues on a similar device, where the gate was built using a stack of Au/Ti/\ce{Al2O3}~\cite{cheng2012high}.

Improvements in the cutoff frequency response ($f_\textrm{T}\simeq\SI{427}{\giga\hertz}$ for a channel length of 67 nm) compared with that of \gls{CVD} graphene devices with similar gate length, however, were eclipsed by only minor improvements to the device maximum frequency response ($f_\textrm{max}=\SI{8}{\giga\hertz}$) when the gate length was lowered to \SI{46}{\nano\metre}. As for the cutoff frequency, it is vital to minimise all parasitic resistances and capacitances in order to optimise $f_\textrm{max}$. In addition, the output conductance must be decreased in order to obtain the highest feasible operating speeds, which is often accomplished by operating the transistor when the drain current is saturated.

Furthermore, researchers of IBM investigated on the saturation behavior of short-channel graphene transistors leveraging CVD graphene transferred to diamond-like and SiC substrates, in an attempt to improve the power gain~\cite{wu2012state}. These are particularly attractive possibilities since the substrates are commercially accessible on high-quality wafers, which makes it quite straightforward to scale up graphene arrays. Since the work function of graphene ($\phi^\textrm{g} = \SI{4.5}{\electronvolt}$) is greater than that of silicon carbide ($\phi^{\ce{SiC}} = \SI{3.7}{\electronvolt}$), negative charges accumulate on the graphene side of the contact, leaving graphene n-doped~\cite{sonde2009electrical}. The authors compensated this unwanted doping by using an innovative technique involving plasma-enhanced CVD silicon nitride as an intrinsic, p-type gate dielectric.

The transistors perform well at high speeds, not only because of their good cutoff frequencies ($f_\textrm{T}\simeq \SI{350}{\giga\hertz}$), but also because of their twofold increase in maximum oscillation frequency  $f_\textrm{max}$ $\simeq\SI{40}{\giga\hertz}$, suggesting undoped graphene substrates as ideal for high frequency applications. To the best of my knowledge, the current graphene transistor power-gain record is approximately \SI{70}{\giga\hertz}, achieved by using a ($0\,0\,0\,\overline{1}$) carbon-terminated surface of silicon carbide instead of the conventional (0\,0\,0\,1) Si. This indicates that a low contact resistance between the gold contacts and graphene is the primary factor responsible for the high maximum frequency~\cite{guo2013record}.

In terms of cutoff frequencies, the performance of graphene-based devices is comparable to that of the leading solid-state competitors, such as GaAs high-electron-mobility transistors (HEMT), InP HEMT, and Si \glspl{MOSFET}. The performance in terms of maximum frequencies, however, is one or two orders of magnitude below the required values for a competing technology~\cite{tan1990InGaAs, post2006RF,lai2007sub}. In the scientific literature, one of the most commonly cited underlying factors for this behaviour is the weak saturation in the drain current in graphene, which ultimately depends on the lack of a bandgap in the channel. Because of their intrinsic semiconductivity, graphene-derived materials such as nanoribbons and carbon nanotube-\glspl{MOSFET} could be a viable solution to this problem. In the following sections, I will investigate some important aspects of the electronic response at the device level for each class of materials.

\section{Carbon Nanotube Electronics}\label{intro:sec:cnt}

\co{Introduction: define your aims and objectives}
The literature on carbon nanotubes is vast and well documented. In the following, I have two primarily aims. Firstly, I will provide a comprehensive review of \glspl{CNTFET}, highlighting crucial elements that have, in my opinion, played a significant role in enhancing the competitiveness of nanotube-based transistors. Secondly, I will quantify the performance in order to compare it to \gls{GNR}-based transistors.

In contrast to graphene, \glspl{CNT} have an electrical bandgap that varies according to the nanotube chirality and diameter~\cite{dresselhaus2000carbon}, and may be defined in real space by two unit vectors $\vect{a_1}$ and $\vect{a_2}$ and any linear combination of these with integer coefficients, which are typically labelled $m$ and $n$~\cite{dresselhaus1998physical}. Depending on these coefficients, a \gls{CNT} can be metallic, quasi-metallic, or semiconducting. As depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:cnt_structure}, states resulting from quantisation in the circumferential direction may or may not pass through one of the $\vect{K}$ points in the first Brillouin zone of graphene, and the resulting \gls{CNT} will be metallic when the electronic states cross a $\vect{K}$ point, else it will be semiconducting. Exceptions to this simple rule arise from to the strong curvature when the diameter of the tube is sufficiently small, which leads to large spin--orbit interactions~\cite{steele2013large,laird2015quantum}.

The discovery of charge-carrier modulations within a nanotube produced by a gate-induced electric field was the first landmark which started the field~\cite{tans1998room}. These early \glspl{FET} were fabricated using a doped silicon substrate as the gate to tune the energy bands, with thermally grown silicon dioxide acting as the dielectric between the nanotube and the substrate, and Au contacts to electrically contacting the nanotube.

Since then, several \gls{CNTFET} designs have been put forward (Tab.~\ref{intro:tab:cnt_devices}), which may be even classified more fundamentally as devices with or without a spacer. The length of the spacer $L_\textrm{spr}$ is the gap between the channel and the metal contacts. Given that a certain class of nanotubes is intrinsically semiconducting, the flow of charge carriers in spacer-less devices is not generated by the electric field $\CMcal{E}_\textrm{SD}$ in the conductive channel, but rather by the concentration gradient of charges that exists between the two ends of the nanotube.

In contrast, \glspl{CNTFET} with spacers are not limited by the at-the-source injection step, since the electric field $\CMcal{E}_\textrm{SD}$ drives the carriers flow, and indeed \citeauthor{javey2009carbon} produced a device with spacers where the mismatch between $\phi^\textrm{M}$ and \gls{CNT} $\phi^\textrm{CNT}$ was large, and found that the device was more resistant to ambipolar conduction because of the substantial energy barrier at the drain~\cite{javey2009carbon, torres2014introduction}.

There is another reason to study how different work functions affect the transistor performance. Solid-state semiconductors have dangling bonds at the surface resulting in Fermi-level pinning, which means that the variation of the band bending is independent of the metal work function even when large~\cite{sankey1984si}. Since, ideally, the \gls{CNT} structure has no open bonds, the Fermi level of the metal will not be pinned to mid-gap states~\cite{bachtold2001logic, svensson2009dependence}. For example, it has been shown that metals with high work functions, such as Pd ($\phi^\textrm{Pd}\simeq$ \SIrange{5.2}{5.6}{\electronvolt}), yields p-type behavior with high on state current for large-enough \gls{CNT} diameter $d_\textrm{CNT}\ge\SI{2}{\nano\metre}$~\cite{leonard2000role}, and that the \gls{SB} height is inversely proportional to $d_\textrm{CNT}$~\cite{svensson2009dependence}. The reason for this observation was explained within the \gls{SB} framework, i.e.\ large work functions prevent the minority carriers to hop over the barrier back into the channel, resulting in low leakage current. Metals with large work functions are, however, more strongly associated with large contact resistances when their wettability is poor~\cite{lim2009contact}, suggesting mentals with good wettability such as Ti and Cr as ideal for low contact resistances.

Often, however, metals with low work functions and good wettability have a strong tendency to oxidise, especially in air. A possible workaround is to cap low work function contacts all-around with metals having high work functions, such as Sc and Y~\cite{ding2009contacted}: the \glspl{CNTFET} produced with such contacts reported high on-state currents ($I_\textrm{on}\simeq\SI{250}{\micro\ampere}$), mobilities $\mu\ge\SI{500}{\cm\square\per\V\per\second}$, and \gls{DIBL} up to \SI{105}{\si{\mV\per\V}}, together with excellent switching behaviour $SS\simeq\SI{73}{\milli\volt}$~dec$^{-1}$~\cite{ding2009contacted,peng2019carbon}.

The subthreshold swing $SS$ measures the rate at which the gate voltage switches on the transistor, and it is a metric that quantifies the control the gate has on the conductive channel. Furthermore, it is often indirectly linked to heat dissipation, which is one the primary obstacles to miniaturising \gls{CMOS} technology. Since the carriers are thermally injected, it is challenging to reduce the operating voltage while at the same time improving CPU operational speed and maintaining a low off-state power dissipation. Since the off-state power is scales quadratically with the off-state current $P_\textrm{off} = RI^2_\textrm{off}$, the objective is to lower the $I_\textrm{off}$ without sacrificing $I_\textrm{on}$. The process of thermally injecting charge carriers imposes a limit, called Boltzmann limit, on the subthreshold slope which for the is approximately $2.3 k_\textrm{B}T/q\simeq\SI{60}{\milli\volt}$~dec$^{-1}$ at room temperature.

One of the most promising approaches to overcoming the Boltzmann limit uses a different source--carrier injection mechanism based on Zener tunneling rather than thermal means. If the transmission probability does not depend on the energy gap between the metal and the nanotube work functions, but instead depends on the interband tunneling, the output current is temperature-independent. Several researchers have proposed both \gls{CNT}- and \gls{GNR}-based transistors with this operating mechanism~\cite{ionescu2011tunnel, hammam2018sub, hwang2019room}.

Crucial is also the conduction type. The vast majority of the \gls{CNTFET} transfer $I_\textrm{SD}$--$V_\textrm{G}$ characteristics reported are p-doped~\cite{allen2007carbon}, and this is largely because (1) photochemical resists in the lithographic steps often leave p-type contaminations on the substrate~\cite{nosho2005n}, and (2) oxygen impurities~\cite{nonoguchi2016simple}. Instead, n-type devices would be desirable.

As a result of the presence of electron-donating dopants, n-type doping assures that, in a heterojunction, \glspl{CNT} do not have to pull electrons from the opposite side of the junction because this need is satisfied by the dopant atom~\cite{brownlie2018advances}. Instead, the nanotube would preferentially donate electrons to the heterojunction p-type side, leaving behind an electron vacancy (hole) that is stabilised by the excess of negative charge carriers provided by the dopant.

An interesting solution for fabricating n-type \glspl{CNTFET} is by doping the nanotube-channel surface with elements of the first two groups of the periodic table, such as \ce{K+} and Mg$^{2+}$~\cite{bockrath2000chemical, zhou2000modulated}. Precise control of the doping density per \gls{CNT} unit length is, however, necessary, and this is often difficult because of the high tendency of \glspl{CNT} to aggregate when dispersed in solution, often yielding uncontrolled doping levels~\cite{brownlie2018advances, yu2012air}. Additionally, ionised impurities, instability in air and to temperature fluctuations have hampered the applications of n-type \glspl{CNTFET}.

\subsection{Channel-Length Scaling}

Whether Si could operate at channel lengths below \SI{10}{\nano\metre} was a question for a long time, but recent scientific advances in FinFETs~\cite{yang20045nm} and Gate-All-Around FETs~\cite{gu2011first} have demonstrated its viability and fostered a plethora of commercial uses (currently, modern devices employ the \SI{4}{\nano\metre} technology). Nonetheless, \gls{CMOS} scaling has always been a good-enough reason for investigating fabrication techniques and materials robust against short-channel effects\footnote[2]{Typicaly short-channel effects are threshold voltage roll-off, off-state leakage current, and channel length-modulation are just a few of the problems}.

In \glspl{CNTFET}, two are the main device parameters that can be scaled down: the contact length $L_\textrm{c}$ and the channel length $L_\textrm{ch}$. The $L_\textrm{ch}$ scaling typically reduces the gate control over the channel, hence the ability of the electric field generated through the gate to bend the silicon band structure. Intuitively, as the channel length is reduced, the electric field created by the source contact will be felt by the charge carriers more and more. \Glspl{CNT} may provide a suitable platform for such scaling because of their ultrathin, hollow nature. This could be, however, a double-edged sword. Since the electron effective mass is smaller\footnote[4]{$m^*_\textrm{CNT} = (0.228 \pm 0.129)m_0$~\cite{marulanda2008carrier}} compared to that of silicon, the tunneling probability may be large enough to increase drastically the source--drain current. From a device performance point of view, this often means an increase in the $SS$ and high $I_\textrm{off}$.

\Citeauthor{franklin2010length} conducted one of the earliest systematic studies on $L_\textrm{ch}$ scaling, through a series of experiments on \glspl{CNT}~\cite{franklin2010length} (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:cnt_device_1}). When the channel length was reduced by more than two orders of magnitude (from \SI{3}{\si{\micro\metre}} down to \SI{15}{\si{\nano\metre}}), a consistent trend was found in both the threshold voltage ($V_\textrm{Th}\simeq\SI[separate-uncertainty = true]{-0.5(0.2)}{V}$) and subthreshold swing ($SS\simeq\SI{90}{\milli\volt}$~dec$^{-1}$). The threshold voltage is the gate voltage required to fully invert the semiconductor. It is typically measured by applying a small $V_\textrm{SD}$ and measuring the $I_\textrm{SD}$ as a function of $V_\textrm{G}$, provided that the drain current is at least one order of magnitude smaller than the applied gate voltage, scaled accordingly by the gate capacitance.

This, essentially, means that the gate-generated electric field controls the carriers flow even when the electric field between source and drain become non-neglibile. An aggressive reduction of the channel length, however, could in principle make the gate less effective in controlling the charge carriers, meaning that larger gate variations are required for pulling the same amount of current from the drain.
This essentially implies that the gate-generated electric field controls the flow of carriers even when the electric gradient between source and drain is no longer negligible. However, an extreme reduction of the channel length might, in theory, reduce the current amplification (the gain), excluding the possibility of employing \glspl{CNT} as molecular op-amp.

And indeed, at that time of \Citeauthor{franklin2010length}'s, experiment part of the scientific community believed that the $L_\textrm{ch}$ scaling could have a negative impact on the \glspl{CNTFET} gain, i.e. low transconductance $g_\textrm{m}$~\cite{avouris2003carbon,javey2009carbon, anantram2006physics}. In a standard \gls{MOSFET}, the transconductance $g_\textrm{m}$ is defined as $g = \partial{I_\textrm{SD}}/\partial{V_\textrm{G}}$, and it is measured by applying a small, constant bias. Typical values lies in the range \qtyrange[range-units = bracket]{1}{30}{\milli\siemens\per\micro\metre} for a small-signal field-effect transistor~\cite{sze2021physics}. However, Franklin and his colleagues measured transconductance values as high as \SI{40}{\micro\siemens\per\micro\metre} for \glspl{CNTFET} with $L_\textrm{ch}\simeq\SI{15}{\nano\metre}$ ((Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:cnt_device_2})), which corresponds to transconductance values of approximately \qtyrange[range-units = bracket]{91}{110}{\milli\siemens\per\micro\metre}, thereby meeting the requirements for a technology compatible and competitive with the silicon.

\subsection{Contact-Length Scaling}
When \glspl{MOSFET} are shrank down to the nanoscale, one must consider also the effect played by the overlap of the \gls{CNT} with source and drain. Numerical investigations of this phenomenon have suggested that high contact transparencies might result when the metal--\gls{CNT} coupling is weak, meaning the electronic states of the tube are, more or less, unchanged after the formation of the lead--\gls{CNT} junctions~\cite{fediai2015electron, teichert2019improved, fediai2016towards}. Especially for long contacts ($L_\textrm{c}\simeq\SI{30}{\nano\metre}$), one suggestion advanced by these studies was that in order to achieve high contact transparencies one should minimize coupling while maximising the overlap region, and that the junction Pd--\gls{CNT} might leads to quasi-Ohmic contacts~\cite{liu2017scaling}.

By using the transfer length\footnote[2]{The transfer length is the distance over which a fraction equal to $1-e^{-1}$ of the total current is \textit{transferred} from the contact metal to the semiconducting channel ($e$ is the Euler's number), and it is frequently used as a metric for quantifying the coupling between electrical contacts and conduction channel (small transfer lengths result in greater couplings, and, consequently, lower contact resistances)~\cite{saraswat2021materials}.} model, experiments performed on small-diameter ($d_\textrm{CNT}\simeq\SI{1.5}{\nano\metre}$) \glspl{CNT} highlighted inverse scaling of the contact length with the resistance between the nanotube and the electrodes, i.e.\ $L_\textrm{c}\propto1/R_\textrm{c}$~\cite{purewal2007scaling, franklin2010length}. These observations were rationalised by considering dipole moments building up at the metal--\gls{CNT} interface~\cite{leonard2000role}, which are likely caused by interface states at the junction

A large density of dipole charges should then translates into a large density of interface states, which could pin the Fermi level. Since in a planar contact, the dipole in a metal--\gls{CNT} contact is localised in all directions, the consequence of having interface states is that the \gls{CNT} energy bands might be shifted away from the junction, inducing the formation of a narrow barrier responsible for the increased contact resistance~\cite{svensson2011schottky}. Therefore, in order to limit the band shift from the junction, electrical contact with work functions close in value to the \gls{CNT} one might protect reduce the Fermi-level pinning caused by surface states.

More sophisticated computational methods have also been used attempting at clarify the nature (and the role) of the chemical bonds produced when the \gls{CNT} interacts with the electrode crystal structure. By applying \gls{NEGF} numerical schemes, \citeauthor{fediai2016towards} suggested that the most unfavourable length scaling should be observed for weakly-interacting metals such as Cr, Pd, Pt, and, on the other hand, metals such as Au, Al, and Sc could be nearly insensitive to the scaling of $L_\textrm{c}$ when it is decreased down to $\approx\SIrange{15}{20}{\nano\metre}$~\cite{fediai2016towards}. Furthermore, the authors proposed a numerical criterion through which they projected a better contact resistance scaling for Rh in comparison to Au, Pt, and Pd. However they also simulated the metal--junction long limit and suggested that differences in the $R_\textrm{c}$ resulted by adopting Pd instead of Rh were neglibile, suggesting that the better scaling for the Rh--\gls{CNT} junctions may be not worth the cost of the material.

At cryogenic temperatures, the smearing of $k_\textrm{B}T$ makes a number of quantum phenomena observables. Because the energy scales for such quantum effects typically increase with decreasing the system size $L$ (see also Sec.~\ref{ch:theory:sec:energy_exchange}), a great deal of these effects have been experimentally observed in \glspl{CNT}.

The platform most commonly used in quantum transport experiments is an electronic device made by \gls{CNT} embedding a \gls{QD} (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:cnt_set}a). The exact position of the \gls{QD} inside \gls{CNT} has been object of scientific debate, but it is now considered to be the nanotube portion between the large \gls{SB} barriers arising at the metal--\gls{CNT} junctions, where electrons can be trapped by the electric fields induced by local gates located beneath the tube~\cite{laird2015quantum}. A \gls{QD} (or, more generally, a \gls{SET}) can be realised if the contact resistances at the junctions source--\gls{QD} and \gls{QD}--drain exceed a certain limit, i.e.\ $R_\textrm{c}\ge\SI{26}{\kilo\ohm}$~\cite{likharev1999single}; when the contacts are such opaque they are tipically called \textit{tunneling barriers}, given that the only transport mechanism through which current can flow thorugh the \gls{QD} is quantum tunneling. \Gls{CNT} energy levels can then be studied by measuring the $I_\textrm{SD}$ through such \glspl{QD} as a function of $V_\textrm{SD}$, $V_\textrm{G}$, and other parameters such as magnetic field $B$, temperature $T$, etc.

\subsection{Energy Scales and characteristic Lengths}\label{ch:theory:sec:energy_exchange}
Mesoscopic systems exhibit quantum transport phenomena. There, the sample size becomes comparable or lower than the characteristic lengths for electron travelling, and, as a result, physical properties such as the electrical conductivity depend on the specimen dimensionality.

Typically, three characteristic lengths are investigated in mesoscopic transport experiments. The mean free path $\ell$, the wavelength of Fermi $\lambda_{F}$, and the phase-relaxation length $L_{\varphi}$. The mean free path is the average distance an electron travels prior to being dispersed by a scattering centre; attempts to measure it for graphene nanoribbons are briefly described in Ch~\ref{ch:introduction}. The Fermi wavelength $\lambda_{F}=2 \pi / k_{F}$ is the wavelength of an electron at Fermi energy $E_\textrm{F}$, whereas the phase-relaxation length is the average distance over which an electron travels before its quantum-mechanical phase becomes randomised.

If the scattering of an electron is \textit{elastic}, such as when the scattering event does not induce any electronic transition between the ground state and any excited states, the wavefunction undergoes essentially a phase shift following the event, remaining coherent. Therefore, an elastic event may only affect $\ell$, leaving $L_{\varphi}$ unchanged. In contrast, if the electron wave loses energy by either excitation of core electrons, spins associated with magnetic impurities, or phonons, both the mean free path and the phase-relaxation length may be affected by such (\textit{inelastic}) scattering events. Tab~\ref{tab:characteristic_lengths} summarises a few of these factors for various materials as a function of carrier concentration $n$.

Because only electrons around the Fermi energy $E_\textrm{F}$ affects the transport properties, the mean free path and phase-relaxation length are often scaled by the Fermi velocity

\begin{equation}
    t_{m}=\frac{L_m}{v_\textrm{F}} , \quad \text { and } \quad t_{\varphi}=\frac{L_\varphi}{v_\textrm{F}},
\end{equation}
%
The introduced $t_m$ and $t_\varphi$ are, respectively, the momentum relaxation time and phase-relaxation time, and are often are compared to the time it takes one electron to travel the length of the sample. However, these two numbers do not correlate to the period of a single scattering event by the relevant scattering mechanism; rather, they represent the average time for the several collisions that result in significant changes of momentum and phase, respectively. In this context, significant entails, for instance, that the sums of the changes in momentum and phase over time $t_{m}$ and $t_{\varphi}$ reach $\hbar k_{\mathrm{F}}$ and $\pi$, respectively~\cite{dresselhaus1998physical}.

There are several ways through which it is possible to control the various scattering mechanisms. For example it is possible to suppress the magnetic scattering by applying a magnetic field~\cite{mccreary2012magnetic, escalera2018spin}, whilst phonons excitations might be suppressed at low temperature, although it is the electron--phonon interaction which controls the relaxation of an electron by an emitting a phonon~\cite{efetov2010controlling}. The electron--electron interaction is determined by the carrier concentration and the extension of the valence band along the Brilluoin zone~\cite{kotov2012electron}. On top of that, all the frequencies corresponding to the various scattering processes might be changed by controlling the Fermi velocity, often obtained by either varying a gate voltage or by changing the conduction band curvature.

\subsection{Electronic-Transport Regimes}\label{ch:theory:sec:electronic_transport_regimes}

In charge-transport experiments, molecules are typically a few nanometer in size, orders of magnitude smaller than what tweezers or other common manipulation tools can handle. Since the number of lateral confinements imposed on these system is three dimensional, the corresponding \gls{DOS} approaches the Dirac delta distribution $\rho_{0\textrm{D}}\sim\delta(E)$.

An analogous platform where the density of state approaches the Dirac delta functions are \glspl{QD}, historically fabricated on a metallic substrate through lithography means (Fig.~\ref{fig:theory:set_schematic}), and capable of hosting electrons. For these platforms, a wide breadth of theoretical and experimental evideces have been accumulated since the 1980, with application ranging from the nanoelectronics to quantum computing. While source and drain are electrically contactd to the central island, one or more gates are just capacitatively coupled.

            \textbf{a}, Cartoon for a \gls{SET} based on quantum dots.

            $\Gamma_{S,D}$ are the tunneling rates.

One way to describe this system is through the constant interaction model, which allows to derive important electrical transport
information within the \textit{orthodox} Coulomb framework~\cite{zimmerli1992noise}. Within this model the Coulomb interactions
both between electrons on the dot and between the dot and the leads, are described by a single capacitance $C$ which stays constant and it is given by the total sum of the individual capacitances between the dot with its terminals
%
\begin{equation}\label{ch:theory:eq:capacitance_interaction_model}
    C = \sum_{i=1}^\textrm{terminals} C_i,
\end{equation}
%
where $i$ runs over all the terminals coupled to the central island. Secondly, the total Hamiltonian may be modelled as a sum of single-particle Hamiltonians, meaning that the total energy of the system can be expressed as a sum of single-particle energies $\Delta\epsilon$ which corresponds to the individual, non-interacting energy levels. Under these assumptions, the total dot energy given for a quantum dot in a three-terminal device with $N$ electrons becomes
%
\begin{equation}\label{ch:theory:eq:total_dot_energy}
    U(N) = \sum_{i}^NE_i + \frac{[e(N-N_0) + C_\mathrm{S}V_\mathrm{S} + C_\mathrm{D}V_\mathrm{D} + C_\mathrm{G}V_\mathrm{G}]^{2}}{2C},
\end{equation}
%
where $eN_0$ is the charge present on the dot when $V_\textrm{SD}=V_\textrm{G}=0$ due to positive background charges in the substrate. One of the central quantity to evaluate whether the current can flow through the dot is the chemical potential, which is the minimum energy required to add an extra electron to the dot, and may be expressed as

\begin{align}
    \mu_\textrm{dot}(N) & = E_\textrm{dot}(N) - E_\mathrm{dot}(N-1), \notag                                                                                                                                       \\
                        & = \left(N-N_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\right) E_{\mathrm{C}}-\frac{E_{\mathrm{C}}}{e}\left(C_{\mathrm{S}} V_{\mathrm{S}}+C_{\mathrm{D}} V_{\mathrm{D}}+C_{\mathrm{G}} V_{\mathrm{G}}\right)+E_{N},
\end{align}
%
where $E_\mathrm{C} = e^2/C$ is the charging energy. In the simplest case as the one describe, it is the interplay between this energy, the single-particle level spacing $\Delta\epsilon$, the coupling $\Gamma$ between the leads and the dot, and the thermal energy $k_\textrm{B}T$ that determine the overall transport regime. In what follows, I will briefly outlined the different transport regimes at the nanoscale based on the relative magnitude of these energy scales.

\subsubsection{Weak-Coupling Regime}\label{ch:theory:sec:weak_limit}

The criteria that has to be satistifed in order to operate in a weak coupling regime is that the lifetime broadening of a molecular state has to be the smallest energy scale in place, i.e. $\Gamma\ll\E_C, E_\textrm{add}(N), k_\textrm{B}T$. This condition is often realised in an electrical break junctions such as the ones used in this thesis.

In this regime, Coulomb blockade (Fig.~\ref{fig:theory:cb}) and single-electron (sequential) tunneling prevails. This transport mechanism typically involves two steps. In the first step, an electron tunnels from one electrode into the molecule: this process is often captured by the notation $N\leftrightarrow N+1$, where the double-ended arrow refers to the possibility of having also an electron going from the dot back to the first electrode. During this event, the spin also changes by $\Delta S = \pm 1 / 2$. In the second step, the electron tunnels from the molecule to the second reservoir. A necessary and sufficient condition for this charge transfer to take place is that the energy levels of the two leads $E_{F,1}$ and $E_{F,2}$ have, at least, an energy equal to the electrochemical potential of the molecule $\mu_N$.

By either applying a bias $eV_\textrm{SD} = E_{F,1} - E_{F,2}$ between the two electrodes or by tuning $\mu_N$ thorugh a gate electrode, it is possible to switch on and off this regime (Fig.~\ref{fig:theory:cb}a). The resulting device conductance may be visualised by plotting it as a function of the gate and bias voltages (\textit{charge stability diagram}). The Coulomb blockade (grey regions) are characterised by very low current levels, and are reffered to as Coulomb diamonds. In these regions transport is blocked because the energy cost to add an electron onto the molecule exceeds the available energy.

\subsubsection{Strong Limit}\label{ch:theory:sec:strong_limit}
When the lifetime broadening of a molecuar/dot state is greater than all the other energy scales at play, i.e. $\Gamma\gg\E_C, E_\textrm{add}(N), k_\textrm{B}T$, a strong hybridisation between lead and dot state occurs, resulting in a suppression of Coulomb blockade by the quantum fluctuations of the electric charge. In this context the dominant transport mechanism is elastic coherent tunneling, and the electrical conductance may be approximated as $G\simeq\frac{2e^2}{h}\mathcal{T}(E)$, where we have indicated with $\mathcal{T}(E)$ the transmission probability for all the available conduction channels thorugh the quantum dot, evaluated at the Fermi energy.

\subsubsection{Intermediate Limit}\label{ch:theory:sec:intermediate_limit}
Finally, the intermediate coupling regime combines characteristics of both weak and strong regime: it retains a noticeable Coulomb blockade, while also exhibiting transport within the zero conductance region. The two most common transport mechanisms of the intermediate coupling regime are elastic and inelastic cotunneling. When compared to sequential single-electron tunneling, these are higher-order transport mechanisms that involve the tunneling of two electrons at the same time. In the first step, the molecular potential of the molecule lies lower in energy than the electrochemical potentials of both leads. When the electron tunnels from the molecule to the drain, and a molecular virtual state is within the bias window, two possible scenarios are possible. In the first scenario, a new electron tunnels from the source electrode to the molecular ground state while simultaneously the electron in the molecular ground state tunnels to the drain electrode. This process, does not involve any energy exchange and is therefore called \textit{elastic cotunneling} (Sec.~\ref{ch:theory:sec:energy_exchange}); experimental signatures are horizontal lines in the zero-conductance region touching a slanted diamond line on the Coulomb edge~\cite{gaudenzi2017transport}. In the second scenario, an electron tunnels into molecular virtual state positioned at an energy $\delta$ away from the Fermy energy in the electrodes, and, as we saw in Sec.\ref{ch:theory:sec:energy_exchange}, results in an exchange of the total energy (\textit{inelastic cotunneling}).

In the case of cotunneling, an electron tunnels into one of the leads as long as another electron tunnels back into the molecule within a short time. This process briefly violates energy conversation, which is quantum-mechanically allowed within a timescale $\tau_\textrm{COT}\sim\hbar/\delta$~\cite{averin1990virtual}. Cotunneling currents are often tiny and difficult to detect, but the effect has been used in quantum dot spectroscopy to explore cotunneling-mediated transport in single quantum dots~\cite{schleser2005cotunneling} or spin effects in double quantum dots~\cite{liu2005pauli}.

\subsubsection{Accounting the Vibrational Degree of Freedoms: the Rate-Equation Model}\label{ch:theory:sec:vibrations}

The rate equation model starts from a kinetic description of the electrode--molecule junction and assumes the overall current flowing through the molecule uniquely determine by how fast an electrons tunnels in and out from a weakly coupled molecular level~\cite{seldenthuis2008vibrational, esposito2010self}. A promising extension of this framework attempts to quantify the quantum mechanical effects of the vibrational \gls{DOF} on the electron transfer rates. Here we will adopt the description reported in references~\cite{thomas2019understanding} and~\cite{sowa2018beyond, sowa2020beyond} where the electronic degrees of freedom interact with an unstructured environment modelled by a superohmic bath having an exponential cut-off:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{J}(\omega)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{\omega^3}{\omega_\textrm{c}}e^{-\omega/\omega_\textrm{c}},
    \label{eq:initial_vibration_equation}
\end{equation}
%
where $\lambda$ is the reorganization energy as defined from the Marcus model for electron transfer in solution and $\omega_\textrm{c}$ is a cut-off frequency.
%
The current through the junction can be written as
\begin{equation}
    I=|e| \frac{\gamma_\text{ox}^\text{S} \gamma_{\text {red}}^\text{D}-\gamma_{\text {red}}^\text{S} \gamma_\text{ox}^\text{D}}{\gamma_{\text {red}}^\text{S}+\gamma_{\text {red}}^\text{D}+\gamma_{ox}^\text{S}+\gamma_{ox}^\text{D}},
\end{equation}
%
\begin{figure}[hbtp]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter_theory/figures/single_level_model.pdf}
        \caption[Schematic of a Single-Level tunneling-Transport Mechanism]
        {
            Schematic for a single-level transport model. The single-particle states having energy $\epsilon_0$ and lifetime $\Gamma$ is the closest to the Fermi level of source $\mu_\textrm{S}$ and drain $\mu_\textrm{D}$, and solely contributes to the total current flowing across the junction. $\Gamma_\textrm{L}$ and $\Gamma_\textrm{R}$ are the tunneling coupling with the left and right electrode, respectively.
        }
        \label{fig:theory:single_level_model}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}
%
where $\gamma_{\text{red/ox}}^\text{S/D}$ are the electron-transfer rates at each electrode corresponding to a reduction or an oxidation. If one consider the charge transition from $N$ to the $N+1$ charge state, the electron rates are given as follows:
%
\begin{align}
    \gamma_{\text{red}}^\text{S,D} & = 2 \frac{\Gamma_\text{S,D}}{\hbar} \int f_\text{S,D}(\epsilon) ~k_\text{red}(\epsilon) d \epsilon,          \\
    \gamma_\text{ox}^\text{S,D}    & =\frac{\Gamma_\text{S,D}}{\hbar} \int\left[1-f_\text{S,D}(\epsilon)\right]~k_\text{ox}(\epsilon) d \epsilon,
\end{align}
%
where $\Gamma_\text{S,D}$ is the electronic coupling between molecule and the electrodes having Fermi-Dirac distributions $f_\text{S,D}(\epsilon)$. The density of states for the reduced and oxidized might be written as:
%
\begin{equation}
    k_{\text{red/ox}}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{\pi} \operatorname{Re} \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{\sigma i(\epsilon-\epsilon_0) t / \hbar} e^{-t / \tau} B(t) d t,
    \label{eq:redox_dos}
\end{equation}
%
where $\sigma=1(-1)$ for reduction (oxidation) process, $\epsilon_0$ is the molecular energy level, $\tau=2 \hbar /\left(\Gamma_\textrm{S}+\Gamma_\textrm{D}\right)$ is the lifetime of the molecular electronic state. The term $B(t)$ is the phononic correlation function
%
\begin{equation}
    B(t)=e^{D_{q}(t)} \cdot e^{D_{\textrm{bath}}(t)},
\end{equation}
%
written as the product between a single-mode term $D_q$ and a mean-field contribution given by the environment. The two terms might be expressed as follows~\cite{todd1994electron}:
%
\begin{align}
    D_{q}(t)         & = \left(\frac{g_{q}}{\omega_{q}}\right)\left[\left(\cos \omega_{q} t-1\right) \cdot \operatorname{coth}\left(\frac{\omega_{q}}{2 k_{B} T}\right)-i \sin \left(\omega_{q} t\right)\right], \\
    D_\text{bath}(t) & = \int \frac{J(\omega)}{\omega^{2}}\left[(\cos \omega t-1) \cdot \operatorname{coth}\left(\frac{\omega}{2 k_{B} T}\right)-i \sin (\omega t)\right] d\omega,
    \label{eq:final_vibration_equation}
\end{align}
%
where $g_{q}$ is the electron--vibration coupling strength, $\omega$ is the vibron frequency, $J(\omega)$ the spectral density of the phonon bath with reorganisation energy $\lambda$. We will use this model in Chapter~\ref{ch:solubility} to reproduce the low temperature transport features of a molecular graphene nanoribbon with a strong electron--vibron coupling.

\section{Basics of One-dimensional Physics}\label{ch:theory:sec:one_dim_physics_intro}
In this section we will very briefly review the Fermi liquid theory and its failure in describing strongly-correlated 1D systems. As a result of the lateral confinement of the carriers, graphene nanoribbons are indeed a quasi-1D system. The term \textit{quasi} refers to the electron confinement in two directions, leaving only one \gls{DOF}. Not until the introduction of bottom-up techniques, which are capable of manufacturing atomically-precise ribbon widths below \SI{5}{\nano\metre}, did this become reality.

The topics introduced here will explain the peculiarities in the density of states for a fermionic system and later be used to derive the tunneling density of states in the presence of long-range Coulomb interactions for a one-dimensional chain. These results are central to explain a few experimental results exposed in Chapter \ref{ch:gnrset}. A more exhaustive treatement of the following concepts might be reviewed in~\cite{giamarchi2003quantum, cazalilla2011one, giamarchi2016one, bertel2017physics}.


\subsection{A Succinct Primer of Fermi-Liquid Theory}
The Fermi liquid theory was advanced by Lev Landau in 1956 as a phenomenological theory to decribe the low-temperature limit behaviour of liquid $^3$He heat capacity, which is surprisingly similar the ideal gas one. Since then, the theory has been used and extend also to capture many-body effects in fermionic systems, such as conducting electrons.

\begin{figure}[hbtp]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter_theory/figures/1d_chain.pdf}
        \caption[Schematic of a One-Dimensional System]{Cartoon representing a 1D electron system. The density of the system is defined as the number of particles $N$ in confined in a 1D box of length $L$, i.e. $\rho=N/L=1/a$, where $a$ is the inter-particle spacing.}
        \label{fig:theory:1d_chain}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}

The ground state of a non-interacting gas may be fully captured following standard statistical mechanics. In this framework, of a great importance in calculating macroscopic quantities is the gas momentum distribution function $n^0_{\vec{k}}$, which depends on the wave number $\vec{k}$. More generally, the ground state depends also on the spin occupation number $\sigma$, but we will drop the spin subscript for the moment. For a fermionic gas, the zero-temperature limit $n^0_{\vec{k}}$ is the well-known step function which equals to:
%
\begin{equation}
    n^0_{\vec{k}} =
    \begin{cases}
        1 & k\le k_\textrm{F} \\
        0 & k > k_\textrm{F}  \\
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
%
At finite temperature, the momentum distribution function coincides with the Fermi--Dirac distribution for metals and with the Bose--Einstein distribution for bosons. The energy of a free particle follows the well-known parabolic dispersion, $\epsilon_{\vec{k}} = \hbar k^2/m$, and the total (multi-electron) energy change caused by an excitation might be expressed as a functional of the energy deviation from the ground state energy $E_0$:
%
\begin{equation}
    \delta E[\delta n] = E[n] - E_0 = \sum_{\vec{k}}\epsilon_{\vec{k}}~\delta n_{\vec{k}}
\end{equation}
%
Thus the single-particle energy might be written as a functional derivative of the total energy with respect to the total number of particles with a specific $\vec{k}$ vector:
%
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon(\vec{k}) = \frac{\delta E[n]}{\delta n_{\vec{k}}}
\end{equation}
%
An elementary excitation can therefore be thought as the process of adding/removing a particle to/from the ground state and an arbitrary excitated state may consist of a number of such particle-hole pairs. Close to the Fermi surface, $\vec{k} = k_\textrm{F}$, the energy dispersion is sufficiently linear enough to apply a Taylor series expansion:
%
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\vec{k}} = \epsilon_\textrm{F} + \upsilon_\textrm{F}\hbar(k - k_\textrm{F}) + \mathcal{O}((k-k_\textrm{F})^2)
\end{equation}
%
where $\upsilon_\textrm{F}=\grad_{\vec{k}}\epsilon|_{\vec{k}=k_\textrm{F}}$ is the Fermi velocity. Landau's intuition was assuming that low-lying eigenstates of the non-interacting Fermi gas continuously evolve into eigenstates of the multi-body (real) system as the interaction is adiabatically switched on. This process leads to the recovery of an individual excitation description, called \textit{quasiparticle}, which is the starting point of the Fermi-liquid theory. Pictorially, a quasiparticle can be visualised as a fermion surrounded by a cloud of disturbance arises due to interaction with other particles. As a consequence, the mass is replaced by to effective mass $m^*=\hbar k_\textrm{F}/\upsilon$ that captures the strenght of the interaction cloud with the single particle and the, as we will se in a moment, the decay of the interaction strength; for the non interacting case, all correlation functions decay as $\sim1/x^2$, where $x$ is the system dimensionality~\cite{giamarchi2003quantum}.

Therefore, the quasiparticle dispersion may be written as:
%
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\vec{k}} = \epsilon_\textrm{F} + \upsilon\hbar(k - k_\textrm{F}) + \mathcal{O}((k-k_\textrm{F})^2)
\end{equation}
%
In order to quantify the quasiparticle excitation energies, we may expande the energy functional with a Taylor's series up to the second order:
%
\begin{equation}
    \delta E[\delta n] = \sum_{\vec{k}} \epsilon_{\vec{k}}~\delta n_{\vec{k}} + \frac{1}{2V} \sum_{\vec{k}} f({\vec{k}}, {\vec{k'}})~\delta n_{\vec{k}} \delta n_{\vec{k'}},
\end{equation}
%
where the intesive quantity:
%
\begin{equation}
    \frac{f ({\vec{k}}, {\vec{k'}})}{V} = \frac{\delta^2 E[n]}{\delta_{\vec{k}} \delta_{\vec{k'}}},
\end{equation}
%
is known as Landau function and quantifies the interaction strength between quasiparticles. The energy of a quasiparticles can be similarly defined as:
%
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\epsilon}_{\vec{k}} = \frac{\delta E[\delta n ]}{\delta n_{\vec{k}}} = \epsilon_{\vec{k}} + \frac{1}{V}\sum_{\vec{k'}} f({\vec{k}},{\vec{k'}})\delta_{\vec{k'}}.
\end{equation}.

Why does the Fermi liquid theory fail to capture 1D systems? Essentially because the Landau function is treated by means of a mean-field approximation and a mean-field-kind description breaks down in 1D~\cite{giamarchi2003quantum}. This is caused by several reasons.

Firstly, for a system arranged in a linear chain fashion, only collective excitations exist. Pictorially, one might covince herself by thinking of an electron which starts to oscillate around its equilibrium position. Due to the strong electron--electron interactions, the motion is fastly trasmitted all along the chain, resulting in a collective excitation. Secondly, in high dimensions we have seen that even if the particles interact with each other one can still recover an individual excitation description, where the interaction results in the concept of quasiparticles. In the next section we will take a look at how all these issues have been rationalised in 1D through the study of the Tomonaga--Luttinger model.

\subsection{Introduction to the Tomonaga--Luttinger Model}\label{ch2:derivation_tunneling_DOS}

As we have seen, 1D excitations for an interacting fermionic systems may be adequately described as hydrodynamic-like modes which are similar to sound waves, and that are split into charge- and spin-density waves~\cite{giamarchi2016one}. The absence of an efficient charge screening leads to long-range interactions, which cause strong correlations and the breakdown of an independent-particle description. These correlations are the main reason for the necessity of computing correlation functions for calculating any quantity of interest in 1D systems, hence the wide employment of Green's function methods (propagators).

One consequence of this uniqueness is that, conveniently, it is often sufficient to conduct an asymptotic analysis for capturing the physical insights of 1D systems. In particular, one has to provide the wave velocity $u$ and the interaction parameter $K$ (also called $g$), a dimensionless number that controls the decay of all the correlation functions, as well as the thermodynamic quantities~\cite{cazalilla2011one}.

The first model proposed in 1950 by Tomonaga aimed to describe the multi-fermionic ground state of an interacting Fermi gas and its excitations by treating them the solutions to field equations as sound waves~\cite{tomonaga1950remarks}. The model was later extended by Luttinger in 1963 in an attempt to include interaction of arbitrary strengths~\cite{luttinger1963exactly}. The correct solution was firstly advanced by Mattis and Lieb in 1965~\cite{mattis1994exact} and finalised by a genial intuition from Haldane in 1981~\cite{haldane1981luttinger}.

A central tool required to arrive to Haldane's solution is bosonisation, a mathematical technique which maps the excitation of fermions to bosonic modes of spin-density and charge-density waves and which prerequisite is the possibility of linearise the  single-particle (fermionic) dispersion close to the Fermi level~\cite{von1998bosonization}. This linear approximation is pushed to the extreme in the TLL model, meaning that fermions occupy the linearised energy spectrum all the way down to $-\infty$ on the energy scale (also called Dirac sea, Fig.~\ref{fig:theory:TLL}). In order to apply this assumption, the density of states has to be constant. Broadly speaking, TLL hamiltonian might be written as the sum between kinetic and interaction contributions:
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_\textrm{TLL} = \mathcal{H}_0 + \mathcal{H}_\textrm{int}.
\end{equation}
%
Using the second quantisation formalism, the kinetic part may be written as~\cite{giamarchi2003quantum}:
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_0 = \sum_{k; r = R,L} \upsilon_\textrm{F}(\epsilon_r k - k_\textrm{F})c^\dagger_{k, r} c_{k, r},
\end{equation}
%
where we have dropped the arrow notation for the wavevector as we are now dealing only with one dimension. Nothing fancy here: $c^\dagger_{k,r} (c_{k,r})$ is the creation (annihilation) operator acting in corresponding Fock space.
%
\begin{figure}[hbtp]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter_theory/figures/TLL_model.pdf}
        \caption[Schematic of The Tomonaga--Luttinger Liquid Model]{The Tomonaga--Luttinger-liquid (TLL) model. \textbf{(a)} Free electron parabolic dispersion (black solid curve) and the linearisation carried out according to the TLL model for excitations close the Fermi level (pink and blue dashed lines). \textbf{(b)} The linear dispersion used in the TLL model. Pink and blue solid lines correspond, to left-(L) and right (R)-moving electrons, respectively. Shaded area shows the Dirac sea below the Fermi energy. Figure adapted from~\cite{jin2020measurement}.}
        \label{fig:theory:TLL}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}
%
Because the Fermi surface in 1D consists of just two points at $k=\pm k_\textrm{F}$, we can distinguish states in the right branch ($\epsilon_R=+1$) from those in the left ($\epsilon_L=-1$) branch, and the corresponding dispersion relations are:
%
\begin{align}
    \epsilon_{k, R} & = \epsilon_0 + \hbar \upsilon_\textrm{F} k                     \\
    \epsilon_{k, L} & = \epsilon_0 - \hbar \upsilon_\textrm{F} k                     \\
    \epsilon_{0}    & = \epsilon_\textrm{F} + \hbar \upsilon_\textrm{F} k_\textrm{F}
\end{align}
%
Since states in the $\epsilon_R=+1$ branch have positive velocities they are also referred to as \textit{right movers}; conversely, states in the left branch are referred as \textit{left movers}.

The interaction term is where the fun begins. In order to write it down, we shall first introduce a central quantity when one deals with 1D. Because the system is subject to quantum fluctuations, even at zero-temperature processes close to the Fermi surface will drive the system response~\cite{giamarchi2006strong}. Low-energy processes might be well captured by introducing the density operator\footnote[4]{A technicality here. Since the model includes all the negative states, the density operator as it has been written in Eq.~\ref{eq:theory:density_op} is infinite as the number of fermions is infinite. One has to introduce the \textit{normal ordering} of operators, meaning that what matters are deviations in the density with respect to the density of the ground state~\cite{von1998bosonization}.}:
%
\begin{equation}
    \rho^\dagger(q) = \sum_k c^\dagger_{k+q, r}c_{k,r},
    \label{eq:theory:density_op}
\end{equation}
%
and the interaction may be written as:
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_\textrm{int}=\frac{1}{2V}\sum_{k,k',q}V(q)c^\dagger_{k+q}c^\dagger_{k'-q}c_{k'}c_{k},
    \label{eq:theory:lutt_int}
\end{equation}
%
As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:theory:excitation_spectrum}, since $k$ and $k-q$ are restricted to be close the two points at $\pm k_\textrm{F}$, there are only two possible value of $q$ depending on the sign of $k$ and $k-q$. When $k$ and $k-q$ have the same sign, $\abs{q}\sim 0$. This corresponds to a scattering term for fermions moving in the same direction and might be interpreted as a \textit{forward scattering}. When $k$ and $k-q$ have opposite sign, $\abs{q}\sim 2k_\textrm{F}$, meaning that in this excitation fermions exchange side (\textit{backward scattering}).

The problem with the term in Eq.~\ref{eq:theory:lutt_int} is that it is the product between two density operators. When these are expressed in the fermion operator basis, they will expand to be a quartic function of the fermion operators, and problems then arise becuase is all but trivial to diagonalise a quartic Hamiltonian. Haldane's intuition~\cite{haldane1981luttinger} was to map the problem, through a change of the basis, into the bosonic-operator domain, where the interaction is reduced to a quadratic form, so that a closed-form solution is attainable. This process will be briefly outlined in the next section when I will derive the tunneling density of states in case for a fermion tunneling through a Luttinger liquid.


\subsection{Derivation of the Tunneling Density of States}

Here, we will briefly sketch the derivation of the \gls{TDOS} for a Luttinger liquid~\cite{egger1997effective, egger1999luttinger, kane1997coulomb}. We will use the second quantization formalism, in which both the wavefunctions and the operators are expressed as operators (more generally, fields) acting in the Fock space. This formalism greatly simplifies the description of multi-electron interacting systems in situations where single-particle interactions fail to capture all the features. The short and medium correlations between different parts of the system, well captured by the Green's function (propagator) between operators associated with a certain observable between different time frames, are central to this formalism.


The \gls{TDOS} may be written as
%
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\operatorname{dos}}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{d t}{2 \pi} e^{i \omega t}\left\langle\psi_{e}(t) \psi_{e}^\dagger(0)\right\rangle,
\end{equation}
%
where $\psi_{e}(t)$ is the time-dependent fermionic operator (field), $\omega$ is an energy scale, and the brackets indicate the propagator (Green's function) as introduced in Sec.~\ref{ch:theory:sec:one_dim_physics_intro}. The mapping between fermionic and bosonic operators takes place through bosonization~\cite{von1998bosonization}, which allows writing fermionic operators in terms of bosonic fields.
We then may write the bosonized form of the fermionic operator as:
%
\begin{equation}
    \psi_{e} = \psi_{L}+\psi_{R},
\end{equation}
%
where $\psi_{L/R}$ are the left/right fermionic operators for left-moving and right-moving carriers\footnote[1]{This argument, as will be explained later, assumes that chirality symmetry is respected. As a result, applying a strong magnetic field that may cause chiral-symmetry breaking compel this argument to be adjusted~\cite{roy2014chiral}.}, which may be written in terms of a bosonic operator $e^{-i \varphi_{L/R}}$ normalized by the inter-particle spacing $a$ when particles are confined along a 1D chain
$\psi_{L / R}=e^{-i \varphi_{L / R}}/\sqrt{a}.$

In order to diagonalise the Luttinger liquid Hamiltonian, it may be convenient to introduce the following fields:
\begin{equation}
    \phi_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} g^{\mp\frac{1}{2}}\left(\varphi_{L} \mp \varphi_{R}\right),
\end{equation}
%
where $g>0$ (for free electrons $g=1$) is a dimensionless parameter which quantifies the electron--electron interactions in a 1D quantum wire. These fields have also the peculiarity to be canonically conjugated to the displacement field $\theta(x)$ which derivative models the electron density fluctuations:
%
\begin{equation}
    \delta n(x) = n(x) - n_0 = =\frac{\partial_x \theta(x)}{\pi},
\end{equation}
%
where $n_0$ is the average density. This expression is obtained by considering that if $\theta(a)- \theta(0)= -\pi$, then only one extra electron is between two particles separated by a distance $a$.

The two fermionic fields thus read:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
         & \psi_{L}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} e^{-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}} \phi_{-}+\sqrt{g} \phi_{+}\right)}, \\
         & \psi_{R}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} e^{-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}} \phi_{-}-\sqrt{g} \phi_{+}\right)},
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
%
while the fermionic propagator may be written as
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \left\langle\psi_{e}(t) \psi_{e}^+(0)\right\rangle= & \left\langle\psi_{L}(t) \psi_{L}(0)\right\rangle+\left\langle\psi_{R}(t) \psi_{R}(0)\right\rangle \\
        =                                                   & \left\langle\psi_{L}(t) \psi_{L}(0)\right\rangle+ \text{h.c.}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
%
where $\textrm{h.c.}$ represents the Hermitian conjugate terms in the propagator formula. Cross terms such as $\left\langle\psi_{L}^{\dagger} \psi_{R}\right\rangle$ cancel each other since we assume chirality is conserved. For the remaining terms the sum can be opened such as:
%
\begin{align}
    \left\langle\psi_{L}^{\dagger}(t) \psi_{L}(0)\right\rangle & = \frac{1}{a}\left\langle e^{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\phi_{-}(t) \sqrt{g}\phi_{+}(t))} e^{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\phi_{-}(0) \sqrt{g}\phi_{+}(0))}\right\rangle,                          \\
                                                               & = \frac{1}{a}\left\langle e^{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2g}}\phi_{-}(t)} e^{\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2g}}\phi_{-}(0)}\right\rangle\times\left\langle e^{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2g}}\phi_{+}(t)} e^{\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2g}}\phi_{+}(0)}\right\rangle,
\end{align}
%
By using the Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff identity, which is valid for exponential operators and is obeyed by free bosonic fields
%
\begin{equation}
    \left\langle e^{B_{1}} e^{B_{2}}\right\rangle=e^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\langle B_{1}^{2}+2 B_{1} B_{2}+B_{2}^{2}\right\rangle,
\end{equation}
%
and time-translational symmetry, we obtain:
%
\begin{align*}
    \left\langle e^{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2g}} \phi_{-}(t)} e^{-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2g}} \phi_{-}(0)}\right\rangle
     & = e^{\frac{1}{2g}\left\langle \phi_{-}(t)\phi_{-}(0)-\phi_{-}(0) \phi_{-}(0)\right\rangle} \\
     & = \left(1+\frac{i\upsilon_{F}t}{a} \right)^{-\frac{1}{2 g}}
\end{align*}
%
where we introduce the Fermi velocity $v_\textrm{F}$ and used the relation
\begin{equation}
    \left\langle\phi_{-}(t)\phi_{-}(0)\right\rangle = -\ln(1+\frac{i\upsilon_{F}t}{a})
\end{equation}
%
Similarly
\begin{equation}
    \left\langle e^{i\sqrt{\frac{g}{2}} \phi_{+}(t)} e^{-i\sqrt{\frac{g}{2}}\phi_{+}(0)}\right\rangle = (1+i\upsilon_{F}t/a)^{-\frac{g}{2}}
\end{equation}
%
and by combining the two terms, the fermionic propagator reads:
\begin{equation}
    \left\langle\psi_{e}(t)\psi_{e}(0)\right\rangle = \frac{1}{a}(1+\frac{i\upsilon_\textrm{F}t}{a})^{\frac{1}{2}(g+g^{-1})}
\end{equation}
%
Incorporating this expression into the TDOS yields:
%
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \rho_{\operatorname{dos}}(\omega) & =\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\textrm{d t}}{2 \pi} e^{i \omega t}\left\langle\psi_{e}(t) \psi_{e}^{\dagger}(0)\right\rangle                             \\
                                          & =\frac{1}{2 \pi a} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \textrm{d t} e^{i \omega t}\left(1+\frac{i\upsilon_\textrm{F}t}{a}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(g+g^{-1}\right)}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
%
Since we are interested in the low-energy behavior of the TDOS, we look at the limit for which $\omega\rightarrow0$, which in the time-domain corresponds to look at the behavior for $t\rightarrow\infty$:

\begin{align}\label{eq:luttinger_exponent}
    \begin{split}
        \rho_{\operatorname{dos}}(\omega) & \sim \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\textrm{d t} \frac{e^{i\omega t }}{t^{1/2(g+g^{-1})}} \\
        & \sim \omega^{\frac{1}{2}(g + g^{-1})-1}= \omega^{\alpha}
    \end{split}
\end{align}



\subsection{Experimental Evidence: Luttinger Liquid in Carbon Nanotubes}

In interacting 1D systems such as \gls{CNT}, the electrons behaviour cannot be captured within the Fermi liquid theory, but by Luttinger-liquid models~\cite{egger1997effective, egger1999luttinger,kane1997coulomb}. In these models, spin and charge excitations are described as bosonic modes, are separable, and propagate at different velocities. Furthermore, the electron--electron interaction leads to a \gls{TDOS} suppression and interaction-dependent power laws for transport quantities.
The experimental evidence of such interactions was revealed through a series of remarkable experiments first on individual \gls{CNT} bundle (also called \textit{rope}), i.e.\ multiples tubes closely packed together and later on single \gls{CNT}.

One of such experiments, for instance, was performed by \citeauthor{bockrath1999luttinger} on carbon nanotubes ropes contacted either from the top (end-contact) or from below (contacting the tube along its bulk)~\cite{bockrath1999luttinger}. In both cases, researchers found a power-law scaling of the conductance measured at zero bias as the temperature was increased, i.e.\ $G(T) \sim T^\alpha$. The phenomenological interpretation of the exponent $\alpha$ the author gave was that, depending on the fabrication details, the power law exponent may change. In particular, they reported an $\alpha_{\text{end}}\simeq 0.6$, whereas in the case of bulk contact the suppression of the \gls{TDOS} is weaker, setting an $\alpha_{\text{bulk}}\simeq 0.3$. Comparing these values with the expected electron--electron interaction predicted by the theory yielded a $g\simeq 0.28$.

The Luttinger parameter, $g$, which characterizes the strength and sign of the electron interaction, can be related to readily measured nanotube parameters $E_{\mathrm{C}}$, the charging energy, and $\Delta$ the mean orbital level spacing by $g=\left(1+2 E_{\mathrm{C}} / \Delta\right)^{-1 / 2}$.

Further experimental evidences, however, demonstrated that the interpretation of the tunneling exponent may be not that trivial.  Postma et al. measured the conductance of electrons tunneling inside a carbon nanotubes QD where the tunneling barriers were introduced by mechanically bending the nanotube with an AFM tip. Since the \gls{QD} was not end-contacted they explained the conductance-suppression exponent as the result of a tunneling event from a Tomonaga--Luttinger liquid (the nanotube) into a Luttinger island (the quantum dot hosted inside the tube). In this case, the conductance was strongly suppressed, reporting an $\alpha_{\text {bulk }} \sim 0.64$, and rationalised by strong electron--electron interactions $g\sim0.23$ as expected from theory~\cite{egger1999luttinger}. This observation was hypothesised to be the consequence of a different kind of tunneling mechanism called \textit{correlated sequential tunneling}~\cite{thorwart2002correlated}. Briefly, in this mechanism the once the electron tunnel on the \gls{QD} it does not immediately tunnels onto the drain but there is a dephasing due to interaction with plasmon modes.

\subsection{Concluding Remarks}

For the process associated with a tunneling event into the bulk of a \gls{CNT} the exponent of Eq.~\ref{eq:luttinger_exponent} is expected to be $\alpha\simeq 0.4$~\cite{kane1997coulomb}. As we have seen in Ch.~\ref{ch:introduction}, this exponent can be phenomenologically interpreted as the strength of the electron--electron interactions, which lead to a suppression of the conductance when an excess of energy (thermal or electric) is pumped into the system.
The approach detailed in this chapter is, as we have mentioned, applicable to any 1D. As such, it should be possible to describe \glspl{GNR}, and in particular \glspl{MGNR}, within this framework, especially in the $l_\textrm{GNR}\gg w_\textrm{GNR}$ limit. Furthermore, in \glspl{MGNR}, the graphene band structure allows us to label both the valley and the spin degree of freedoms, amounting to a total four-fold degeneracy of the band structure: this should lead to a \gls{TDOS} whose tunneling exponent into one of the \gls{MGNR} ends is $\alpha\simeq\frac{1}{4}(g^{-1}-1)$~\cite{mattsson1997properties, fabrizio1995interacting}. In Ch.~\ref{ch:gnrset} we will outiline the experiment we have setup in order to confirm this theoretical estimate.

\section{Graphene Nanoribbons}

The graphene band structure described in Ch.~\ref{ch:introduction} shows that it is a semimetal, and that many electronics applications rely on the employment of a semiconductor with a finite band gap, through which one can modulate the charge transfer. Similar to \glspl{CNT}, \glspl{GNR}\footnote[2]{Throughout the thesis, we will use the abbreviation \gls{GNR} to qualify any graphene nanoribbons. When we use the abbreviation \gls{MGNR} is because we are identifing ribbon synthesised through \textit{bottom-up} strategies.} are described in terms of an energy gap caused by the lateral quantum confinement of the carriers~\cite{xu2016recent}, in particular depends on the width $w_\textrm{GNR}$ and length $l_\textrm{GNR}$ of the ribbon~\cite{Han2007}.

Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:gnr-bandgap_schwierz} shows various ribbon widths and their corresponding bandgaps, as reported by theoretical and experimental studies. Moreover, this graph illustrates the paucity of ribbons with suitable widths when optimal bandgap range for \gls{CMOS} applications is considered to be in the \qtyrange[range-units = bracket]{0.1}{1}{\electronvolt} range~\cite{geng2017graphene, schwierz2013graphene}. One of the key reasons for the scarcity of appropriate \glspl{GNR} is that the bottom-up process for the synthesis of graphene ribbons was still in its infancy at the time this graph was created.

The \textit{bottom-up} methodology is a technique for synthesising graphene ribbons in which the ribbon structure is created from molecular precursors reacting using reaction patterns commonly inherited from polymer chemistry and pericyclic reactions. Pioneered by two seminal papers by Hongjie Dai~\cite{li2008chemically} and Klaus M{\"u}llen~\cite{cai2010atomically}, it is now deemed the ideal technique to produce high-quality graphene nanoribbons.

With these class of techniques, hundred nanometer-long ribbons with widths less than \SI{5}{\nano\metre} have been synthesised~\cite{narita2015bottom}, which may be appropriate for interconnection applications requiring high current density to be maintained without causing voltage drop. Secondly, important nano-and- optoelectronics properties such as electronic bandgap, charge-carrier mobility, and optical absorption have become tunable by either varying \glspl{GNR} chemical structures or by introducing opportune decorations along the ribbon edges~\cite{Liu2015, hu2018bandgap}. Fig.\ref{fig:intro:gnr-canvas} gives a glimpse on the vast variety of edge structures generated by bottom-up techniques, suggesting the effectiveness and versatility of this approach over early \textit{top-down} approaches. These were typically accomplished by either patterning ribbons through lithography with \gls{EBL}~\cite{RN345, Han2007} or by longitudinal unzipping of single-walled nanotubes~\cite{kosynkin2009longitudinal}, or by cutting graphene sheets with metallic nanoparticles~\cite{RN346}. These methods, however, frequently produced wide widths and uncontrollable structural defects~\cite{han2010electron, Evaldsson2008}. Finally, bottom-up technology has made it possible to synthetically control the kind of defects injected into the carbon lattice, and this technological superiority is considered by many to be a substantial advantage compared to graphene and carbon nanotubes~\cite{lombardi2021synthetic,lombardi2019quantum}.

\subsection{Crystal and Band Structure}
\Glspl{AGNR} and \gls{ZGNR} (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:gnr-canvas}) are the two main ribbon geometries that were first investigated, and the different connectivity the atoms have at the edges has a significant impact on the respective band structures~\cite{wakabayashi2010electronic, son2006energy, celis2016graphene, barone2006electronic}.

In general, the energy spectrum of a \gls{GNR} with length $l_\textrm{GNR}$ and width $w_\text{GNR}$ may be approximately written as:
%
\begin{equation}
    E_{n,m} = \pm\hbar v_\textrm{F}^*~\sqrt{\qty(\frac{n\pi}{l_\textrm{GNR}})^2 + \qty(\frac{m\pi}{w_\text{gnr}})^2}
    \label{eq:gnr_energy}
\end{equation}
%
where $v_\textrm{F}^*$ is the group velocity for an energy band indexed by the pair of quantum numbers $n,m$~\cite{baringhaus2014exceptional}.

Steven Louie's group was one of the first to characterise the band structure properties of \glspl{AGNR} based on the number of carbon dimer lines $N$ perpendicular to the ribbon width using \gls{TB} calculations~\cite{son2006energy}. This seminal work revealed that when $N$ is a multiple of three of an integer $p$, resulting ribbons feature large bandgaps that scale inversely with the width of the ribbon\footnote[4]{The $E_g \propto 1/w_\text{gnr}$ relation holds when $\SI{15}{\nano\metre} \le w_\text{gnr} \le \SI{90}{\nano\metre}$~\cite{yang2007quasiparticle}} (Tab.~\ref{tab:agnr-tb}), and, even when the criterion $N=3p+1$ is met, this trend persists qualitatively. On the other hand, if $N=3p+2$, \glspl{AGNR} are metallic~\cite{nakada1996edge}. When the width of the ribbon falls below \SI{10}{\nano\metre}, electron--electron interactions cannot be no longer excluded, and theyr introduce corrective terms to the above-mentioned criteria (Tab.\ref{tab:agnr-gw})~\cite{yang2007quasiparticle}.

These theoretical estimates were for the first time observed and quantified experimentally on surface-synthesised 5-atom wide \glspl{AGNR} through \gls{STM}, reporting bandgaps of $E_g\simeq\SI{100}{\milli\electronvolt}$~\cite{kimouche2015ultra, de2016substrate, carbonell2018electronic}.~\citeauthor{kimouche2015ultra} experiment aimed, in particular, at quantifying the orbital energy size as a function of $l_\textrm{GNR}$ by measuring the differential conductance of increasingly longer ribbons, and to shed some light on the implication that finite-size effects have on the bandgap scaling with $l_\textrm{GNR}$.

The electronic properties of \gls{ZGNR} are qualitatively and quantitatively distinct from those of \glspl{AGNR}. In order to define the unit cell of a \gls{ZGNR}, $2\times4=8$ atoms per unit cell are required, as opposed to $4\times3+2=14$ atoms for an \gls{AGNR} (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:gnr-canvas}). The positions of the two non-equivalent atoms in the unit cell and the translation symmetry of the lattice give rise to a $2_1$ screw axis along the width direction. Most notably, the presence of glide planes and screw axes in nonsymmorphic space groups\footnote[3]{Nonsymmorphic groups are space groups that are not uniquely defined by the lattice translations repeating of a point-group motif. Te and $\alpha$-quartz are examples of nonsymmorphic groups.}, which include also the wavevector group, causes energy bands to converge along certain high symmetry points and axes in the Brillouin zone~\cite{dresselhaus2007group}. This \textit{flat band} region is positioned at wavevectors $2\pi/3 \le k \le \pi$, and the electronic states in this region have been interpreted as localised states along the zigzag edges by looking at charge distribution maps~\cite{wakabayashi2010electronic}. In one-dimensional systems, these electronic states are known as \textit{edge states}, and their contribution to the entire wavefunction becomes minimal when their widths approach the micrometre range, other than exhibiting an exponential decay of their \gls{DOS} with distance from the ribbon centre~\cite{kobayashi2006edge,son2006energy}.

When the wavevector is between $\left[-\frac{\pi}{d_z}, -\frac{2\pi}{3d_z}\right]$ and $\left[\frac{2\pi}{3d_z}, \frac{\pi}{d_z}\right]$, where $d_z$ is the unit cell distance, a bandgap is opened in the band structure (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:zgnr_bandgap_edge_state_delocalisation}c). It has been proven by spin-polarized ab-initio calculations that two distinct magnetic orderings are distinguishable in the ground state: a ferromagnetic ($\uparrow\uparrow$, FM) configuration for spins aligned along a single edge and an antiferromagnetic ($\downarrow\downarrow$, AFM) condition for spins belonging to two distinct edges~\cite{son2006half}. Numerical studies have also suggested that \gls{GNR} could display \textit{half metallicity}, i.e.\ to undergo a phase transition from an AFM configuration (semiconducting) to an FM state (metallic) when a transverse electric field is applied perpendicular to the ribbon plane~\cite{son2006half, dutta2009intrinsic}.

Similar to \glspl{CNT}, \glspl{GNR} can have chiral edges. Typically, chirality may be described by a chiral angle $\theta$ which may be defined as:
%
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \arcsin\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}\qty(\frac{m^2}{n^2+nm+m^2})}
\end{equation}
%
where the nanoribbon edge orientation is determined by translation vector $(n,m)$ of the graphene lattice. Zigzag and armchair are characterised by $\theta=0^\circ$ and $\theta=30^\circ$, respectively, while chiral edges have $\theta$ in the range between.
Steven Louie and coworkers employed a mean-field Hubbard Hamiltonian for modelling the \gls{GNR} dispersion relation, assuming the on-site Coulomb repulsion $U$ comparable to the hopping integral $t$ between two adjacent sites ($U/t \sim 1$):
%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gnr_chiral_hubbard_hamiltonian}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{H}_{\textrm{MF}} = &- t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,~\sigma}\qty(a^\dagger_{\sigma,i}a_{\sigma,j} + \textrm{h.c.}) \\
        &+ U\sum_{i}\qty(n_{i\uparrow}\langle n_{i\downarrow}\rangle + \langle n_{i\uparrow}\rangle n_{i\downarrow} - \langle n_{i\uparrow}\rangle \langle n_{i\downarrow}\rangle),
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
%
whereby the second summation is performed over spin-polarised densities $n_{i\sigma} = a^\dagger_{i\sigma}a_{i\sigma}$. This Hamiltonian preserves the electron--hole symmetry, which means that the density of states is symmetric with respect to the Fermi level.

\subsection{Edge States}
A few years later, the chiral \gls{DOS} for the electronic states at the ribbon edges had to be incorporated into the interpretation of the previous mentioned localised states and the exotic features that followed these~\cite{yazyev2011theory}. Chirality is a remarkable phenomenon that arises when the charge carriers' time-reversal symmetry is broken, and it is possible to discriminate between carrier wavefunctions evolving in time with a clockwise phase and those evolving with a counterclockwise phase. In the molecular domain, geometrical chirality refers to any molecule belonging to a symmetry group without improper rotations, i.e. the spatial symmetry is such that the rotamer cannot be superimposed on its mirror image~\cite{lazzeretti2017chiral}.

The experimental evidence is captured by the solutions of the associated eigenvalue problem, which predict the origin of two peaks splitting in the \gls{DOS}, which have been interpreted as manifestation of two opposite magnetic orderings. One peak split caused by the AFM correlation between opposite edges, whereas the second, greater peak split should be caused by FM ordering along a single edge (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:zgnr_bandgap_edge_state_delocalisation}b,c). The energy difference between the two magnetic orderings was estimated to be of order of tens of \si{\milli\electronvolt} per edge atom and to decrease as the ribbon width increases, eventually becoming negligible when the width is significantly larger than the exponential decay length of spin-polarised states ($\lambda\simeq\SIrange{2}{5}{\nano\meter}$)~\cite{lee2005magnetic}. In theory, it should then be possible to probe this energy difference by either studying the \gls{TDOS} or by using high-frequency-resonance techniques.

Although the experimental evidence for the edge states\footnote[2]{The peculiarities of these edge states is that they are instrinsically magnetic} of \glspl{ZGNR} and chiral \glspl{GNR} still remains elusive, \citeauthor{slota2018magnetic} have demonstrated, using electron-spin resonance techniques, that magnetic edge states can be induced in cove \glspl{GNR}. Stable nitronil-nitroxide radicals were grafted along the edges as spin injectors, allowing to separate the magnetic edge fingerprint from the signal of the radical. In addition, their study shown that the edge state has longer coherence times than the majority of $\pi$-extended organic systems, even at room temperature and in powder, which makes graphene nanoribbons particularly interesting for spintronic applications.

The ribbon was synthesised through sonochemistry methods, and deposited on a \ce{Si}/\ce{SiO2} substrate, with electrical contacts realised in Pd. As previously discussed, the large work function of Pd should reduce the back-injection of the minority carriers in the conductive channel. \Citeauthor{wang2008room} examined the impact of ribbon widths as small as $w_\textrm{GNR}\simeq$ \SI[multi-part-units = single]{2.0(5)}{\nano\metre} on the device performance, while the ribbon lengths were kept roughly constant $l_\textrm{GNR}\simeq\SI{236}{\nano\metre}$.

Despite a few remarkable transistor key numbers such as $I_\textrm{on}\simeq\SI{2000}{\micro\ampere\per\micro\metre}$, $I_\textrm{on}/I_\textrm{off}\simeq 10^6$, $SS\simeq\SI{210}{\milli\volt}$~dec$^{-1}$, and $g_\textrm{m} = 900$ \si{\micro\siemens\per\micro\metre}, lower carrier mobilities $\mu\simeq$ \SIrange{50}{200}{\centi\metre\squared\per\volt\per\second} were measured for narrower ribbons. These results led the researchers to two conclusions. Firstly, narrow \glspl{GNR} entail in higher bandgaps, leading to lower mobilities~\cite{peter2010fundamentals}. Secondly, scattering at the edge effects may dominate carrier transport in narrow $w_\textrm{GNR}\le\SI{5}{\nano\metre}$ ribbons. \Citeauthor{wang2008room} proposed a phenomenological model in which edges, defects, and phonons were regarded as independent factors in order to link the mean freee path $\ell$ with the $w_\textrm{GNR}$ and $E_g$~\cite{wang2008room}:
%
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\ell} = \frac{1}{\ell_\textrm{edge}} + \frac{1}{\ell_\textrm{phonon}} + \frac{1}{\ell_\textrm{defect}}.
\end{equation}
%
From the definition of mean free path (see also Sec.~\ref{ch:theory:sec:energy_exchange}), the likelihood $P$ of a scattering event to happen may be phenomenologically conceived as the inverse of the average time between two consecutive collisions:
%
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:mfp_edge}
    \ell_\textrm{edge} = \frac{k_\parallel w_\textrm{gnr}}{P k_\perp},
\end{equation}
%
As we saw in the previous section, when $w_\textrm{GNR}<\SI{20}{\nano\metre}$, it is possible to approximate the ribbon energy gap as inversely proportional to the ribbon width, provided electron--electron correlations are neglected~\cite{stampfer2008tunable}:
%
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:bandgap_width}
    E_g\simeq\frac{\hbar v_\textrm{F}}{w_\textrm{gnr}}.
\end{equation}
%
By inserting Eq.~\ref{ch1:eq:bandgap_width} and the dispersion relation $E_k$ into Eq.~\ref{ch1:eq:mfp_edge}, an expression for the dependence on the width of the mean free path can be written as:
%
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:mfp_edge_backscattering}
    \ell_\textrm{edge} = \frac{w}{P}\sqrt{\qty(1 + \frac{E_k}{E_g})^2 - 1}
\end{equation}
%
According to Eq.~\ref{ch1:eq:mfp_edge_backscattering}, both a wider width and well-defined edges should improve the $\ell$. These hypotheses were experimentally confirmed by higher mobilities when \glspl{GNR} as wide as \SI{10}{\nano\metre} were measured~\cite{wang2008room, geng2017graphene}.

\subsection{Bandgap Engineering}
The theoretically predicted relationship between ribbon width and energy gap was confirmed experimentally in 2007 by Philip Kim's team (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:han2007_EgvsW}) and shed lights on the difference between a true energy gap and the transport gap typically measured in an electron transport experiment~\cite{Han2007,han2010electron}.

The experiments they perfomed suggested that edge disorders may produce charge localisations, and this effect becomes greater as the ribbon length increases~\cite{areshkin2007ballistic, martin2009transport, chen2007graphene}. This hypothesis was later confirmed by a detailed analysis conducted by the group of Manchester, which demonstrated that the electron--electron interactions cannot be neglected when the ribbon width is reduced below \SI{5}{\nano\metre} and that the conductance is likely to be suppressed by Coulomb blockade, which also explained the deviation of larger energy gaps for small ribbons compared to theoretical studies~\cite{martin2009transport, ponomarenko2008chaotic}.

In an attempt to solve this conundrum, Kim's group suggested an explanation in between. Charge transfer via hopping through localised states will lead to a transport gap rather than a single energy gap when the distance between these state is of the order of magnitude of the ribbon size~\cite{han2010electron}. Through electrical transport experiments, \citeauthor{han2010electron} advanced the explanation that the gate voltage region $\Delta V_\text{G}$ where the conductance is suppressed may be related to the single-particle energy gap via~\cite{castroneto2009}:
%
\begin{equation}
    E_g = \hbar v_\textrm{F}\sqrt{2\pi C_\textrm{G} \Delta V_\text{G}/\abs{e}},
\end{equation}
%
where $C_\textrm{G}$ is the capacitive coupling between the ribbon and the gate.

In addition, \Citeauthor{han2010electron} measured the conductance temperature-dependence, reporting a reported a strong suppression of the minimum of conductance $G_\textrm{min}$, which they interpreted by considering a thermally-activated process of the carriers, in contrast to first earlier suggestions which explained the observation within the Coulomb blockade theory~\cite{beenakker1991theory}. Even with different ribbon widths, the scientists discovered a linear scaling of the conductance $G = \sigma(w_\textrm{GNR} - w_0)/l_\textrm{GNR}$ when this was normalised across an active transport region $(w_\textrm{GNR} - w_0)$, being $\sigma\simeq \SI{1.7}{\milli\siemens}$ for the sheet conductivity. Comparing similar trends reported for \gls{SB}-restricted junctions led to the conclusion that the graphene nanoribbons-metal connection may have been limited by the formation of a Schottky barrier that dominates the charge injection~\cite{DiBartolomeo2016}.

\subsection{Graphene Nanoribbons Electronics}
Utilizing pure carbon nanotubes and compressing them under high pressure to make graphene nanoribbons has recently yielded excellent device performance~\cite{chen2021sub}. The nanotube samples were loaded and sealed in a sample chamber positioned in the centre of a pre-indented tungsten gasket, which was then compressed between two diamond anvils from ambient pressure to \SI{22.8}{\giga\pascal} while the structural evolution was monitored by in-situ Raman spectroscopy through the diamond window. The authors observed a resonant radial breathing mode peak at 116 cm$^{-1}$, corresponding to a nanotube with a diameter  $\simeq\SI{2.1}{\nano\metre}$, dropping in peak intensity as the pressure climbed, and finally becoming practically invisible when the pressure surpassed 5.5 GPa. The peak position shift and absence of the radial breathing mode was attributed to an irreversible structural transition in nanotubes that occurs at high pressure and affects their sub-band energy gaps.

The authors found a remarkable on/off ratio of approximately four orders of magnitude at extremely low bias. This was criticised by the scientific community, as ratios in this range for such modest biases have only previously been reported for carbon nanotube transistors and never for graphene nanoribbon devices (Tab.~\ref{tab:gnr_fet}). In addition to this criticism, the authors said that they did not observe any radial-breathing-like mode. Previous research on seven- and nine-atom-wide armchair GNRs generated by on-surface synthesis indeed revealed a low-frequency radial-breathing-like mode in the Raman spectra using 532 and 785 nm excitation lasers, respectively.~\cite{talirz2017surface}.

The device they fabricated was a standard back-gated silicon dioxide type of the device which showed p-type on-state conductivity of about:
%
\begin{equation}
    \sigma = \frac{G_\textrm{sd}L_\textrm{ch}}{w_\textrm{gnr}} = 7.42~\text{mS}
\end{equation}
%
for a channel of width of ($w_\textrm{gnr}\simeq \SI{2.8}{\nano\metre}$). They further test the relationship between the carrier mobilities for increasingly wider ribbon widths up to 10 nm. The experiments reported hole mobilities\footnote[2]{Calculated according to standard FET theory $\mu = \frac{g_\textrm{m}L_\textrm{ch}}{C_\textrm{G}V_\textrm{SD}}$~\cite{sze2021physics}} of about $\mu\simeq$ \SI{2,443}{\centi\metre\squared\per\volt\per\second} for the \SI{2.8}{\nano\metre} wide and $\simeq$ \SI{3.776}{\centi\metre\squared\per\volt\per\second} for the \SI{10}{\nano\metre} wide, which are currently among the highest values observed, despite possible uncontrolled scattering events of the carriers with the non-spherical bulb-like ends cross at the two sides.

\co{Bottom-up GNRFET}

Another issue in creating reproducible devices is transferring ribbon onto a substrate to measure its electrical characteristics. Because stamping methods that rely on mechanical contact of the ribbon growth and transfer substrate frequently result in poor-performing or irreproducible \glspl{GNRFET}~\cite{cai2010atomically}, Michael Crommie's group developed a robust transfer employing a stack of PMMA/\gls{GNR}/Au/Mica~\cite{Bennett2013}. Poly-methyl methacrylate (PMMA) was first spun-cast onto \glspl{GNR} and baked to generate a PMMA/GNR/Au/Mica stack, which was then floated on concentrated HF (40\%), causing the mica substrate to delaminate from the Au growth layer. The leftover PMMA/GNR/Au film stack was then rinsed twice in water before being put to Au etchant. Finally, the PMMA/GNR film was washed and drawn onto a silicon substrate before being baked to eliminate any remaining water. Finally, acetone was used to remove PMMA, leaving GNRs on an insulating surface.

At $V_\textrm{SD}=\SI{1}{\volt}$, the resultant transistor displayed gate-modulated conductance with on-currents ranging from tens of pA to a few nA. The authors reported that the electrical transport was mostly dominated by a significant Schottky barrier $\phi_\textrm{B}\simeq\SI{1.25}{\electronvolt}$, which was caused by adopting a much narrower ribbon with a wider bandgap. They also reported that, despite the fact that the contact between the ribbons and Pd must have been only a few to perhaps ten nanometers long, they were able to measure $I_\textrm{on}/I_\textrm{off}\simeq 10^3$ at $V_\textrm{SD} = \SI{1}{V}$, even if experiment on nanotube transistors have demonstrated resistance increases as contact length is reduced past the electron mean free path\footnote[4]{In graphene and \glspl{CNT} this value is often referenced at $\ell\simeq\SI{200}{\metre}$~\cite{xia2011origins, franklin2010length}. See also Sec.~\ref{intro:sec:cnt}}. The off-state leakage was attributed to the holes tunneling through the drain barrier, and therefore being largely temperature independent but strongly sensitive on the bias, as larger ones will significantly shrink the width of the Schottky barrier.

The authors also proposed a certain number of measures to mitigate the device shortcomings. Reducing the contact work function ($\phi^\textrm{Pd}\simeq$ \SIrange{5.2}{5.6}{\electronvolt}) would decrease the barrier height of the source conduction band and increase the barrier height of the drain valence band, resulting in enhanced on- and off-state performance. As effective mass and Schottky barriers are projected to be lower for smaller bandgaps, the usage of wider GNRs with bandgaps $E_g \simeq \SI{1.4}{\electronvolt}$ could also result in a further enhancement of band alignment. In conclusion, the researchers hypothesised that longer GNRs could also lower contact resistance by increasing $L_\textrm{c}$~\cite{wang2017precision}.

Using high-$\kappa$ dielectrics is another viable method for enhancing the properties of transistors. The usage of such dielectrics is necessitated by the fact that, when scaling down the geometrical dimensions of a transistor, the oxide thickness cannot be scaled in the same manner as the other geometrical parameters, as this would result in leakage currents through the oxide. This phenomenon could also result in a field that exceeds the breakdown field of silicon dioxide. By raising the permittivity of the dielectric, one can afford a thicker dielectric while still achieving the same capacitance as with a thinner silicon dioxide dielectric.

Furthermore, \citeauthor{llinas2017short} investigated the output characteristics of \glspl{GNRFET} 9- and 13-atoms wide fabricated using two distinct gate dielectrics~\cite{llinas2017short}. They compared the output response of a typical \SI{50}{\nano\metre}-thick back-gated silicon dioxide substrate to that of a high-$\kappa$ dielectric local gate (\ce{HfO2}, \gls{EOT}$ \simeq $\SI{1.5}{\nano\metre}). For the latter device at room temperature, current densities $I_\textrm{on}>\SI{1}{\micro\ampere}$ measured at $V_\textrm{SD}=$\SI{-1}{\volt} with the narrower ribbon were amongst the highest ever reported, combined with an excellent $I_\textrm{on}/I_\textrm{off}\simeq 10^5$.

Through a temperature-dependent experiment, however, the authors noted that Schottky barriers generated at the contacts again dominated transport. While the high-$\kappa$ dielectric lowers the carrier tunneling, the Au/Pd--\gls{GNR} junction leads to the formation of Schottky junctions partly due to the mismatch between the work functions of the metal and the ribbon. In order to lessen the mismatch and prevent the Schottky-dominated transport regime, adopting graphene as a contacting material could be one feasible approach~\cite{braun2021optimized, el2020controlled}.
