\chapter{\label{ch:introduction}Introduction}

\minitoc

\co{what this chapter is about}
In this chapter, I will explain the rationale and context for this thesis: may graphene and, more broadly, graphene-derived structures serve as a replacement or supplement for the ubiquitous Silicon in integrated circuits? To this goal, I will present a quick overview of the key all-carbon alternatives studied since the late 1990s. The chapter is organised such that it begins with a concise overview of solid-state crystal characteristics, followed by a discussion of how these qualities lead to unique dispersion relations in graphene, \glspl{CNT}, and \glspl{GNR}. Second, a selection of the most significant experimental solutions developed to date will be critically analysed.

\section{Motivation}\label{intro:sec:motivation}

\co{There is less Silicon around and it is incrementally difficult to maintain costs associated with its production}
Silicon has been the undisputed leader in the fabrication of \glspl{IC} for almost thirty years. The widespread shortage of semiconductors that began in late 2020, however, has prompted both major chipmakers and the scientific community to think more deeply and actively about which non-silicon technologies and materials are valid candidates for replacing or, more realistically, complementing silicon.
Except for market leaders such as Intel, Samsung, and TSMC, long-term manufacturing costs remain largely expensive. Bloomberg reports that the fundamental costs of constructing a semiconductor manufacturing are approximately \$15 billion, with profit requirements of roughly \$3 billion to maintain market competition and avoid obsolescence~\cite{king2021}. These findings are also backed by a study conducted by the Boston Consulting Group last year, which ranked investments in research and development (22\% of annual semiconductor sales) and capital spending (26\%) as the highest in all industries~\cite{veras2021}.

\co{The success of CMOS is in the density scaling}
The first point-contact transistor prototype developed at the beginning of the 1940s by Bardeen, Brattain, and William Shockley at Bell Labs and the creation of \gls{CMOS} techology by Atalla and Kahng ten years later marked the beginning of integrated components~\cite{shockley1984path}.
Possibly, the density scaling capabilities of this technology has been one of the primary factors driving development. In this scenario, Moore's law is considered not just as a dependable method for estimating the future number of transistors per integrated circuit, but also as a driver of innovation and competitiveness.

\co{One of the problem of the advancement of modern IC is the scaling limitations of CMOS}
The search for new materials for integrated electronics is not just related to the race to increase the number of transistors per chip. Figure~\ref{fig:intro:moore_law} displays several trends in microprocessor figures of merit over the past five decades. The clock frequency is the number of clock cycles (number of electrical pulses) that a CPU can execute every second and is measured in Hz. During these electrical pulses, the CPU can conduct fundamental operations such as memory access, data writing, and instruction retrieval. The clock speed (or rate) is architecture-dependent, hence it must be compared within the same architecture (x86-32/64 architecture is the benchmark architecture).

\co{Another problem with CMOS technology is the power dissipated with the clock speed}
One of the most interesting positive correlation that it is possible to appreciate from the plot is, arguably, between the clock speed quantity with the power dissipated by the processor, typically static power, which is the power dissipated when a transistor is not switching. \Gls{CMOS} technology is well-known for low static power values for standard supply voltages \qtyrange[range-units = bracket]{3}{5}{\volt}. As the gate oxide thickness decreseas, however, the probability of tunnelling for the charge carriers increases, resulting larger leakage currents and higher disspation powers~\cite{taur2021fundamentals}.

\section{Graphene}\label{intro:sec:graphene}

\co{crystal structure of graphene}
Graphene has a hexagonal structure where the carbon atoms are hybridised $sp^2$, as detailed by the Pauling theory on chemical bonds. Based on the experimental evidence collected in the last 20 years, the model
that seems to best describe the proprierties of graphene is the band-structure Wallace model, which dates back in 1947, and describes graphene as a semimetal with a linear dispersion of the electronic excitations.
In the International Table of Crystallography, graphene is included in the symmorphic space group \#191, with symmetry $D_{6h}$ ($P6/mmm$).
The triangular lattice is obtained by considering two atoms per unit cell having the respective lattice vectors:

The carbon atoms in graphene form a two-dimensional hexagonal crystal with two atoms per unit cell, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:graphene_lattice}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_lattice_vectors}
    \vect{a}{_1} = \frac{a}{2}(3, \sqrt{3}),~
    \vect{a}{_2} = \frac{a}{2}(3, -\sqrt{3}),
\end{equation}

with $a\simeq1.42$ \si{\angstrom} be the carbon-carbon bond length.
Each atom belonging to one sublattice, called sublattice A, has three first neighbours belonging to the other sublattice, called sublattice B. With respect to one atom chosen from sublattice A as origin,
the position of these first three neighbours in real space are given by:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_first_neighbours}
    \vect{\delta}{_1} = \frac{a}{2}(1, \sqrt{3}),~
    \vect{\delta}{_2} = \frac{a}{2}(1, -\sqrt{3}),~
    \vect{\delta}{_3} = -a(1,0),
\end{equation}

While the reciprocal-lattice vectors are given may be written as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_reciprocal_vectors}
    \vect{b}{_1} = \frac{2\pi}{3a}(1, \sqrt{3}),~
    \vect{b}{_2} = \frac{2\pi}{3a}(1, -\sqrt{3}),
\end{equation}

In order to define the vertices of the Brillouin zone one has to apply the Bragg condition:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Bragg}
    2\vect{k}\cdot\vect{G} = 0
\end{equation}

where:

\begin{equation}
    \vect{G} = n\vect{b}_1 + m\vect{b}_2,
\end{equation}

The first amongst these are:

\begin{align*}
    n & = \pm 1, & m & =0,     & \vect{G} & =\pm\vect{b}_1,              \\
    n & = 0,     & m & =\pm 1, & \vect{G} & =\pm\vect{b}_2,              \\
    n & = \pm 1, & m & =\pm 1, & \vect{G} & =\pm(\vect{b}_1+\vect{b}_2),
\end{align*}

and all these vectors have the same modulus $\abs{\vect{G}} = \frac{4\pi}{3a}$. By applying Bragg's condition in the first of the three cases we get:

\begin{equation}
    \sqrt{3}k_{x} + k_{y} = \mp~\frac{4\pi}{3a},
\end{equation}

which gets satisfied by the points:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:K_point_components}
    K \equiv\mp~\frac{2\pi}{3a}\qty(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, 1)
\end{equation}

For the second case one gets:

\begin{equation}
    \mp(\sqrt{3}k_{x} - k_{y}) = \frac{4\pi}{3a},
\end{equation}

which gives the points:

\begin{equation}
    K' \equiv\pm~\frac{2\pi}{3a}\qty(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, -1)
\end{equation}

The third and last case gives the point:

\begin{equation}
    M \equiv\pm\qty(0, \pm~\frac{2\pi}{3a})
\end{equation}

\co{Electronic Band Structure}
Within the second quantization formalism ($\hbar = 1$ units), the tight-binding hamiltonian may be written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_hamiltonian}
    \mathcal{H}_\textrm{TB} = -t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,~\sigma}\qty(a^\dagger_{\sigma,i}b_{\sigma,j} + \textrm{H.c.}) - t' \sum_{\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle,\sigma}\qty(a^\dagger_{\sigma,i}a_{\sigma,j}+b^\dagger_{\sigma,i}b_{\sigma,j}+ \textrm{H.c.}),
\end{equation}
The notation $\langle ij\rangle$ means the sum runs over pairs of electrons which are nearest neighbours; similarly, the notation $\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle$ specify a summation over the next nearest neighbours.

The two operators $a^\dagger_{i,\sigma}$ ($a_{i,\sigma}$) and $b^\dagger_{i,\sigma}$ ($b_{i,\sigma}$) creates (annihilates) an electron with spin $\sigma$ ($\sigma=~\uparrow,\downarrow$) for atoms on sublattice A and B, respectively.
The value of $t$ which reproduces best experimental results is $t\simeq2.8$ \si{\electronvolt}, and quantifies the nearest-neighbour hopping energy; similarly, $t'$ denotes the next nearest-neighbour hopping energy.

By diagonalising this hamiltonian it is possible to work out the energy dispersion of graphene, which reads:

\begin{equation}\label{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands}
    E_{\pm}(\vect{k}) = \epsilon_p - t'f(\vect{k}) \pm t\sqrt{3 + f(\vect{k})},
\end{equation}

with $f(\vect{k})$ defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands_fk}
    f(\vect{k})=2\cos\qty(\sqrt{3}k_ya) + 4\cos\qty(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}k_ya)\cos\qty(\frac{3}{2}k_xa).
\end{equation}

Eq.~\ref{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands} identifies an upper ($\pi^*$) band associated with the plus sign and a lower ($\pi$) band described by the minus sign, and these are symmetric with respect to the single-particle zero energy.
The experimental estimate reported for next nearest-neighbour hopping energy is $t'\simeq 0.1$ \si{\electronvolt} thus the asymmetry introduced is small.

If the system is not doped, there will be one electron per carbon atom, resulting in two electrons per cell: the lower band will be full, while the top band will be empty. Therefore, the system results to be semi-metallic.
When examining the trend between the points \vect{K} and \vect{K'}, the peculiarities of graphene's dispersion become clear. By taking the positive components for the \vect{K} point as define in Eq.~\ref{eq:K_point_components} and expanding the
Eq.~\ref{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands} as $\vect{k} = \vect{K} + \vect{q}$, with $\abs{(\vect{q})}\ll\abs{(\vect{K})}$, one gets:
\begin{equation}
    E_{\pm}(\vect{q}) = \epsilon_p \pm \frac{3at}{2}\abs{\vect{q}}
\end{equation}
This remarkable result tells us that the dispersion is linear in \vect{q}. For intrinsic graphene, at $T= \SI{0}{\kelvin}$  $E_F = \epsilon_p$. The Fermi velocity is defined by the usual relation $v_F = \frac{1}{\hbar}\qty(\dv{E}{\vect{k}})$,
and in graphene $v_F\simeq1\times 10^6~\textrm{ms}^{-1}$ $= 0.66~\textrm{eV}\times\textrm{nm}\times\hbar^{-1}$.

An important consequence of this result is that whilst for a two-dimensional electron gas the \gls{DOS} is constant, in graphene close to the Fermi level the \gls{DOS} reads:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:grafene:density_of_states}
    \rho(E)=\frac{2A_\textrm{cell}}{\pi}\frac{\abs{E}}{v_F^2}
\end{equation}

If graphene is charge-neutral, there are two Van Hove singularities in the DOS at $\rho(\omega_\textrm{VH}) = \pm 1$ \si{\electronvolt}~\cite{bena2005quasiparticle}. This behavior stands in stark contrast to the \gls{DOS} of
carbon nanotubes and graphene nanoribbons as they exhibit $1/\sqrt{E}$ singularities due to their 1D structure, which imposes a quantization perpendicular to the longitudinal direction of the ribbon or tube.

Using the Einstein-Smoluchowski relation $D = k_BT/\eta = \mu k_BT$, which links an average friction coefficient $\eta$ experienced when a flow of semiclassical charge carriers diffuse in a medium with coefficient $D$, the semiclassical conductivity may be computed from the \gls{DOS} as:

\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:semiclassical_conductivity}
    \sigma_{\textrm{sc}}=e^{2} \rho(E) v(E) \ell_{\mathrm{el}}
\end{equation}
where $v(E)$ is the carrier velocity, $\ell_{\mathrm{el}}$ the elastic mean free path, and $e$ the elementary charge. From Eq.~\ref{ch1:eq:semiclassical_conductivity} and the charge density $n(E)$, one can calculate the charge mobility, which, at zero temperature, reads:
\begin{equation}
    \mu(E)=\frac{\sigma_\mathrm{sc}}{e n}
\end{equation}
Close to the Dirac points, the experimental conductivities lie within $\simeq 2-5~e^{2}/ h$ range, although several experiments have demonstrated a variation of more than an order of magnitude due to doping from the electrical contancts and/or the substrate~\cite{jiang2007quantum, ozyilmaz2007electronic}.


\subsection{Graphene Transistors}

For transistor applications, a material needs a highly conductive on-state and an off-state where the source-drain current $I_\textrm{SD}$ does not flow. For RFs applications, on the other hand, the full speed operation is obtained by operating in the (drain) current saturation regime.
Both applications requires a sizeable bandgap.
Furthermore, the IC scaling trend is often juxtaposed to chip-level issues such as power densities (currently on the order of 1000 W/cm$^2$), heat generation, and chip temperatures reaching levels that will prevent reliable operations.

In CMOS logic, the charge carriers are accellerated by the electric field $\CMcal{E}_\textrm{sd}$ between source and drain, thus undergoing five possible sources of scattering: electron--electron scattering, electron--phonon (lattice vibrations),
material interfaces, imperfections or impurity atoms. The first two are more fundamentals, while the last three are material-related. The most dominant mechanism through which charge carriers lose net energy is by
scattering with phonons, consequently heating up the lattice via Joule heating. For RF applications, on the other hand, heat is produced by the operational frequency at which the carriers operate.
Thus, two further requirements are needed: on one hand a good thermal conductivity, but also small heat transfer with the substrate. Clearly trade-offs are here necessary.

The 2020 International Roadmap for Devices and Systems - which has replaced since 2017 the International Roadmap for Semiconductors - dedicates ample attention to graphene in its report \textit{Beyond CMOS},
in which technologies and semiconductor materials to complement and replace Si are considered. The race to scale the channel in a \gls{FET} still motivates a significant effort on introducing high-mobility channel materials,
since the carrier mobility is an appropriate parameter to address carrier transport even in nanometer \glspl{FET}~\cite{schwierz2010nanometer}.
In fact, most of the early excitements in using graphene as channel material in a standard FET technology were sparked by considering the outstanding mobility values reported since its discovery.

At 290 K, the intrinsic mobility $\mu_e^{\textrm{in}}$ of graphene is exceptionally high. The first data were the one obtained in 2004 on large-area graphene, showing an electron mobility $\mu_e^\textrm{in}\simeq10,000$ cm$^{2}$V$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$~\cite{novoselov2004electric}.
Further studies have demonstrated mobilities spanning between $\mu^{\text{in}}\sim 3000 - 12000$ cm$^2$ V$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ for carrier concentrations $n\simeq1 - 0.1 \times 10^{12}$ cm$^{-2}$, with
low-field field-effect mobility\footnote[2]{This differs from the Drude ($\mu_{\textrm{Drude}}=\sigma/ne$) and Hall ($\mu_\textrm{Hall}=-\sigma_e R_H$) mobilities.} ($\mu = (1/C_{\textrm{g}})(\partial \sigma/\partial V_{\textrm{gs}})$)
that is constant over a quite wide range of $n$ (yielding in this case $\mu_h\simeq1900$ cm$^2$ V$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$ and $\mu_e\simeq1800$ cm$^2$ V$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$ for the low $n$ region)~\cite{Meric2008}.

The mobility is limited by different factors such as scattering by charged impurities or defects and electron-phonon scattering. In very clean and suspended samples (where the underling SiO$_2$ substrate has been etched away) the mean free path $l$ increases by almost two order of magnitude, becoming comparabe to the device size, an indication that the ballistic transport regime is reached %\cite{Bolotin2008, du2008andrei}.

Later in 2008~\cite{bolotin2008ultrahigh} and 2013~\cite{wang2013one}, higher mobility values were reported: $\simeq120,000$ cm$^{2}$V$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ at 240 K and $\simeq1,000,000$ cm$^{2}$V$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ at 4 K. The reason for these higher values was attributed to the use of suspended graphene rather
onto substrate, where surface effects inevitably affect the carrier dynamics. At the same time, results ($\simeq120,000$ cm$^{2}$V$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$) obtained at room temperature with graphene onto a single-layer of hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN)~\cite{dean2010boron} and sandwiched between two hBN layer~\cite{wang2013one} have suggested
that this flat and inert materials can be act as a excellent passivation film for graphene devices.

One of the figure of merit often taken into account for addressing logic applications for a materials is the ratio between the on-state current and the off-state current. The so called on-off ratio $I_\text{on}/I_\text{off}$ has to be in the range 10${^4}$--10${^7}$. While the $I_\text{on}$ is not directly related to the bandgap $E_g$, it is possible to link the value of $I_\text{off}$ with the $E_g$.

In an metal--semiconductor junction (M/S), the current is built by the majority of the carriers, these being electrons or holes for $n$-type or $p$-type semiconductors. Most of the graphene--semiconductor junctions formed during experiments have typical dopant concentration $N\leq10^{17}$ cm$^{-3}$. The $I-V$ characteristics of M/S junctions with such dopant concentrations are well reproduced by the thermoionic emission theory. If we then exclude other possible conduction mechanisms (standard and thermoionic field emission, generation/recombination, diffusion of electron, injection of holes and leakage current), the $I_\text{off}$ becomes proportional to the barrier the electrons have to overcome from the semiconductor to the metal, namely

\begin{equation}
    I_\textrm{off}\propto\exp{(e\beta\phi_B)}.
\end{equation}

where $e$ is the electron charge, $\phi_B$ is the Schottky barrier height, and $\beta=k_BT$.
If we assume that the work function of graphene and the metal are the same, then $\phi_B = E_g/2$, where $E_g$ is the bandgap energy originating from the difference of the work function of grahene and the metal; thus, the on--off ratio can be estimated from the useful proportionality~\cite{kim2011role}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bandgap_current_ratio}
    \frac{I_\textrm{on}}{I_\textrm{off}}\propto\exp{\frac{E_g}{2k_BT}},
\end{equation}

where the thermal energy $k_BT$ and the bandgap are both expressed in meV.
While for high frequency applications the on--off ratio is of minor importance, several references in literature report suitable values for CMOS bandgap should fall in a range between 300 and 500 meV~\cite{schwierz2010graphene, reddy2011graphene}.

Hysteresis between the forward and reverse sweeps have been reported depending on the sweeping rate and range~\cite{wang2010hysteresis}, and attributed mainly to charge trapping in silanol groups (Si--OH), mediated and assisted by surface-bound \ce{H2O} molecules~\cite{joshi2010intrinsic}.
Despite the usefulness for its proof-of-concept purposes, back-gate devices suffered from parasitic capacitances, making impossible further integration with other components.
Alternatives geometries have since then been explored, in order to increase the carriers mobility, to decrease contact resistances and parasitic capacitances, and to avoid Schottky barriers formation between the graphene and the semiconductor.
Top-gate devices have been the first to chronologically appear in the literature.
Back then in 2007, Lemme and co workeres used a stack of \ce{SiO2} (20 nm), Ti (10 nm), and Au (100 nm) as top gate for modulating the current inside a large-area graphene channel transistor obtained by the exfoliation method~\cite{lemme2007graphene}.
Whilst the gate characteristics exhibit a similar trend of the characteristics (the top gate reduces the back-gate current modulation, lowering the drain current by one order of magnitude) and the hysteresis, weak current saturation at high drain-source bias have been reported~\cite{moon2009epitaxial}.
This effect seem due to a partial charge pinning at the contacts at high back-gate voltages~\cite{di2011charge} and carrier drift velocity eventually saturating due to optical-phonon scattering~\cite{meric2008current}. Top-gated graphene \glspl{MOSFET} with CVD and epitaxial graphene have been also made~\cite{kedzierski2008epitaxial, lin2010100}; \ce{SiO2}, \ce{Al2O3}, and \ce{HfO2} have been  used as top-gate dielectric.

However, despite the improvements obtained with a top gate, most graphene \gls{MOSFET} with large-area graphene channels show on-off switching ratios of less than 10 at room temperature~\cite{schwierz2010graphene}, much too little for complex logic circuits.

In 2012, Mehr \textit{et al}.\ proposed a vertical arrangement of emitter, base, and collector similar to the original vacuum triode, where graphene plays the base role and electrons travel vertically rather than transversely.
When the device is in the ON state, the electrons tunnel with a Fowler-Nordheim tunnelling mechanism:

\begin{equation}
    I\propto\qty(\frac{V_\textrm{SD}}{d})^2~\exp{-\frac{\alpha~d}{V_{sd}}},
\end{equation}

through the emitter--base layer (of thickness $d$); ON/OFF ratios of the order of $10^4$ have been demonstrated using graphene between two h-BN layers~\cite{britnell2012field} and using \ce{WeS2}--graphene~\cite{georgiou2013vertical} heterostructures.

Solution methods have also been explored, in which graphene is dropcasted on top of a semiconducting wafer, and then spin-coated~\cite{liu2010simple}.
The main difficulties arise when attempting to quantify the structure and the quality of the deposited graphene, and not many methods can guarantee a good dispersion in organic solvents~\cite{sun2011graphene}.
Simpler ways to obtain graphene oxide in solution have been reported~\cite{van2016effect}, but once deposited, graphene oxide needs to be reduced for obtaining pure graphene -- low mobility values (up to, at best, 1000 \si{\square\centi\metre\per\volt\per\second}) caused by defects during the fabrication steps have discouraged further investigations along this direction.

Yet in spite of the very different dc behavior compared to the most used semiconductors in digital electroncis, graphene FETs have reported good performance in terms of high-frequency applications. This comparison is made by measuring each system’s frequency response, its output signal strength when it is given a particular input signal: features such as ballistic transport, linear current-voltage characteristics, high transconductance, and very high ($> 10^8$ \si{\ampere\per\square\centi\metre}) sustainable currents are all ideal features for analog/RF applications~\cite{meric2008rf, meric2008current}.

The primary means of estimating frequency response is through the evaluation of two transistor charactersitic frequencies, namely cutoff $f_\textrm{T}$ and maximum of oscillations $f_\textrm{max}$. The former indicates how quickly a signal can travel from the gate to the drain of the device, and it is usually determined when the small-signal current gain drops to unity, while the latter is measured when the power gain decreseas to unity~\cite{schwierz2010graphene}.
The frequency response depends inversely on gate length and proportionally to the transconductance, which is loosely proportional to mobility. Thus, reducing the gate length and using higher mobility materials are valid approaches to increase the device operational speed~\cite{schwierz2013graphene}. In this context, many research groups have successfully demonstrated RF capabilities with gapless graphene channels \glspl{MOSFET}.

The first device with cutoff frequency $f_\textrm{T}$ in the GHz range was reported in late 2008, and was farbicated by exfoliated graphene deposited on top of high-resistivity \ce{Si} substrate, with 30-nm-thick top-gate \ce{HfO2} dielectric and 500 nm of gate length~\cite{meric2008rf}.
Despite a measured $f_\textrm{T}\simeq 14.7$ GHz, it was argued that large contact resistances and gate length were the main responsible of poor perfomance $f_\textrm{max} < 1$ GHz.

The following year, Pd/Au contacts and 12-nm-thick \ce{Al2O3} were employed in a 350-nm-gate~\cite{lin2009development}. While the cutoff frequency improved up to $f_\textrm{T}\simeq 50$ GHz, little gains in terms of $f_\textrm{max}$ were obtained of only 2 GHz. In general, limited performance in $f_\textrm{max}$
represents a major issue as for most RF applications $f_\textrm{T}$ is less important than $f_\textrm{max}$.

It was only after two years that an increase of one order of magnitude both in the cutoff and the maximum frequency of oscillation was demosntrated~\cite{lin2010100}. The device was realised with epitaxially-grown on silicon carbide (\ce{SiC}) graphene \gls{MOSFET} having 240 nm of gate length, with Ti/Pd/Au for contacts and 10-nm-thick \ce{HfO2} of insulating layer on top gates. Highest frequency as well as $f_\textrm{T}\simeq 100$ GHz and $f_\textrm{max}\simeq 14$ GHz were measured,
exceeding any device then present. The authors also argued that since $f_\textrm{max}$ is strongly correlated to device layout and parasitic resistances originating from the metallic contacts, viable ways to improve this parameter would be a partial passivation of the metal contacts and a multi-finger gate layout

These perfomance were soon followed later that year by a 144-nm-length gate device with $f_T \simeq 300$ GHz using mechanically exfoliated graphene and \ce{Co2Si}–\ce{Al2O3} nanowire as self-aligned multi top-gates~\cite{liao2010high}. This result was also corrobated by comparing it with the projected
value using the relation that links dc transconductance and gate capacitance with the cutoff frequency when total input parasitic capacitance is negligible, namely $f_\textrm{T} = g_\textrm{m}/(2\pi C_\textrm{g})$~\cite{sze2021physics}. The authors did not report the power $G^{1/2}_\textrm{max}$ but only the current $\abs{h_{21}}$ gain, thus hampering an evaluation of $f_\textrm{max}$,
but in 2012 a similar proposal was advanced by Cheng \textit{et al.}, where Au/Ti/\ce{Al2O3} gate stacks were employed in self-aligned transistors~\cite{cheng2012high}. Despite the improvements reported ($f_\textrm{T}\simeq 427$ GHz for a channel length of 67 nm) respect to CVD graphene transistors with comparable channel length, $f_\textrm{max}$ decresead down to 8 GHz when the length was shrunk down to 46 nm, showing the
key role played by gate and parasitic resistances at high frequency.

To further improve the power gain, works on the saturation behavior of short channel graphene transistor were also conducted by IBM in 2012, by using CVD graphene grown on a Cu foil and then transferred on diamond-like and SiC substrates~\cite{wu2012state}. These are appealing because high-quality
wafers are available, thus making a scale up of graphene arrays relatively straightforward. Since the work function of graphene is $\phi^\textrm{g} = 4.5$ eV and $\phi^\textrm{4H-\ce{SiC}} = 3.7$ eV, this means that $\Delta\phi^\textrm{g--4H-SiC} = 0.8$ eV, thus $n$-doping graphene~\cite{sonde2009electrical}.
Very cleverly, the authors then used plasma-enhanced CVD silicon nitride as an intrisic p-type gate dielectric, counterbalancing the n-doped graphene channels. Encouraing performance at high speed were not only measured in good cutoff frequencies ($f_\textrm{T}\simeq 350$ GHz), but also in a factor of 2 improvement
of $f_\textrm{max}$ ($\simeq 40$ GHz).
To the best of my knowledge, the current graphene transistor power gain record of about 70 GHz was obtained in 2013 by using (000-1) carbon-terminated surface of SiC instead of the (0001) Si one, indicating the contact resistance $R_\textrm{c}$ between Pd/Au contacts and graphene as key responsible for the high $f_\textrm{max}$.

While these results have aligned graphene MOSFET with the main solid-state competitors -- such as GaAS pHEMT, InP HEMT and Si MOSFET -- in terms of $f_\textrm{T}$, the perfomance is terms of $f_\textrm{max}$ are off by about one~\cite{tan1990InGaAs, post2006RF} or two orders of magnitude~\cite{lai2007sub}. In literature, one of the often cited fundamental reason
for this behaviour is the poor drain saturation current in graphene, which ultimately depends on the missing bandgap of the channel. Graphene-derived materials such as nanoribbons and carbon nanotubes MOSFET could then, given their intrisic semiconducting nature, represent a possible solution to this problem.

\section{Carbon-Nanotube}

\subsection{Crystal and Band Structure}

This crystalline form of carbon can be considered as a rolled up sheet of graphene and comes in two major flavours: \gls{SWNT} and \gls{MWNT}.
\Glspl{CNT} display an electronic bandgap which depends on the chirality and the diameter of the nanotube~\cite{dresselhaus2000carbon}.
Having defined two unit vectors in the real space, $\vect{a_1}$ and $\vect{a_2}$, a \gls{CNT} can be described by the linear combination of these with integers coefficients ($m$, $n$). Based on the values of $m$ and $n$, \glspl{CNT} can be metallic , quasi-metallic ($m - n$ is a multiple of 3, with $m\cdot n \neq 0$ and $m \neq n$),
and semiconducting for the majority of the remaining cases -- exceptions due to the curvature (spin--orbit couplings) in small-diameter tubes are also known~\cite{laird2015quantum}.

\begin{figure}[hbtp]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter_introduction/figures/cnt_structure.pdf}
        \caption{
            \textbf{(a)} Cartoon structure of a chiral (8, 2), an armchair (5, 5), and zigzag (10, 0) nanotube.
            \textbf{(b)} Graphene Brillouin zone and allowed k-vectors for the (8, 2) and (10, 0) CNTs. A metallic tube include one of the \vect{K} points among the set of allowed k-vectors, as shown in \textbf{(b)}. It is then possible to linearise the energy in the momentum space for low-excitations (close to the Fermi energy). Conversely, the \vect{K} point is not an allowed vector for semiconducting nanotube, and this leads to an energy gap. Inspired by~\cite{torres2014introduction}.}
        \label{fig:intro:cnt_structure}
    \end{center}

Thus, a quantization of the electronic states in the circumferential direction is resulted when the nanotube diameter $d_\textrm{CNT}$ is between 0.8 and 2.5 nm. Each of these substates is called subband
and are determined by the periodic boundary condition. Having defined a circumferential wave vector $\vect{k_c}$ such that $\vect{k_c}\cdot\vect{c} = k_c\abs{c} = k_c\pi d_\textrm{CNT} = 2\pi\nu$, for $\nu$ integer, it is possible from this relation to draw parallel lines in the first Brillouin zone of graphene.
If the subband intersect one of the $\vect{K}$ points, than the resulting nanotube will have no energy gap and thus exhibit metallic properties (this condition is realised by having $m = n$ for the coefficients of the chiral vector).
If none of the subbands pass through $\vect{K}$ then the resulting \gls{CNT} will be semiconducting, having a $E_g$ proportional to the smallest distance between a $\vect{K}$ point and the subband lines:

\begin{equation}
    E_g = 2\qty(\pdv{E}{k})\frac{k_c}{3}=\frac{2}{3}\hbar v_F\frac{2\pi}{\abs{c}} = \frac{4}{3}\hbar v_F\frac{1}{d_\textrm{CNT}}\simeq\frac{0.7}{d_\textrm{CNT}}
\end{equation}

where $v_F = 8\times10^5$ \si{\meter\per\second} is the Fermi velocity, $E_g$ is expressed in eV, and $d_\textrm{CNT}$ is expressed in nm.

\subsection{Carbon Nanotube Field-Effect Transistors}

\subsubsection{General Operation}

The modulation of a \gls{CNT} channel by a gate-induced field was realised in the 1998 by the group of Cees Dekkar \cite{tans1998room}. These early \glspl{FET} were fabricated
by using doped silicon substrate as a gate to modulate the energy bands, separated from the nanotube by thermally-grown \ce{SiO2} oxide acting as dielectric.
For contacting the nanotube, metal conctats were employed.

Since then, a great variaty of \gls{CNTFET} structures and geometries have been demonstrated, and can be essentially classified in devices with or without a spacer.
where the spacer length $L_\textrm{spr}$ is the distance between the gated channel and the source/drain metal conctacts.
Given that the nanotubes are intrinsically semiconducting, in spacer-less devices charge carriers are not induced in the channel by the gate-field but are injected from the contacts: the role of the gate is to
lower the thermal barrier in the channel, thus allowing a tunnelling through the \gls{SB} at the source. In this scenario, a critical role for the \gls{CNTFET} performance is played by the metal work function $\phi^\textrm{M}$.

\glspl{CNTFET} with spacers, on the other hand, are less limited by the injection at the junction source--\gls{CNT}, as they are regulated by the modulating effect played by in the channel as in conventional. Since the carrier barrier
injection at the drain is large, device with spacers have been proved to be more resistant to ambipolar conduction. In Table \ref{tab:CNFET} an overview of gated-spacer (top and bottom) devices is reported.

\subsubsection{Contacts}

One of the principal issues when study the interface metal--{CNT} is the role played by carbon and metal atoms at the interface.
Solid-state semiconductors have dagling bonds at the surface, leading to Fermi level pinning, meaning that the variation of the band bending is independent of the work function of the metal, even when this is large.
Since the ideal structure of \glspl{CNT} has no open bonds, no pinning of the metal Fermi level to mid-gap states occurs, thus legitimating the study of different work functions for maximising performance and tuning polarity.
Many metals have been studied for contacting \glspl{CNT}. Interestingly, it has been demonstrated that Pd ($\phi^\textrm{Pd}\simeq$ \SIrange{5.2}{5.6}{\electronvolt})
builds an almost ideal $p$-channel contact to the valence band of \glspl{CNT}. Despite having a larger workfunction ($\phi^\textrm{Pt}\simeq5.65$ eV) than Pd, Pt provides very poor contacts due to the physical wetting of the metal on the nanotube.

\subsubsection{$n$-Type CNTFETs}
In a $n$-type \glspl{CNTFET} the injection of electrons is favoured over the holes. Given that $\phi^\textrm{CNT}\simeq 4.7$ eV and with an average $E_g\simeq 0.6$ eV, it is possible to configure n-type channel with nanotube
by either doping the spacer region or by using a metal with lower work function. One of the main drawbakcs of doping is the challenge to keep it under control: instability in air, low consistency, variability to temperature variations,
are amongst the most frequent sceanrios. The other issue in working with low work function metals as contacts is the high tendency to oxidize. A simple and effective workaround to this is to use high work function metals to cap the contact metals,
passivating the contact surface tendency to destabilise in air. One example of high performance n-type device was obtained by using Y contacts, achieving an \gls{SS} of 73 mV/dec, mobility of 5100 \si{\cm\square\per\V\per\second}, and \gls{DIBL} of 105 \si{\mV\per\V}~\cite{ding2009contacted}.

\subsubsection{Scaling}
While Si technology was for long time questioned whether it could work below 10-nm of channel length, recent impressive techological developements from the industry have proved not only its feasibility
but also a widestream commercial application (currently modern laptop employ the 5-nm technology). Nevertheless, the interest in Si scalability has been always a crucial starting point for exploring different technologies and materials
capable of overcoming short-channel effects (threshold voltage roll-off, off-state leakage current, channel length modulation are just a few of the problematics).

In carbon nanotubes transistors a crucial and important--but somehow overlooked--is the difference between two critical lengths for scaling: the contact ($L_\textrm{c}$) and the channel ($L_\textrm{ch}$) lengths.
Aggressive scaling for the channel length generally leads to lose control of the band bending through the gate: intuitively, by shrinking the channel length the electric field generated by the source contact will be felt more and more
by the charge carriers. The argument here is that carbon nanotubes offer, however, a good enough platform for such scaling given by the ultrathin hollow structure (recall an effective wall thickness about $d_\textrm{CNT}\simeq0.68$ \si{\angstrom}). On the other hand, this could be a double edge-sword.
The effetive carrier mass in nanotubes is $m^*_\textrm{CNT} = (0.228 \pm 0.129)m_0$~\cite{marulanda2008carrier}, thus potentially leading to tunnelling current from source to drain: this effect can be appreciate by an increase in the \gls{SS} and the OFF current.

One of the first few systematic study on channel length scaling effects was conducted by Franklin \textit{et al.} in 2010 through a series
of measurements on nanotubes with relatively small diameters (1.1 nm $<$ d$_\textrm{CNT}$ $<$ 1.3 nm) by using local bottom gates covered
with 2 nm \gls{EOT} of \ce{HfO2}~\cite{franklin2010length}. A consistent trend was observed when the channel length was scaled down more than two orders of magnitude (from 3 \si{\micro\metre} to 15 nm),
both in the values of $V_\textrm{th}$ and $SS$ ($-0.5\pm0.2$ V and 90 mV/dec, respectively).
The authors reported also encouraging figures on the transconductance $g_m$.

In a standard \gls{MOSFET}, the transconductance is the change in the drain
current divided by the amount varied in the gate/source voltage $V_\textrm{GS}$ when the bias voltage ($V_\textrm{SD}$) is kept constant.

Typical values of $g_m$ for a small-signal field effect transistor are between 1 and 30 \si{\milli\siemens\per\micro\metre}.
In a n-channel (nMOS) FET with threshold voltage $V_\textrm{th}$, the drain current at a certain $V_\textrm{GS}$ in saturation mode (so when $V_\textrm{th} > V_\textrm{GS} - V_\textrm{SD}$) is given by:

\begin{equation}
    I_\textrm{SD} = \frac{1}{2}\mu_n C_\textrm{ox}\frac{W}{L}\qty(V_\textrm{GS} - V_\textrm{th})^2, \label{eq:transistor-drain-current}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_\textrm{n}$ is the electron mobility, $C_\textrm{ox}$ is the gate oxide capacitance, $W$ and $L$ are the gate width and length, respectively.
It is possible to work out the value of $g_m$ by using the Shichman–Hodges model and assuming that the threshold voltage $V_\textrm{th}$ is a device constant, such that $V_\textrm{ov} = V_\textrm{GS} - V_\textrm{th}$:

\begin{equation}
    g_\textrm{m} = \pdv{I_\text{SD}}{V_\text{GS}} =\frac{2~I_\textrm{SD}}{V_\textrm{ov}},\label{eq:schiman-hodges-model}
\end{equation}

Here $V_\textrm{ov}$ is the so called \textit{overdrive voltage} and has the physical meaning of being the voltage above the threshold voltage of the \gls{MOSFET}. It is useful in the designing phase of a transistor
and elegantly links the drain current with a voltage: in fact, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:transistor-drain-current} and identify $k_n = \mu_n C_\textrm{ox}\frac{W}{L}$ one gets:

\begin{equation}
    I_\textrm{SD} = \frac{1}{2}k_n V_\textrm{ov}^2 \implies V_\textrm{ov} = \sqrt{\frac{2I_\textrm{SD}}{k_n}}
\end{equation}

The usefulness for this equation is that the relation expressed in terms of overdrive voltage rather thant threshold voltage holds both in the $n$ and in the $p$ case.
Some reference numbers: $V_\textrm{ov}$ is tipically set at about 70–200 mV for the 65 nm technology node
(with $I_\textrm{SD} = 1.13$ \si{\milli\ampere\per\micro\metre} of width) with value of $g_\textrm{m}\simeq 11-32$ \si{\milli\siemens\per\micro\metre}.
In their work, Franklin \textit{et al.} achieved transconductance peak up to 40
\si{\micro\siemens\per\micro\metre} for a \gls{CNTFET} with 15 nm of channel length,
corresponding to values of $g_\textrm{m}$ between approximately 91 -- 110 \si{\micro\siemens\per\micro\metre},
thus meeting the requirements for being compatible with the Si technology.


The first \gls{CNTFET} in which $L_\textrm{ch}$ was scaled below 10 nm came two years later, again by Franklin \textit{et al.}~\cite{Franklin2012}.
By using a CNT with d$_\textrm{CNT}$ $\simeq$ 1.3 nm $\pm$ 0.2 nm and $E_g$ $\simeq$ 0.62 eV $\pm$ 0.1 eV contacted through local bottom gates, they manage to scale down the $L_\text{ch} = 9$ nm
The \gls{EOT} was reduced to $\simeq$ 0.65 nm to achieve a better electrostatic coupling with the nanotube.

The authors reported an increase on the minimum current as $L_\textrm{ch}$ decreseas from 320 nm to 9 nm, attributed to a back-injection of the
carriers into the conduction band from the drain, while the on-current approached the ballistic value for $L_\textrm{ch}\leq 41$ nm~\cite{franklin2010length}.
Impressive switching behaviour, with \gls{SS} about 94 mV/dec, was measured, thus far outperforming any silicon-based of that time.
based on previous results~\cite{franklin2010length}, the authors claimed the possibility of lowering the \gls{SS} and the off-current at minimal cost to I$\textrm{on}$ by reducing the diameter of the nanotubes.

\subsubsection{Contact Length Scaling}
MOSFET devices, when shrunk down to the nanoscale, need to consider not only $L_\textrm{ch}$ but also the lenght of the nanotube with the source and drain, $L_\textrm{c}$. The first study to address the impact of contact scaling
in \glspl{CNTFET} revealed the $L_\textrm{c}$ to be inversely dependent on the channel length. Initial theoretical attempts to describe this trend using dipole theories were started following the results obtained by the group of Hongjie Dai, where they
showed it is possible to recover a perfect ohmic contact -- thus accessing the ballistic transport regime -- even with a semiconducting nanotube, which were at tha time notorious of suffering several Schottky barriers effects (tunnelling and thermoionic emission).

Further investigations using more sophisticated techiniques such as \gls{DFT} coupled with the \gls{NEGF} were employed to consider not only different contact geometries, but mostly to clarify the role in the chemical bonds formed between the metal to carbon structures,
or in some combination of metal-/carbon-dependent interfacial charging properties. In particular, Cuniberti and coworkes addressed the chemisorption problem of carbon nanotubes with different electrodic surfaces. Chemisorption consists of the creation of a chemical bond between the atom deposited on a substrate (adatome) and its surface.
The bonding leads to changes in the local density of states that produce specific alteration of the surface properties. They considered metal surfaces (such as Ni and Ti) inducing a chemical reaction with the carbon structure (chemisorption), elements such as Pd, Pt, Cr and Sc which interact weakly with the nanotube band structure leaving it unchanged, and surfaces which interact strongly through metallization and/or hybridisation of the $\pi$ orbitals of the nanotube (Cu, Au, and Rh).



Low-ohmic contact scaling trends were found when using weakly and intermediate interacting metallic surfaces, while they envisioned optimal contact length scaling with metals such as Cu and Rh.
While Rh contacts would be unaffordable at large scale due to manufacturing costs, it is well know that Cu present ill-defined native oxide issues when used under ambient conditions, causing scattering at their surfaces and grain boundaries, thus hampering good electrical contacts with external probes.
Recently, it has been advanced graphene as interlayer between the metal contact and the nanotube: the idea would be to have a work function closer to the nanotube one in order to avoid the level pinning effects due to gap states and interface contaminations.


\section{Graphene Nanoribbons}

Among carbon alternatives, \glspl{GNR} have certainly gained attentions for logic applications. These require two well-defined voltage states (low/high voltage for off/on state) and fast switching behaviour between them. The confinement of large-area graphene carries into 1D channels opens an electronic bandgap, which depends on the channel width and length~\cite{Han2007}. Early theoretical calculations have in fact shown that when the ribbon width approached the widhts around 2-3 nm, the resulting bandgap becomes closer to those of Ge or InN. By further reducing down the width (1-2), this should technically approach bangaps closer to those of Si or GaAs~\cite{barone2006electronic}. As can be appreciated in Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:gnr-bandgap_schwierz} ribbons having widths below 5 nm are considered suitable for bangaps in the 0.3 -- 0.5 eV, which are required for \gls{CMOS} techonlogy~\cite{geng2017graphene}.

    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{chapter_introduction/figures/bandgap_GNRwidth.pdf}

After the discovery of graphene, \glspl{GNR} were produced using \textit{top-down} approaches, typically by patterning ribbons through lithography means with \gls{EBL}~\cite{RN345, Han2007}, by longitudinal unzipping of single-walled nanotubes~\cite{kosynkin2009longitudinal}, or by cutting graphene sheets with metallic nanoparticles~\cite{RN346}. These fabrication methods, however, were often yielding large widths and uncontrolled structural defects~\cite{han2010electron, Evaldsson2008}.

Two pioneering works reported independently by Hongjie Dai~\cite{Li2008} and Klaus M{\"u}llen~\cite{cai2010atomically} sparked the deployment of chemical strategies by using precisely- engineered molecular precursors. The advantages of these \textit{bottom-up} protocols are severals. Firstly, it has been possible to synthesise several-hundreds-nm-long ribbons with widths below 5 nm~\cite{narita2015bottom}: long ribbons are desirable when interconnection applications are considered. Secondly, important nano-and- optoelectronics properties such as electronic bandgap, charge-carrier mobility, and optical absorption have become tunable by either varying \glspl{GNR} chemical structures or by introducing opportune decorations along the ribbon edges~\cite{Liu2015, hu2018bandgap}. The wide variaty of edge structures synthesised through bottom-up approaches demonstrates the success and the versatility of this fabrication route (Fig.\ref{fig:intro:gnr-canvas}).

\subsection{Crystal and Band Structure}
Armchair (AGNRs) and Zigzag (ZGNRs) ribbons were the first group of \glspl{GNR} to be studied. The difference between AGNRs and ZGNRs rests in a different edge connettivity and has profound consequences on the respective bandstructure. In general, the energy spectrum of a \gls{GNR} with length $L$ and width $w_\text{GNR}$ may be written as:

\begin{equation}
    E_{n,m} = \pm\hbar v_F^*~\sqrt{\qty(\frac{n\pi}{L})^2 + \qty(\frac{m\pi}{w_\text{gnr}})^2}
    \label{eq:gnr_energy}
\end{equation}

where $v_F^*$ is the group velocity for energy band indexed by the quantum numbers pair $n,m$~\cite{baringhaus2014exceptional}.
Through \gls{TB} calculations, the group of Steven Louie was one of the first in classifying the electronic properties of AGNRs based on the number of carbon dimer lines across the ribbon width $N$~\cite{son2006energy}. When $N$ is a multiple of 3 of an integer $p$, the resulting ribbons have wide bandgaps which scales inversely with the ribbon width\footnote[2]{The $E_g \propto 1/w_\text{gnr}$ relation holds when $15~\text{nm} \le w_\text{gnr} \le 90~\text{nm}$~\cite{yang2007quasiparticle}} (Tab.~\ref{tab:agnr-tb}). The same happens when $N=3p+1$. In contrast, the ribbon family characterised by $N=3p+2$ were predicted to be metallic~\cite{nakada1996edge}.

The electronic properties of Z\glspl{GNR} behave qualitatively and quantitatively differently from A\glspl{GNR}. The unit cell of a ZGNR is specified by providing only 2 atoms per unit cell, in contrast to the four one has to provide for A\glspl{GNR}. This gives origin to a $2_1$ screw axis along the width extension direction. The major consequence of having glide planes and screw axes in nonsymmorphic space groups\footnote[3]{Nonsymmorphic groups are space groups which are not uniquely defined by the repetition of a point-group motif by the lattice translations. An example of nonsymmorphic groups are the structure for Te and $\alpha$-quartz.} for the wave vector group is to cause energy bands to stick together along some of the high symmetry points and axes in the Brillouin zone~\cite{dresselhaus2007group}. This \textit{flat band} region is located at wave vectors $2\pi/3 \le k \le \pi$, and the electronic states in this region have been well understood by looking at the charge distribution maps and assigned to localized states near the zigzag edge~\cite{wakabayashi2010electronic}. In one-dimensional systems these electronic states are called \textit{edge states}, display an exponential decay of the \gls{DOS} with distance from the ribbon centre $\rho(\epsilon)\sim\epsilon^{-\vect{r}}$, and their weights to the total wavefunction become negligible for widths approaching the micrometer range~\cite{kobayashi2006edge,son2006energy}.

Things become even more interesting upon including the spin \gls{DOF} into the band description. Indeed, it is possible to bandgap when the wave vector matches the ratio between $\pi$ and the unit cell distance $d_z$, i.e. $k = \pi/d_z$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:zgnr_bandgap_edge_state_delocalisation}\textbf{c}). Through spin-polarised ab-initio calculations one might be able to assign a magnetic ordering to the ground state. Interestingly, it has been shown that two magnetic orderings are allowed: a ferromagnetic configuration ($\uparrow\uparrow$, FM) for those spins which aligned along one edge, and an antiferromagnetic state ($\downarrow\downarrow$, AFM) for those that belong to two distinct edges~\cite{son2006half}. The property of undergoing the phase transition from an AFM configuration (semiconducting) to an FM state (metallic) has been called \textit{half metallicity} and it has been demonstrated that should be theoretically accessible if one induces a magnetic field along the direction paralell to the ribbon extension plane.

\subsection{Edge States}
The elucidation of localised state exotic properties came a few years later and had to include chiral \gls{DOS} for the electronic states at the edges~\cite{yazyev2011theory}. Chirality is a fascinating property arising when the time-reversal symmetry of the charge carriers is broken, and one can distinguish between carrier wavefunctions evolving in time with a clockwise phase from those moving with a counterclockwise phase. Through the usage of a mean-field Hubbard hamiltonian, and assuming the on-site Coulomb repulsion $U$ is comparable to the hopping integral $t$ between two adjacent sites ($U/t \sim 1$), a \gls{GNR} may be modelled with the following hamiltonian:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:gnr_chiral_hubbard_hamiltonian}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{H}_{\textrm{MF}} = &- t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,~\sigma}\qty(a^\dagger_{\sigma,i}a_{\sigma,j} + \textrm{H.c.}) \\
        &+ U\sum_{i}\qty(n_{i\uparrow}\langle n_{i\downarrow}\rangle + \langle n_{i\uparrow}\rangle n_{i\downarrow} - \langle n_{i\uparrow}\rangle \langle n_{i\downarrow}\rangle),
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

where the second summation is carried out over the spin-polarised densities $n_{i\sigma} = a^\dagger_{i\sigma}a_{i\sigma}$. This hamiltonian respects the electron-hole symmetry, meaning that the \gls{DOS} is symmetric respect to the Fermi level.

The wavefunction solutions of the corresponding eigenvalue problem described correctly the experimental evidence, predicting two pairs of peaks split in the \gls{DOS} and which were linked to two different magnetic orderings. One peak split connected with the AFM correlation between opposite edges, while the second and larger split due to the FM ordering along one single edge (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:zgnr_bandgap_edge_state_delocalisation}).

A further experimental evidence of magnetic orderings localised along the edges was discovered by employing \gls{ESR} techniques, as demonstrated by the work carried out by Slota \textit{et al.} using microwaves (94 GHz) ~\cite{slota2018magnetic}. The peculiarity of their ribbon structure was to carry stable nitroxyl radicals decorations along ribbon's edges acting as spin injectors and enabling the possibility to disentangle the magnetic edge fingerprint from the signal of the radical. Additionally, it was demonstrated that the edge state has superior coherence time compared to most $\pi$-extended organic systems even at room temperature and  in a non-diluted phase (powder), which makes \glspl{GNR} very promising for spintronics applications. Desirable would be therefore to study the tunability of magnetic properties with electrical control single zigzag graphene nanoribbons, for example through the usage of molecular gaps. This has indeed been one of the initial aims for this research thesis.

Although a few impressive transistor key figures, such as $I_\textrm{on}\simeq$ 2000 \si{\micro\ampere\per\micro\metre}, $I_\textrm{on}/I_\textrm{off}\simeq 10^6$, $SS = 210$ mV/dec, and $g_\textrm{m} = 900$ \si{\micro\siemens\per\micro\metre} were reported, degraded carrier mobility  within the $\simeq$ 50--200 cm$^{2}$V$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ range were also seen for narrower \glspl{GNR}.  The reason for this unexpected result is double-fold. Firstly, it is well estabilished the tendecy in  solid-state (conventional) semiconductors of decreasing mobilities for increasing bandgaps. Secondly, it
has been attributed that carrier transport in \glspl{GNR} is dominated by edge effects.

In order to quantify the contribution from the edges to the total carrier mean free path $\ell$, Wang \textit{et al.} prososed a phenomenological model where edges, defects, and phonons are considered independent~\cite{wang2008room}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\ell} = \frac{1}{\ell_\textrm{edge}} + \frac{1}{\ell_\textrm{phonon}} + \frac{1}{\ell_\textrm{defect}}
\end{equation}
Without loss in generality, the probability of a scattering event occurring should be inversely proportional to the free mean path:
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:mfp_edge}
    \ell_\textrm{edge} = \frac{k_\parallel w_\textrm{gnr}}{P k_\perp}
\end{equation}
Since for ribbons having widths below 20 nm - and exluding interaction effects - the energy gap should scale inversely with the ribbon width~\cite{stampfer2008tunable}:
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:bandgap_width}
    E_g\simeq\frac{\hbar v_F}{w_\textrm{gnr}}
\end{equation}
it is possible to work out a dependence of the edge mean free path with the total ribbon width by plugging Eq.~\ref{ch1:eq:bandgap_width} and the dispersion relation $E_k$ into Eq.~\ref{ch1:eq:mfp_edge}:
\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:mfp_edge_backscattering}
    \ell_\textrm{edge} = \frac{w}{P}\sqrt{\qty(1 + \frac{E_k}{E_g})^2 - 1}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\ref{ch1:eq:mfp_edge_backscattering} suggests that an increase of width and/or an improvement of the edge quality should lead to an increase of the mean free path. These considerations were confirmed experimentally, as higher mobilities were reported for sub-10 nm ribbons with increasing widths~\cite{wang2008room, geng2017graphene}.

\co{Energy Band-gap Engieering}
Theoretical predictions seemed to suggest a simple, almost straightforward, framework to undestand the energy gap in graphene nanoribbon.
In the first experimental evidence, the Kim's group~\cite{Han2007} showed that, however, this is not the case.
The \glspl{GNR} were fabricated by means lithographically, with lengths $L$ in the order of $\mu$m and sub-100-nm widths.
Narrow and large ribbons respectively display larger and narrower bangdap compared to the theoretical predictions, and showed no dependence on the crystallographic orientration of the edges. In several studies~\cite{areshkin2007ballistic, martin2009transport, chen2007graphene} published as an attempt to explain these discepancies, a few of them  highlighted the role played by rough edges resulting from litographic patterning in breaking the nanoribbons into a series of quantum dots. In particular, when charge localisations have comparable length to the ribbon widths, disorder effects start to play a role~\cite{martin2009transport}. Similarly, charge transfers between the dots could be partly modualted by the electron--electron interactions~\cite{ponomarenko2008chaotic}; thus, Coulomb blockade could be an explanation for the larger gaps in for smaller ribbons.

In an attempt to experimentally solve this dilemma, only three years later evidences showed the solution for this dilemma could be explained by a combination of both. Hopping charge transfer mechanism through localised states whose size is comparable to the ribbon width would result in a transport gap rather than a true energy gap. Within a single-particle model~\cite{han2010electron}, this could be linked to the transport gap via~\cite{castroneto2009}:
\begin{equation}
    E_g = \hbar v_F\sqrt{2\pi C_g \Delta V_\text{G}/\abs{e}}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta V_\text{G}$ is the region where the conductance is suppressed, and $C_g$ is the capacitive coupling of the ribbon to the gate.
\co{Explain now which are the way to estimate the capactive coupling with the gate}

A strong temperature dependence of the conductance was measured for all the ribbons which widths was below 100 nm, with the narrowest kind ($\simeq$25 nm) exhibiting stronger suppression of the conductance minimum $G_\textrm{min}$. For all the the three ribbons, the conductance $G$ scaled linearly with the width, $G = \sigma(W - W_0)/L)$, where $\sigma\simeq 1.7$ mS is the sheet conductivity and the $(W - W_0)$ is the active transport region.
Despite the graphene contact to the ribbon, the formation of Schottky barrier at the graphene--metal contact cannot be ruled out~\cite{DiBartolomeo2016}.

The physical origin for the gap observed in their studies was clarified only 2 year later~\cite{han2010electron}. At thecharge neutrality point, a length-independent transport gap forms whose size is inversely proportional to the width

While it has been advanced in the past the possibility to obtain graphene nanoribbons from nanotubes, it is relatively recent the proposal of using high-pressure conditions and thermal treatments to produce ultranarrow (sub-10-nm-wide) \glspl{GNR} from \glspl{CNT}, resulting in average lengths in the order of $\mu$m range~\cite{chen2021sub}.

The p-type on-state conductivity of the ribbon channel (w$_\textrm{gnr}\simeq 2.8$ nm) when placed in a MOSFET geometry is:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma = \frac{G_\textrm{sd}L_\textrm{ch}}{w_\textrm{gnr}} = 7.42~\text{mS}
\end{equation}
and $I_\textrm{on}/I_\textrm{off} \simeq 10^5$. Using Eq.~\ref{eq:bandgap_current_ratio}, the authors reports a $E_g \simeq 494$ meV. A further comparison was carried out by comparing the mobilities values obtained for while increasing up to 10 nm the GNRs width. Hole field-effect mobility was calculated using the classic MOSFET model through the relation:
\begin{equation}
    \mu = \frac{g_\textrm{m}L_\textrm{ch}}{C_\textrm{gs}V_\textrm{SD}}
\end{equation}
and estimating the gate capacitance with an electrostatic 3D model.

Interesting enough, the hole mobilities values (2,443 cm$^{2}$V$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ for the 2.8 nm wide and 3.776 cm$^{2}$V$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ for the 10 nm wide) are amongst the best values obtained so far. The role of strained bulbs and non-spherical cross section at the two edges is not clarified yet and thus could reproduce unpredictable effects due to uncontrolled scattering mechanism.


\co{Bottom-up GNRFET}

In order to measure bottom-up \gls{GNR} several strategies can be followed. One can first synthesise the ribbon onto a metallic film and transfer the resulting layer of \glspl{GNR} on top of the target substrate, upon dissolution of the metallic part in acidic solution, through mechanical contact. A more robust method developed by Crommie and co-workers instead used a stack of PMMA/\gls{GNR}/Au/Mica: thanks to the protective role played by the PMMA, the quality of the GNRs was preserved during the transfer. The resulting \glspl{GNRFET} reported $I_\textrm{on}=$ \SI{10}{\pico\ampere}--\SI{1}{\nano\ampere}, resulting in p-type doped in air when contacted with Pd metal contacts, and large hysteresis due to absorbed oxygen, water and PMMA residuals. The narrow ribbon width contributes to have a large bandgap ($E_g\simeq$ \SI{2.5}{\electronvolt}) and a transport dominated by the presence of \gls{SB} originating from the significant difference between the work function of Pd ($\phi^\textrm{Pd}\simeq$ \SIrange{5.2}{5.6}{\electronvolt}) and of \gls{GNR}.

Another possible solution to improve the transistor characteristics is to use high-k dielectrics. The reason to employ such dielectrics is because when scaling down the geometrical dimensions of the transistors the thickness of the oxide cannot be scaled as the other geometrical parameters as this would yield oxide thickness to think that would result in leakage currents through the oxide. This phenomena would potentially also lead of having a field beyond the breakdown field of SiO$_2$. Thus, by increasing the permittivity of the dielectric, in order to obtain the same capacitance that one would have obtained with a thinner silicon dioxide dielectrics one can afford a thicker dielectric.

Llinas \textit{et al.} measured the output charactersistics of bottom-up 9- and 13-\glspl{AGNR} field-effect transistors made by using two different gate dielectrics \cite{llinas2017short}. They compared the output response obtained when using a standard \SI{50}{\nano\metre}-thick SiO$_2$ (back-gate) and a high-k dielectric (HfO$_2$, \gls{EOT}$\simeq$\SI{1.5}{\nano\metre}, local gate). At room temperature, the latter device reported values $I_\textrm{on}>$ \SI{1}{\micro\ampere} for $V_\textrm{SD}=$\SI{-1}{\volt} when using the narrower ribbon; nine carbon dimer lines are equivalent to a width close to \SI{0.95}{nm}. The excellent gate control over the channel was further demonstrated by an excellent $I_\textrm{on}/I_\textrm{off}\simeq 10^5$ ratio.

One of the major hurdle of their device however is the metal contact choice. Even thought the tunneling is enhanced by adopting the high-k dielectric, the junction Au/Pd--GNR will always result in \gls{SB} due to an important difference between the two work functions. The adoption of graphene as the contact material might yield to a complete suppression of \gls{SB}.



\subsubsection{Tunnelling Field-Effect Transistor}
One of the main problems in scaling down the CMOS technology is heat dissipation. As the carriers are thermally injected, it is difficult to scale down $V_\textrm{SD}$ whilst increasing the switching (operational) speed and the keeping a low off-state power dissipation; thus, since $P_\textrm{off} = RI_\textrm{off}^2$, this boils down to hold a low off-state current. Furthermore, the mechanism of charge carrier transfer by thermal means places a limit to the subthreshold slope figure, which for a \gls{MOSFET} is limited to $2.3 k_BT/q = 60$ mv/dec at room temperature~\cite{sze2021physics}.

Interesting alternatives arise when instead of focusing on the maximum output on state current the on/off ratio is increased by lowering the off state current. If the source-carrier injection happens through an interband tunnelling mechanism instead of a thermal injection, the tunnelling transmission probability becomes temperature independent (Zener tunnelling).

A proposal from Hammam \textit{et al.} reports band-to-band tunneling (BTBT) in a sub-10 nm GNR controlled through a triple top gate transistor~\cite{hammam2018sub}. In their device, the outer gates are used to define the $p$ and $n$ doped regions while the middle gate modulates the channel current. Width and bandgap estimated with Eq.~\ref{ch1:eq:bandgap_width} of about 9.4 nm and 70 meV were reported.

\section{Perspective and Outlook}

Graphene nanoribbons shows physical and chemical properties interesting not only for a technological advancement in those fields where graphene and carbon nanotubes lack in performance, but especially in the rich physics they can unveil. At the current stange, bottom-up graphene nanoribbons have shown remarkable transport and exotic properties, but these have been exclusively studied on synthesis substrate. One of the next main challenge in the nanoribbon field is the attempt to investigate such properties also at the device level. At this stange, these evidences remains very scarces and unclear.

Three roles are envisioned where graphene nanoribbons could techonologically contribute. The first one is the application as sensors. The great deal of studies in different gas atmospheres have proved excellent selectivities and good flexibility, given the possibility to functionalise the edges with different chemical substituents. A second papable field could be the integration in low-power devices, where by reducing required supply voltages one can allow to have ribbons with lower bandgaps hence increased mobilities. A third and more enthusiastic application would see the use their topological properties to build dechorence-tolerant quantum hardware.
