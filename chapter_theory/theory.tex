\minitoc

The lateral confinement of the carriers makes \glspl{GNR} a quasi-1D system. The \textit{quasi} referes to the fact that the electron is confined across two directions.
This fact became true not just until recently with the advent of bottom-up techiniques, which are capable synthesising atomically-precise \gls{GNR} with width below 5 nm.

In general, quantum transport phenomena takes place on a mesoscopic scale, that is, when dealing with condensed phase systems, in particular (but not exclusively) those of micro-nano dimensions, for which behaviours not present in macroscopic bodies are observed. The different behaviour of mesoscopic phases compared to macroscopic ones liesin the wavelength nature of electrons.
When the size of the sample $L$ (which can be different for the three directions in space, $L_x, L_y, L_z$) becomes small compared with the characteristic lengths for the motion of electrons, then the conductivity depends on the size $L$.

There are three kinds of relevant characteristic lengths: the Fermi wavelength $\lambda_F = h / \sqrt{2m_eE_F} = 2\pi/k_F$, and the coherence length $L_\phi$, which is the spatial distance that an electron travels before the phase of its wave function changes of $2\pi$.

Additionally, two characteristic lengths associated with the mean distance traveled after elastic ($l_\textrm{el}$) and inelastic ($l_\textrm{in}$) collisions are also introduced; for inelastic collision, it is convenient to consider the mean distance before losing an amount of energy $k_BT$.

For a metal with carrier concentrations $n \sim 10^{23}$ cm$^{-3}$, $l = 1 - 10$ nm, $\lambda_F = 0.5$ nm, and $L_\phi = 0.5$ \si{\micro\metre}, where $l$ is the mean free path and includes both elastic and inelastic events.
In comparison, for carrier concentrations $n \sim 10^{11}$ cm$^{-2}$, graphene displays $l = 50$ nm$-3$\footnote[2]{For suspended graphene, $l (\textrm{4 K}) \simeq 100$ nm, while Mayorov et al. $l \simeq 3$ \si{\micro\metre} at 290 K for graphene sandwiched between two hBN crystals~\cite{mayorov2011micrometer}.}
\si{\micro\metre}, $\lambda_F = 2\sqrt{\pi/n}$ nm, and $L_\phi = 0.5$ \si{\micro\metre}~\cite{torres2013introduction}, while \gls{MWNT}-\glspl{CNT} $n \sim 10^{11}$ cm$^{-2}$, $l = 1$ \si{\micro\metre}, $\lambda_F = 0.74$ nm, and $L_\phi = 100$ nm~\cite{stojetz2005effect}.


\section{Charge Transport In Zero-Dimensional Objects}

\co{Transport Spectroscopy in Quantum Dot}

\Glspl{QD} defined through lithography can be seen as a metallic island filled with
electrons, coupled to one or multiple source, drain and gate electrodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:theory:set_schematic}).
While source and drain are coupled both capacitively thorugh the island thanks to tunnel junctions, the gate (or multiple
gates) is just capacitatively coupled.

\begin{figure}[hbtp]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter_theory/figures/set_schematic.png}
        \caption{Cartoon schematic for a single-electron transistor based on a quantum dot.}
        \label{fig:theory:set_schematic}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}

One way to describe this system is through the constant interaction model, which allows to derive important electrical transport
information within the \textit{orthodox} Coulomb framework. The assumptions made within this model are the following: all Coulomb interactions,
both between electrons on the dot and between the dot and the leads, are described by a single constant capacitance $C$ given by the total sum of the
single individual capacitance between the dot and the terminals:

\begin{equation}\label{ch:theory:eq:capacitance_interaction_model}
    C_\textrm{tot} = \sum_{i=1}^\textrm{terminals} C_i,
\end{equation}

where $i$ runs over all the terminals coupled to the central island. The second important assumption is that the total hamiltonian can be modelled as a sum of single-particle hamiltonians;
thus, the total energy can be expressed as a sum of single-particle energies $E_i$ corresponding to individual, non-interacting energy levels.

Under these assumptions, the total dot energy given for a quantum dot in a three-terminal device with $N$ electrons becomes:

\begin{equation}\label{ch:theory:eq:total_dot_energy}
    U(N) = \sum_{i}^NE_i + \frac{[e(N-N_0) + C_\mathrm{S}V_\mathrm{S} + C_\mathrm{D}V_\mathrm{D} + C_\mathrm{G}V_\mathrm{G}]^{2}}{2C_\textrm{tot}},
\end{equation}

where $eN_0$ is the charge present on the dot at zero bias and zero gate voltage due to positive background charges in the substrate.
Central quantity to evaluate whether the current can flow through the dot is its the chemical potential, which is the minimum energy required to add an extra electron
to the dot, and can be expressed as:

\begin{align}
    \mu_\textrm{dot}(N) & = E_\textrm{dot}(N) - E_\mathrm{dot}(N-1), \notag                                                                                                                                       \\
                        & = \left(N-N_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\right) E_{\mathrm{C}}-\frac{E_{\mathrm{C}}}{e}\left(C_{\mathrm{S}} V_{\mathrm{S}}+C_{\mathrm{D}} V_{\mathrm{D}}+C_{\mathrm{G}} V_{\mathrm{G}}\right)+E_{N},
\end{align}

where $E_\mathrm{C} = e^2/C_\textrm{tot}$ is the charging energy.


\section{Molecule--Lead Couplings}

\subsection{Weak Limit}

The weak coupling regime is defined in the limit where $\Gamma\ll\E_C, E_\textrm{add}(N), k_BT$, and is dominated by Coulomb blockade. The effect shows most clearly in a charge stability diagram, which is obtained by measuring the gate and bias dependence of the current and then plotting it in a two-dimensional map . The Coulomb blockade manifests as diamond shaped regions of very low current, so
called Coulomb diamonds, in which transport is blocked. In these regions the energy cost to add an electron onto the \gls{QD} exceeds the available energy and therefore no current can flow through the \gls{QD} .

\begin{figure}[hbtp]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter_theory/figures/coulomb_blockade.pdf}
        \caption{
            \textbf{(a)} Cartoon structure of a chiral (8, 2), an armchair (5, 5), and zigzag (10, 0) nanotube.
            \textbf{(b)} Graphene Brillouin zone and allowed k-vectors for the (8, 2) and (10, 0) CNTs. A metallic tube include one of the \vect{K} points among the set of allowed k-vectors, as shown in \textbf{(b)}. It is then possible to linearise the energy in the momentum space for low-excitations (close to the Fermi energy). Conversely, the \vect{K} point is not an allowed vector for semiconducting nanotube, and this leads to an energy gap. Inspired by~\cite{torres2014introduction}.}
        \label{fig:theory:cb}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}

The blockade areas are separated by regions of high current, the Sequential Electron Tunnelling (SET) zones, where current flow through the \gls{QD} is allowed via sequential electron tunnelling. Each Coulomb diamond corresponds to a certain number of electrons N occupying the \gls{QD} where the number of electrons changes sequentially between neighbouring diamonds. When looking closely at the SET regions, often lines running parallel to the edges
of the Coulomb diamonds can be observed. These originate from orbital excited states, spin states or vibrational states, which will be discussed later. These states are able to contribute to the current flow through the \gls{QD} if their energy levels are reached by applying a higher bias voltage . Such excited or vibrational states can yield information about the \gls{QD}’s energy spectrum and are especially helpful when attempting to identify specific molecules as each molecule has a distinctive vibrational fingerprint.

Furthermore, the height of the Coulomb diamond in the stability diagram is equal to the addition energy of the \gls{QD} or molecule .
While it is possible to readily read out the addition energy and the energy of excited states, it is very challenging or almost impossible to infer the charge state of the \gls{QD}, that is the number of electrons on the dot, from the stability diagram. Since the \gls{QD}
is part of the device and therefore a larger system, the neutral charge state can be deceiving when only considering the dot itself. Assuming the \gls{QD} is at no applied bias and no gate voltage and therefore seemingly in the neutral charge state, it might still be partially charged due to interactions with the junction or background charges in the substrate (cf. Eq.~\ref{ch:theory:eq:total_dot_energy}).

One can correlate the stability diagram or a gate sweep measuring the conductance or current through the quantum dot to the different level alignments of the contacts and the dot orbitals . When the electrochemical potential of the source and drain is aligned with an energy level of the dot, transport through it is allowed and results in a current peak in the gate trace. The corresponding transport mechanism is called resonant tunnelling. While the levels are aligned the number of electrons on the dot fluctuates between $N$ or $N+1$ as electrons hop on and off the dot. In between the peaks the electrochemical potentials are not aligned, so there is no resonant tunnelling due to Coulomb blockade, and the number of electrons on the dot is fixed. Additionally, various capacitances between the \gls{QD} and the contacts as well as
the gate lever arm (or gate coupling parameter) can be extracted from the slopes of the diamonds as the negative slope and positive slope are equal to $-\abs{e}C_\mathrm{G}/(C - C_\mathrm{S})$ and $\abs{e}C_\mathrm{G}/C_\mathrm{S}$, respectively. Since the gate lever arm is given by $\alpha = C_\mathrm{G}/C$ one can thereby quantify the strength of the gate coupling.

\subsection{Intermediate Limit}
Lastly, the intermediate coupling regime has attributes of both limits, it still exhibits
a noticeable Coulomb blockade, but also shows transport within the zero current region.
The two most noticeable new transport channels available in this regime are elastic and inelastic cotunnelling: these higher-order transport
mechanisms involve the simultaneous tunnelling of two electrons. Initially, the \gls{QD} is in a state with an unoccupied excited state still below the electrochemical potential of the leads, in contrast to resonant tunnelling where the excited state is between the source and drain electrochemical potential. When the electron tunnels out of the \gls{QD} onto the drain, the \gls{QD} is left in a virtual state and there are two possibilities for the orbit to be refilled. First, the new electron simultaneously tunnelling in from the source lead occupies the ground state again, therefore leaving the \gls{QD} in exactly the same configuration as before. This is called elastic cotunnelling. If the new electron, however, tunnels into the excited state, the \gls{QD} has changed its energy and the process is called inelastic cotunnelling. The electron can subsequently relax into the ground state, rendering the \gls{QD} again in the initial configuration. Importantly, for inelastic cotunnelling to take place, a bias larger than the excitation energy of the dot has to be applied and since the \gls{QD} excitation is created at the expense of the bias applied, electron energy is conserved. Overall, both elastic and inelastic cotunnelling lead to conduction in the blocked Coulomb diamond region.

\subsection{Strong Limit}
In the strong coupling limit $\Gamma\gg\E_C, E_\textrm{add}(N), k_BT$ In this regime quantum dot and lead's states hybridise, the Coulomb blockade is smeared out by quantum fluctuations of the electric charge, and the main transport mechanism is now played by elastic coherent tunnelling. In this limit, the conductance can be approximated as $G\simeq\frac{2e^2}{h}\mathcal{T}(E)$, where $\mathcal{T}(E)$ is the transmission probability for all the conduction channels thorugh the quantum dot evaluated at the Fermi energy. In the case of a single, well-isolated energy level, it is possible to recover the Breit--Wigner model for a single resonance.


\subsection{Landauer Theory}\label{ch:theory:sec:landauer_theory}
In order to make predictions about the electronic and thermoelectric properties of QDs, a good model for electronic transport in mesoscopic to molecular-scale devices is necessary. In the limit of predominantly SET and assuming coherent transport, extending the Landauer formalism (originally a model for electrical conduction) towards thermoelectric effects has proven successful and is widely used. The Landauer formalism is a concept formulated in 1947 by Rolf Landauer that models conduction as transmission. The basic assumption is that two electron reservoirs are connected by a scatterer (QD island for the transport measurements presented here) through which electron transport is possible. The transport is then determined by the probability of electrons moving from one reservoir to the other
through the scatterer. This probability is always between 0 and 1, which means there
is an upper limit on conductance.

Employing the Landauer approach, the current through such a structure can be
written as:
\begin{equation}
    I = \frac{2e}{h} \int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathcal{T}(\epsilon)[f_\textrm{L}(E) - f_\textrm{R}(E)]\text{d}\epsilon,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}$ is the transmission probability of an electron with single-particle energy $\epsilon$ to move across the source and drain leads. The Fermi-Dirac statistics
$f_\textrm{FD}(\epsilon, T) = \frac{1}{1+e^{\epsilon_i - \mu/k_BT}} $ details the average number of fermions (electrons in our case) in a single-particle state $i$ with energy $\epsilon_i$ in thermodynamic equilibrium at temperature $T$. Therefore, it is used to quantify how many electrons from the source leads have a certain energy to be able to cross the molecular junction: this event, as everything in quantum mechanics, is detailed by the amplitude of the transmission probability $\mathcal{T}(\epsilon)$.

One can calculate the number of electrons in a crystal by multiplying the density of
states $N(\epsilon)$ with the FD distribution and summing up this product for all energies:
\begin{equation}
    n = \int\limits_0^{\infty}f_\textrm{FD}(\epsilon)N(\epsilon)\text{d}\epsilon,
\end{equation}

The density of states $N(\epsilon)$ denotes the number of electron states per unit of energy and volume and differs for different materials.
In such a system the chemical potential is defined as the amount of energy needed
to add an additional electron to the system and naturally this quantity has an
inherent temperature dependence as the energy of the system changes with $T$. An
approximation for the chemical potential as a function of temperature and energy
has been developed using the Sommerfeld expansion (cf. Appendix~\ref{app:1-sommerfeld}): since we are interested in low-temperature properties, we will consider only terms up to the second order of Eq.~\ref{app-1:eq:coefficients_sommerfeld}:
\begin{equation}
    \mu \simeq \epsilon_{F}-\left.\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\left(k_{B} T\right)^{2} \frac{1}{N\left(\epsilon_{F}\right)} \frac{\partial N(\epsilon)}{\partial \epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=\epsilon_{F}}
\end{equation}
As is evident from this equation, the chemical potential decreases as the temperature
rises, resulting in a potential gradient whenever a temperature gradient is applied.
Any difference in $T$ will therefore lead to a flow of electrons as the cost of adding
another electron to the hot side would be lower than adding it to the cold side. This
results in the build-up of a thermoelectric voltage in an open circuit.

By multiplying this probability with the elementary charge $e$ gives the current for one energy level with a factor of two added due to spin degeneracy. After adding all of these possible transport channels together, which is done by taking the integral over all energies, and accounting for
dimension normalization via the Planck constant $h$ it is possible to calculate the total current $I$ across the junction.

The electrical conductance is then retrieved by taking the derivative of the current:

\begin{align}
    G & = -\frac{2e}{h} \int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathcal{T}(\epsilon)\pdv{f(T, \epsilon)}{\epsilon}\text{d}\epsilon,\nonumber \\
      & = - G_0 \int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathcal{T}(\epsilon)\pdv{f(T, \epsilon)}{\epsilon}\text{d}\epsilon,
\end{align}

As we will see in more detail later (cf. Sec.~\ref{sc:NEGF}), it is possible to compute the set of real eigenvalues for the transmission thorugh different approaches with varying degrees of complexity. One of them, which it has been used in cf. Ch.~\ref{ch:fet}, calcultes the product between the tunnel couplings and Green function terms. Other and more sophisticated approaches can be found in literature~\cite{cuevas2010molecular}.


\section{Thermoelectric Phenomena in Zero-Dimensional Structures}

In this section we will present electrical phenomena arising when a thermal gradient is present between two junctions, even without applying any external bias.

Macroscopically, the thermoelectric power that arises when two different materials are welded together in two points is proportional to the temperature drop resulting when one of the junction is heated up at temperature $T_1 > T_2$; i.e.:
\begin{equation}\label{ch2:eq:seeback_coeff}
    \Delta V = S\Delta T
\end{equation}
where $\Delta V $ denotes the electrical potential drop and $S$ is the proportionality factor called Seebeck coefficient. For reasons that will become clear later, it is prefferred to define the as the negative voltage difference $\Delta V$ induced per unit temperature change $\Delta T$, namely $S = - \lim_{\Delta T\to 0} \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta T}$.

There are two approaches used to model the electronic distribution in a solid when external paramaters such as heat and/or non-uniform fields are applied. The Boltzmann approach infers the dynamics of a diluted gas of semi-classical particles: in the semiclassical description of transport, the electron ensamble is described by a probability distribution function $f(\vec{r}, \vec{k}, t)$, which details the number of electrons that a certain time $t$ are within a volume $\text{d}\vec{r}$ with wave vector within the interval $\text{d}\vec{k}$. In the case where quantum effects play a role in transport, that is if the potential of the structure varies sharply with respect to the particle average thermal wavelength, the Boltzmann approach is no longer valid.

The second applicable framework is the Landauer theory (cf. Sec.~\ref{ch:theory:sec:landauer_theory}) which is used for low temperature thermoelectric calculations in mesoscopic structures \cite{cuevas2010molecular}. The Landauer formalism has the advantage of assuming a constant mean-free path, rather than a constant relaxation time of the electrons as the Boltzmann approach does, which
is more useful in experiments. In addition, the Landauer approach is valid in the ballistic and quasi-ballistic regime.

It is possible to formulate a similar expression for the flow of heat $Q$ from one reservoir to the other by multiplying the particle transmission with the particle
energy ($\epsilon - \mu$):
\begin{equation}
    I = \frac{2}{h} \int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(\epsilon - \mu)\mathcal{T}(\epsilon)f(\epsilon, T))\text{d}\epsilon.
\end{equation}
If the electrical or thermal gradient are treated within a linear approximation, such that $\Delta T = T_0 - T$ and $e\Delta V = \mu_0 - \mu$, the Fermi-Dirac distrubtion can be rewritten in a Taylor series and stopping and the first order of the expansion:
\begin{equation}
    f(T, \epsilon)=f^{0}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial \epsilon}\left[e \Delta V-\frac{\epsilon-\mu}{k_{B} T} \Delta T\right]
\end{equation}
Thus, the total amount of current flowing within the device is given from the thermoelectric current due to the thermal gradient $\Delta T$ applied to the junction and the electric current due to bias applied between source and drain:
\begin{align}
    I & = G\Delta V - L\Delta T,\label{eq:current_voltage_temperature} \\
    Q & = LT\Delta V - K\Delta T.
\end{align}
Here $G$, $L$, and $K$ denotes the electric, the thermoelectric and the thermoconductance, resepctively. The relation expressed in Eq.~\ref{eq:current_voltage_temperature} is particularly convenient, as through the Wiedemann-Franz law it is possible to retrieve the Seebeck coefficient. For an open circuit we will then have:
\begin{align}
    I                         & = 0 = G\Delta V - L\Delta T,\nonumber \\
    \sigma\Delta V            & = \alpha\Delta T,\nonumber            \\
    \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta T} & = \frac{\alpha}{\sigma}
\end{align}
where we called with $\sigma$ the conductivity. This relation allows us to identify the Seebeck coefficient as:
\begin{equation}
    S = - \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta T} = \frac{\sigma}{\alpha}.
\end{equation}

The thermoelectric conductance might be written as the derivative of the charge conductance following a Sommerfeld expansion:

$$
    \alpha=\frac{\pi^2 k_\mathrm{B}T}{3e}\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial\epsilon}|_{\epsilon=\epsilon_{F}}
$$
By using this expression it is possible to write the Seeback coefficient as solely a function of the charge conductance; this is the well-known Mott formula (see ~\cite{behnia2015fundamentals} for a complete derivation):
$$
    S=-\left.\frac{\pi^{2}k_\mathrm{B} T}{3e}\frac{\partial \ln (\sigma)}{\partial \epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=\epsilon_{F}} .
$$

From this equation one can appreciate the dependence of the Seebeck coefficient changes with the carrier transmission close to the Fermi level. By changing the molecular orbitals responsible for the electronic properties, an asymmetry of transmission around the Fermi energy (in the energy window $k_{\mathrm{B}} T$ ) leads to electrons migrating to the cold (warm) side, leading to a negative (positive) Seebeck coefficient.

The main assumptions we have tacitly made in order to derive the Mott equation is the neglection of any sort of interaction between the carriers and the thermal vibrations \cite{dubi2011colloquium}. Moreover, the Eq. is derived within the linear response theory, thus assuming a smooth varying density of states and excluding the presence of many-body effects. There are prominent examples for which the Mott formula breaks down such as low-disorder graphene \cite{ghahari2016enhanced} and quantum dots in the Coulomb blocked region \cite{beenakker1992theory}.

In order to quantify the efficiency of a thermoelectric device the three conductances can be combined in the dimensionless figure of merit:
$$
    Z T=\frac{S^{2} T \sigma}{\kappa}
$$
It is thus essential to maximize the numerator and minimize the denominator for achieving high figure of merits. All the transport coefficients, however, are intimately connected and particularly $\sigma$ and $S$ are mutually contra-indicated, meaning that it is impossible to increase one without decreasing the other.

To a certain extent these peculiarities can be addressed and tackled in nanostructures and one can in principle make two quantities independent \cite{venkatasubramanian2001thin}. As such, $ZT>1$ \cite{fu2015realizing} and greater than two have been observed in $\mathrm{PbTe}_{0.7} \mathrm{~S}_{0.3}$ and SnSe \cite{wu2014broad}.

The figure of merit $ZT$ is also informative for the heat flow across a device in an open circuit configuration. Since no bias current flows, there is a heat flow equal to $-\kappa \Delta T$, which consists of a purely thermal contribution and an electrical contribution due to electrons moving under the influence of the finite electric field created by the thermal gradient $(E \alpha T)$. The figure of merit $Z T$ sets the relative strength between the heat flow caused by the electric field and the purely thermal conductivity, meaning that if $Z T=1$ these components are equal in strength \cite{behnia2015fundamentals}.

Another figure often used to quantify the usefulness of a thermoelectric material as
a heater or cooler is the power factor given by
$$
    \mathcal{P}=S^{2} \sigma .
$$
The power factor is proportional to the maximum achievable output power density of a thermoelectric device in the linear regime and can therefore be seen as more meaningful than the figure of merit for unlimited or cost-free heat sources (such as geothermal heat and waste heat from car engines). In addition, it tends to be much easier to probe than the figure of merit, which necessitates a thermal conductance measurement

\section{Accounting of Vibrational Coupling: the Rate-Equation Model}

The rate equation model starts from a kinetic description of the electrode--molecule junction and assumes the overall current flowing through the molecule uniquely determine by how fast an electrons tunnels in and out from a weakly coupled molecular level \cite{seldenthuis2008vibrational, esposito2010self}. A promising extension of this framework attempts to quantify the quantum mechanical effects of the vibrational \gls{DOF} on the electron transfer rates. Here we will adopt the description reported in references \cite{thomas2019understanding} and \cite{sowa2018beyond, sowa2020beyond} where the electronic degrees of freedom interact with an unstructured environment modelled by a superohmic bath having an exponential cut-off:
$$
    \mathcal{J}(\omega)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\frac{\omega^3}{\omega_\textrm{c}}e^{-\omega/\omega_\textrm{c}}
$$
where $\lambda$ is the reorganization energy as defined from the Marcus model for electron transfer in solution and $\omega_\textrm{c}$ is a cut-off frequency.

The current through the junction can be written as:
$$
    I=|e| \frac{\gamma_\text{ox}^\text{S} \gamma_{\text {red}}^\text{D}-\gamma_{\text {red}}^\text{S} \gamma_\text{ox}^\text{D}}{\gamma_{\text {red}}^\text{S}+\gamma_{\text {red}}^\text{D}+\gamma_{ox}^\text{S}+\gamma_{ox}^\text{D}},
$$

\begin{figure}[hbtp]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter_theory/figures/single_level_model.pdf}
        \caption{Schematic for a single-level transport model. The single-particle states having energy $\epsilon_0$ and lifetime $\Gamma$ is the closest to the Fermi level of source $\mu_\textrm{S}$ and drain $\mu_\textrm{D}$, and solely contributes to the total current flowing across the junction. $\Gamma_\textrm{L}$ and $\Gamma_\textrm{R}$ are the tunnelling coupling with the left and right electrode, respectively.}
        \label{fig:theory:single_level_model}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}


where $\gamma_{\text{red/ox}}^\text{S/D}$ are the electron-transfer rates at each electrode corresponding to a reduction or an oxidation. If one consider the charge transition from $N$ to the $N+1$ charge state, the electron rates are given as follows:

\begin{align}
    \gamma_{\text{red}}^\text{S,D} & = 2 \frac{\Gamma_\text{S,D}}{\hbar} \int f_\text{S,D}(\epsilon) ~k_\text{red}(\epsilon) d \epsilon,          \\
    \gamma_\text{ox}^\text{S,D}    & =\frac{\Gamma_\text{S,D}}{\hbar} \int\left[1-f_\text{S,D}(\epsilon)\right]~k_\text{ox}(\epsilon) d \epsilon,
\end{align}

where $\Gamma_\text{S,D}$ is the electronic coupling between molecule and the electrodes having Fermi-Dirac distributions $f_\text{S,D}(\epsilon)$.

The density of states for the reduced and oxidized might be written as:
$$
    k_{\text{red/ox}}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{\pi} \operatorname{Re} \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{\sigma i(\epsilon-\epsilon_0) t / \hbar} e^{-t / \tau} B(t) d t,
$$
where $\sigma=1(-1)$ for reduction (oxidation) process, $\epsilon_0$ is the molecular energy level, $\tau=2 \hbar /\left(\Gamma_\textrm{S}+\Gamma_\textrm{D}\right)$ is the lifetime of the molecular electronic state. The term written as $B(t)$ is the phononic correlation function $t$:
$$
    B(t)=e^{D_{q}(t)} \cdot e^{D_{\textrm{bath}}(t)},
$$
Here we have written the phononic correlation function $B(t)$ as the product between a single-mode term and a mean-field contribution given by the environment. The two terms might be expressed as follows \cite{todd1994electron}:

\begin{align}
    D_{q}(t)         & = \left(\frac{g_{q}}{\omega_{q}}\right)\left[\left(\cos \omega_{q} t-1\right) \cdot \operatorname{coth}\left(\frac{\omega_{q}}{2 k_{B} T}\right)-i \sin \left(\omega_{q} t\right)\right], \\
    D_\text{bath}(t) & = \int \frac{J(\omega)}{\omega^{2}}\left[(\cos \omega t-1) \cdot \operatorname{coth}\left(\frac{\omega}{2 k_{B} T}\right)-i \sin (\omega t)\right] d\omega,
\end{align}

where $D_{q}$ is the single-mode contribution and $D_\text{bath}$ is the contribution from the superohmic bath. $g_{q}$ is the electron-vibration coupling strength, $\omega$ is the vibron frequency and the spectral density, $J(\omega)$, of the phonon bath with reorganisation energy, $\lambda$, and cut-off frequency, $\omega_{c}$, is

We use this model in Chapter~\ref{ch:solubility} to reproduce the low temperature transport features of a molecular graphene nanoribbon with a strong electron--vibron coupling.


\section{A Succint Primer of Fermi-Liquid Theory and Its Inadequacies}
In this section we will very briefly review the Fermi liquid theory and its failure in describing strongly-correlated 1D systems. The topics introduced here will explain the peculiarities in the density of states for a fermionic system and later be used to derive the tunneling density of states in the presence of long-range Coulomb interactions for a one-dimensional chain. These results are central to explain a few experimental results exposed in Chapter \ref{ch:gnrset}. A more exhaustive treatement of the following concepts might be reviewed in \cite{giamarchi2003quantum, cazalilla2011one, giamarchi2016one, bertel2017physics}.

The Fermi liquid theory was advanced by Lev Landau in 1956 as a phenomenological theory to decribe the low-temperature limit behaviour of liquid $^3$He heat capacity, which is surprisingly similar the ideal gas one. Since then, the theory has been used and extend also to capture many-body effects in fermionic systems, such as conducting electrons.

\begin{figure}[hbtp]
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter_theory/figures/1d_chain.pdf}
        \caption{Cartoon representing a 1D electron system. The density of the system is defined as the number of particles $N$ in confined in a 1D box of length $L$, i.e. $\rho=N/L=1/a$, where $a$ is the inter-particle spacing.}
        \label{fig:theory:1d_chain}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}

The ground state of a non-interacting gas may be fully captured following standard statistical mechanics. In this framework, of a great importance in calculating macroscopic quantities is the gas momentum distribution function $n^0_{\vec{k}}$, which depends on the wave number $\vec{k}$. More generally, the ground state depends also from the spin occupation number $\sigma$ but we will drop the spin subscript for the moment. For a fermionic gas, the zero-temperature limit $n^0_{\vec{k}}$ is the well-known step function which equals to:

\begin{equation}
    n^0_{\vec{k}} =
    \begin{cases}
        1 & k\le k_F \\
        0 & k > k_F  \\
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

At finite temperature, the momentum distribution function coincides with the Fermi--Dirac distribution for metals and with the Bose--Einstein distribution for bosons. The energy of a free particle follows the well-known parabolic dispersion, $\epsilon_{\vec{k}} = \hbar k^2/m$, and the total (multi-electron) energy change caused by an excitation might be expressed as a functional of the energy deviation from the ground state energy $E_0$:
\begin{equation}
    \delta E[\delta n] = E[n] - E_0 = \sum_{\vec{k}}\epsilon_{\vec{k}}~\delta n_{\vec{k}}
\end{equation}

Thus the single-particle energy might be written as a functional derivative of the total energy with respect to the total number of particles with a specific $\vec{k}$ vector:
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon(\vec{k}) = \frac{\delta E[n]}{\delta n_{\vec{k}}}
\end{equation}

An elementary excitation can therefore be thought as the process of adding/removing a particle to/from the ground state and an arbitrary excitated state may consist of a number of such particle-hole pairs. Close to the Fermi surface, $\vec{k} = k_F$, the energy dispersion is sufficiently linear enough to apply a Taylor series expansion:

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\vec{k}} = \epsilon_F + \upsilon_F\hbar(k - k_F) + \mathcal{O}((k-k_F)^2)
\end{equation}

where $\upsilon_F=\grad_{\vec{k}}\epsilon|_{\vec{k}=k_F}$ is the Fermi velocity. Landau's intuition was assuming that the low-lying eigenstates of the non-interacting Fermi gas continuously evolve into eigenstates of the multi-body (real) system as the interaction is adiabatically switched on. This process leads to the recovery of an individual excitation description, called quasiparticle, which is the starting point of the Fermi-liquid theory. Pictorially, a quasiparticle can be visualised as a fermion surrounded by a cloud of disturbance arises due to interaction with other particles. As a consequence, the mass is replaced by to effective mass $m^*=\hbar k_F/\upsilon$ that captures the strenght of the interaction cloud with the single particle.

Therefore, the quasiparticle dispersion may be written as:

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\vec{k}} = \epsilon_F + \upsilon\hbar(k - k_F) + \mathcal{O}((k-k_F)^2)
\end{equation}

In order to quantify the quasiparticle excitation energies, the energy functional has to be expanded to the second order throught a Taylor series expansion as I did before:

\begin{equation}
    \delta E[\delta n] = \sum_{\vec{k}} \epsilon_{\vec{k}}~\delta n_{\vec{k}} + \frac{1}{2V} \sum_{\vec{k}} f({\vec{k}}, {\vec{k'}})~\delta n_{\vec{k}} \delta n_{\vec{k'}}
\end{equation}

where the intesive quantity:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{f ({\vec{k}}, {\vec{k'}})}{V} = \frac{\delta^2 E[n]}{\delta_{\vec{k}} \delta_{\vec{k'}}}
\end{equation}
is known as Landau function and quantifies the interaction strength between quasiparticles. The energy of a quasiparticles can be Similarly defined as
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\epsilon}_{\vec{k}} = \frac{\delta E[\delta n ]}{\delta n_{\vec{k}}} = \epsilon_{\vec{k}} + \frac{1}{V}\sum_{\vec{k'}} f({\vec{k}},{\vec{k'}})\delta_{\vec{k'}}
\end{equation}.

So why the Fermi liquid theory fails to capture one-dimensional systems? Essentially because the Landau function is treated by means of a mean field approximation and a mean field kind of description breaks down in 1D \cite{giamarchi2003quantum}. This is caused by several reasons.

Firstly, for a system arranged in a linear chain fashion, only collective excitations exist. Pictorially, one might covince herself by thinking of an electron which starts to oscillate around its equilibrium position. Due to the strong electron--electron interactions the motion is fastly trasmitted all along the chain, resulting in a collective excitation. Secondly, in high dimensions we have seen that even if the particles interact with each other one can still recover an individual excitation description, where the interaction results in the concept of quasiparticles.

All these conditions break down for quantum systems in 1D because the one dimensionality introduces first strong quantum fluctuations that destroys the mean phase of your systems (for example in mean field theories one has $\psi = \abs{\psi} e^{i\phi}$ and you replace the phase $\phi$ with an averaged one).

\subsection{A Zest of the Tomonaga-Luttinger Model}

Several peculiarities encompass one-dimensional systems. As already mentioned, 1D excitations for an interacting fermionic systems are hydrodynamic-like modes--similar to sound waves--which are split into charge- and spin-density waves \cite{giamarchi2016one}. The absence of an efficient charge screening leads to long-range interactions, which cause strong correlations and the break down of an independent particle description. These presence of such strong correlations are the main reason why in one-dimensional systems one is required to compute correlation functions for calculating quantity of interest. From here, the wide employment of Green's functions (propagators).

One consequence of these characteristics is that--very conveniently-- in 1D systems it is often sufficient to conduct an asymptotic analysis for cahracterising your system. In particular, one has to provide the wave velocity $u$ and the interaction parameter $K$ (also called $g$), which is a dimensionless number which controls the decay of all the correlation functions as well as the thermodynamic quantities \cite{cazalilla2011one}.

The first model proposed in 1950 by Tomonaga aimed to describe the multi-fermionic ground state of an interacting Fermi gas and its excitations by treating them as solutions to field equations describing sound waves, which are bosonic \cite{tomonaga1950remarks}. The model was later extended by Luttinger in 1963 in an attempt to include arbitrary interaction strengths and range (short and long) \cite{luttinger1963exactly}. The correct solution was firstly advanced by Mattis and Lieb in 1965 \cite{mattis1994exact} and finalised by a genial intuition from Haldane in 1981 \cite{haldane1981luttinger}.

A central tool required to arrive to Haldane's solution is bosonisation, a mathematical technique which maps the excitation of fermions to bosonic modes of spin-density and charge-density waves and which prerequisite is the possibility of linearise the  single-particle (fermionic) dispersion close to the Fermi level \cite{von1998bosonization}.

This linear approximation is pushed to the extreme in the TLL model, meaning that fermions occupy the linearised energy spectrum all the way down to $-\infty$ on the energy scale (also called Dirac sea, Fig.~\ref{fig:theory:TLL}). In order to apply this assumption, the density of states has to be constant. Broadly speaking, TLL hamiltonian might be written as the sum between a kinetic and an interaction contribution:

$$
    \mathcal{H}_\textrm{TLL} = \mathcal{H}_0 + \mathcal{H}_\textrm{int}
$$

Using the second quantisation formalism, the kinetic part may be written as \cite{giamarchi2003quantum}:
$$
    \mathcal{H}_0 = \sum_{k; r = R,L} \upsilon_F(\epsilon_r k - k_F)c^\dagger_{k, r} c_{k, r}
$$

        \label{fig:theory:TLL}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}

where we have dropped the arrow notation for the wave vector as we are now dealing only with one dimension. Nothing fancy here: $c^\dagger_{k,r} (c_{k,r})$ is the creation (annihilation) operator acting in corresponding Fock space. Because the Fermi surface in 1D consists of just two points at $k=\pm k_F$, we can distinguish states in the right branch ($\epsilon_R=+1$) from those in the left ($\epsilon_L=-1$) branch. The corresponding dispersion relations are:

\begin{align*}
    \epsilon_{k, R} & = \epsilon_0 + \hbar \upsilon_F k   \\
    \epsilon_{k, L} & = \epsilon_0 - \hbar \upsilon_F k   \\
    \epsilon_{0}    & = \epsilon_F + \hbar \upsilon_F k_F
\end{align*}

Since states in the $R$ branch have positive velocities they are sometimes called \textit{right movers}; conversely, states in the left branch are referred as \textit{left movers}.

The interaction term is where the fun begins. In order to write it down, we shall first introduce a central quantity when one deals with one-dimensional systems. Because the system is subject to quantum fluctuations, even in the zero-temperature limit \cite{giamarchi2006strong}, the physics of the system will be governed by those processes which are close to the Fermi surface. Low-energy processes might be well captured by introducing the density operator\footnote[1]{A technicality here. Since the model includes all the negative states, the density operator as it has been written in Eq.~\ref{eq:theory:density_op} is infinite as the number of fermions is infinite. One has to introduce the \textit{normal ordering} of operators, meaning that what matters are deviations in the density with respect to the density of the ground state.}:

\begin{equation}
    \rho^\dagger(q) = \sum_k c^\dagger_{k+q, r}c_{k,r}
    \label{eq:theory:density_op}
\end{equation}

The interaction might then be written as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_\textrm{int}=\frac{1}{2V}\sum_{k,k',q}V(q)c^\dagger_{k+q}c^\dagger_{k'-q}c_{k'}c_{k}
    \label{eq:theory:lutt_int}
\end{equation}

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:theory:excitation_spectrum}, since $k$ and $k-q$ are restricted to be close the two points at $\pm k_F$, there are only two possible value of $q$ depending on the sign of $k$ and $k-q$. When $k$ and $k-q$ have the same sign, $\abs{q}\sim 0$. This corresponds to a scattering term for fermions moving in the same direction and might be interpreted as a \textit{forward scattering}. When $k$ and $k-q$ have opposite sign, $\abs{q}\sim 2k_F$, meaning that in this excitation fermions exchange side (\textit{backward scattering}).

The problem with the term in Eq.~\ref{eq:theory:lutt_int} is being the product between two density operators, when these are expressed in the fermion operator basis, these will expand to be a quartic function of the fermion operators. Problems then arises as it is not trivial to diagonalise a quartic hamiltonian. The genial intuition we were reffering to played by Haldane \cite{haldane1981luttinger} was to map the problem through a change of the basis into the bosonic operator world, where the interaction is reduced to a quadratic form, hence a closed-form solution is attainable. This process will be briefly outlined in the next section when I will derive the tunnelling density of states in case for a fermion tunneling thorugh a Luttinger liquid.





\subsection{Derivation of the Tunneling Density of States}

We will here briefly sketch the derivation for the tunneling \gls{DOS} for a Luttinger liquid. We will use the formalism typically encountered in the second quantization, where the both the wavefunctions and the operators are all expressed os operators (or--more generally--fields) acting in the Fock space. This formalism greatly simplies the descripition of multi-electron interacting sytems where single-particle interactions fails to capture all the features. Of central importance in this formalisms are the short and medium correlations between different parts of the systems, well captured by the Green's function (propagator) between the operator associated to a certain observables between to differnt time frames.


We start with the def. of tunnelling density of states:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\operatorname{dos}}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{d t}{2 \pi} e^{i \omega t}\left\langle\psi_{e}(t) \psi_{e}^\dagger(0)\right\rangle
\end{equation}

Now the bosonized form of the electron operator is
$$
    \psi_{e} = \psi_{L}+\psi_{R}
$$
where $\psi_{L / R}=e^{-i \varphi_{L / R}}/\sqrt{a}$
In order to diagonalise the LL Hamiltonian, it is convenient to introduce the following fields
$$
    \phi_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} g^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\varphi_{L} \mp \varphi_{R}\right)
$$
which canonically conjugated to the field $\theta(x)$. The latter models the fluctuations of the electron density $\theta ' = \delta n(x)/\pi$, where we used $\delta n(x)$ to denote the derivative of a functional. The electron operators for the two moving carriers might be rewritten then as:
$$
    \begin{aligned}
         & \psi_{L}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} e^{-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}} \phi_{-}+\sqrt{g} \phi_{+}\right)} \\
         & \psi_{R}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} e^{-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}} \phi_{-}-\sqrt{g} \phi_{+}\right)}
    \end{aligned}
$$
The fermionic propagator then reads:
$$
    \begin{aligned}
        \left\langle\psi_{e}(t) \psi_{e}^+(0)\right\rangle= & \left\langle\psi_{L}(t) \psi_{L}(0)\right\rangle+\left\langle\psi_{R}(t) \psi_{R}(0)\right\rangle \\
        =                                                   & \left\langle\psi_{L}(t) \psi_{L}(0)\right\rangle+ \text{h.c.}
    \end{aligned}
$$

The cross-terms such as $\left\langle\psi_{L}^{+} \psi_{R}\right\rangle$ vanish since chirality is conserved.

\begin{align*}
    \left\langle\psi_{L}^{\dagger}(t) \psi_{L}(0)\right\rangle & = \frac{1}{a}\left\langle e^{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\phi_{-}(t) \sqrt{g}\phi_{+}(t))} e^{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\phi_{-}(0) \sqrt{g}\phi_{+}(0))}\right\rangle                          \\
                                                               & = \frac{1}{a}\left\langle e^{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2g}}\phi_{-}(t)} e^{\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2g}}\phi_{-}(0)}\right\rangle\times\left\langle e^{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2g}}\phi_{+}(t)} e^{\frac{-i}{\sqrt{2g}}\phi_{+}(0)}\right\rangle
\end{align*}

We take advantage the Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff identity valid for exponential operators, and obeyed by the free bosonic fields

$$
    \left\langle e^{B_{1}} e^{B_{2}}\right\rangle=e^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\langle B_{1}^{2}+2 B_{1} B_{2}+B_{2}^{2}\right\rangle
$$

Using time-translational symmetry:

\begin{align*}
    \left\langle e^{\frac{i}{\sqrt{2g}} \phi_{-}(t)} e^{-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2g}} \phi_{-}(0)}\right\rangle
     & = e^{\frac{1}{2g}\left\langle \phi_{-}(t)\phi_{-}(0)-\phi_{-}(0) \phi_{-}(0)\right\rangle} \\
     & = \left(1+\frac{i\upsilon_{F}t}{a} \right)^{-\frac{1}{2 g}}
\end{align*}

where we used the relation $\left\langle\phi_{-}(t)\phi_{-}(0)\right\rangle = -\ln(1+i\upsilon_{F}t/a)$

Similarly,
\begin{equation}
    \left\langle e^{i\sqrt{\frac{g}{2}} \phi_{+}(t)} e^{-i\sqrt{\frac{g}{2}}\phi_{+}(0)}\right\rangle = (1+i\upsilon_{F}t/a)^{-\frac{g}{2}}
\end{equation}

Combining the two terms, the fermionic propagator then reads:
\begin{equation}
    \left\langle\psi_{e}(t)\psi_{e}(0)\right\rangle = \frac{1}{a}(1+i\upsilon_Ft/a)^{\frac{1}{2}(g+g^{-1})}
\end{equation}

Plugging in this expression into the one for the tunneling density of states yields:

$$
    \begin{aligned}
        \rho_{\operatorname{dos}}(\omega) & =\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{d t}{2 \pi} e^{i \omega t}\left\langle\psi_{e}(t) \psi_{e}^{\dagger}(0)\right\rangle                  \\
                                          & =\frac{1}{2 \pi a} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} d t e^{i \omega t}\left(1+i \upsilon_{F} t / a\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(g+g^{-1}\right)}
    \end{aligned}
$$

We are interested in the behaviour at low-energy, i.e. $\omega\rightarrow0$, hence--in the time--domain $t\rightarrow\infty$:

\begin{align*}
    \rho_{\operatorname{dos}}(\omega) & \sim \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt \frac{e^{i\omega t }}{t^{1/2(g+g^{-1})}} \\
                                      & \sim \omega^{\frac{1}{2}(g + g^{-1})-1}= \omega^{\alpha}
\end{align*}

Where the exponent $\alpha$ quantifies the strenght of the electron--electron interaction at short and medium range and it is commonly reported as:

\begin{equation}
    \alpha = \frac{1}{2}(g + g^{-1} - 2)
\end{equation}
