\co{what this chapter is about}

In this chapter, I will explain the rationale and context for this thesis: can we create graphene and, more broadly, graphene-derived structures that may one day offer a competitive advantage over ubiquitous Silicon in integrated circuits? To this goal, I will present a quick overview of the key all-carbon alternatives studied since the late 1990s. The chapter is organised such that it begins with a concise overview of solid-state crystal characteristics, followed by a discussion of how these properties lead to unique dispersion relations in graphene, \glspl{CNT}, and \glspl{GNR}. Second, a selection of the most significant experimental solutions developed to date will be critically analysed.

\section{Motivation}\label{intro:sec:motivation}

\co{There is less Silicon around and it is incrementally difficult to maintain costs associated with its production}
Silicon has been the undisputed leader in the fabrication of \glspl{IC} for almost thirty years. The widespread shortage of semiconductors that began in late 2020, however, has prompted both major chipmakers and the scientific community to think more deeply and actively about which non-silicon technologies and materials are valid candidates for replacing or, more realistically, complementing silicon.
Except for market leaders such as Intel, Samsung, and TSMC, long-term manufacturing costs remain largely expensive. Bloomberg reports that the fundamental costs of constructing a semiconductor manufacturing are approximately \$15 billion, with profit requirements of roughly \$3 billion to maintain market competition and avoid obsolescence~\cite{king2021}. These findings are also backed by a study conducted by the Boston Consulting Group last year, which ranked investments in research and development (22\% of annual semiconductor sales) and capital spending (26\%) as the highest in all industries~\cite{veras2021}.

\co{The success of CMOS is in the density scaling}
The first point-contact transistor prototype developed at the beginning of the 1940s by Bardeen, Brattain, and William Shockley at Bell Labs and the creation of \gls{CMOS} techology by Atalla and Kahng, ten years later, marked the beginning of integrated components~\cite{shockley1984path}.
Arguably, the density scaling capabilities of this technology has been one of the main reasons responsible for the costant technological development over a time span of more than 50 years. In this scenario, Moore's law is considered not just as a dependable method for estimating the future number of transistors per integrated circuit, but also as a driver of innovation and competitiveness.

\co{One of the problem of the advancement of modern IC is the scaling limitations of CMOS}
The search for new materials for integrated electronics is not just related to the race to increase the number of transistors per chip. Figure~\ref{fig:intro:moore_law} displays several trends in microprocessor figures of merit over the past five decades. The clock frequency is the number of clock cycles (number of electrical pulses) that a CPU can execute every second and is measured in Hz. During these electrical pulses, the CPU can conduct fundamental operations such as memory access, data writing, and instruction retrieval. The clock speed (or rate) is architecture-dependent, hence it must be compared within the same architecture (x86-32/64 architecture is the benchmark architecture).

\co{Another problem with CMOS technology is the power dissipated with the clock speed}
One of the most interesting positive correlation that it is possible to appreciate from the plot is, arguably, between the clock speed quantity with the power dissipated by the processor, typically static power, which is the power dissipated when a transistor is not switching. \Gls{CMOS} technology is well-known for low static power values for standard supply voltages \qtyrange[range-units = bracket]{3}{5}{\volt}. As the gate oxide thickness decreases, however, the probability of tunnelling for the charge carriers increases, resulting larger leakage currents and higher disspation powers~\cite{taur2021fundamentals}.

\section{Graphene}\label{intro:sec:graphene}

\co{crystal structure of graphene}
Graphene has a hexagonal structure where the carbon atoms are hybridised $sp^2$. In the International Table of Crystallography, graphene is included in the symmorphic space group \#191, with symmetry $D_{6h}$ ($P6/mmm$). The triangular lattice is obtained by considering two atoms per unit cell having the respective lattice vectors:

The carbon atoms in graphene form a two-dimensional hexagonal crystal with two atoms per unit cell, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:graphene_lattice}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_lattice_vectors}
    \vect{a}{_1} = \frac{a}{2}(3, \sqrt{3}),~
    \vect{a}{_2} = \frac{a}{2}(3, -\sqrt{3}),
\end{equation}
%
with $a\simeq1.42$ \si{\angstrom} be the carbon-carbon bond length.
Each atom belonging to one sublattice, called sublattice A, has three first neighbours belonging to the other sublattice, called sublattice B. With respect to one atom chosen from sublattice A as origin,
the position of these first three neighbours in real space are given by:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_first_neighbours}
    \vect{\delta}{_1} = \frac{a}{2}(1, \sqrt{3}),~
    \vect{\delta}{_2} = \frac{a}{2}(1, -\sqrt{3}),~
    \vect{\delta}{_3} = -a(1,0),
\end{equation}
%
While the reciprocal-lattice vectors are given may be written as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_reciprocal_vectors}
    \vect{b}{_1} = \frac{2\pi}{3a}(1, \sqrt{3}),~
    \vect{b}{_2} = \frac{2\pi}{3a}(1, -\sqrt{3}),
\end{equation}
%
In order to define the vertices of the Brillouin zone one has to apply the Bragg condition:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Bragg}
    2\vect{k}\cdot\vect{G} = 0
\end{equation}
%
where:

\begin{equation}
    \vect{G} = n\vect{b}_1 + m\vect{b}_2,
\end{equation}
%
The first amongst these are:

\begin{align*}
    n & = \pm 1, & m & =0,     & \vect{G} & =\pm\vect{b}_1,              \\
    n & = 0,     & m & =\pm 1, & \vect{G} & =\pm\vect{b}_2,              \\
    n & = \pm 1, & m & =\pm 1, & \vect{G} & =\pm(\vect{b}_1+\vect{b}_2),
\end{align*}

and all these vectors have the same modulus $\abs{\vect{G}} = \frac{4\pi}{3a}$. By applying Bragg's condition in the first of the three cases we get:

\begin{equation}
    \sqrt{3}k_{x} + k_{y} = \mp~\frac{4\pi}{3a},
\end{equation}
%
which gets satisfied by the points:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:K_point_components}
    K \equiv\mp~\frac{2\pi}{3a}\qty(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, 1)
\end{equation}
%
For the second case one gets:

\begin{equation}
    \mp(\sqrt{3}k_{x} - k_{y}) = \frac{4\pi}{3a},
\end{equation}
%
which gives the points:

\begin{equation}
    K' \equiv\pm~\frac{2\pi}{3a}\qty(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, -1)
\end{equation}
%
The third and last case gives the point:

\begin{equation}
    M \equiv\pm\qty(0, \pm~\frac{2\pi}{3a})
\end{equation}

Based on the experimental evidence collected in the last 20 years, the model that seems to best describe the proprierties of graphene is the Wallace model of the band structure, which dates back in 1947, and describes graphene as a semimetal with a linear dispersion of the electronic excitations close to the Fermi energy~\cite{wallace1947band}. Within the second quantization formalism ($\hbar = 1$ units), the tight-binding Hamiltonian may be written as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:graphene_hamiltonian}
    \mathcal{H}_\textrm{TB} = -t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,~\sigma}\qty(a^\dagger_{\sigma,i}b_{\sigma,j} + \textrm{H.c.}) - t' \sum_{\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle,\sigma}\qty(a^\dagger_{\sigma,i}a_{\sigma,j}+b^\dagger_{\sigma,i}b_{\sigma,j}+ \textrm{H.c.}),
\end{equation}
%
The notation $\langle ij\rangle$ means the sum runs over pairs of electrons which are nearest neighbours; similarly, the notation $\langle\langle ij\rangle\rangle$ specify a summation over the next nearest neighbours.

The two operators $a^\dagger_{i,\sigma}$ ($a_{i,\sigma}$) and $b^\dagger_{i,\sigma}$ ($b_{i,\sigma}$) creates (annihilates) an electron with spin $\sigma$ ($\sigma=~\uparrow,\downarrow$) for atoms on sublattice A and B, respectively. The value of $t$ which reproduces best experimental results is $t\simeq2.8$ \si{\electronvolt}, and quantifies the nearest-neighbour hopping energy; similarly, $t'$ denotes the next nearest-neighbour hopping energy.

By diagonalising this Hamiltonian it is possible to work out the energy dispersion of graphene, which reads

\begin{equation}\label{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands}
    E_{\pm}(\vect{k}) = \epsilon_p - t'f(\vect{k}) \pm t\sqrt{3 + f(\vect{k})},
\end{equation}
%
with $f(\vect{k})$ defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands_fk}
    f(\vect{k})=2\cos\qty(\sqrt{3}k_ya) + 4\cos\qty(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}k_ya)\cos\qty(\frac{3}{2}k_xa).
\end{equation}
%
Eq.~\ref{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands} identifies an upper ($\pi^*$) band associated with the plus sign and a lower ($\pi$) band described by the minus sign, and these are symmetric with respect to the single-particle zero energy. The experimental estimate reported for next nearest-neighbour hopping energy is $t'\simeq 0.1$ \si{\electronvolt} thus the asymmetry introduced is small.

If the system is not doped, there will be one electron per carbon atom, resulting in two electrons per cell: the lower band will be full, while the top band will be empty. Therefore, the system results to be semi-metallic. When examining the trend between the points \vect{K} and \vect{K'}, the peculiarities of graphene's dispersion become clear. By taking the positive components for the \vect{K} point as define in Eq.~\ref{eq:K_point_components} and expanding the Eq.~\ref{intro:sec:graphene:eq:energy_bands} as $\vect{k} = \vect{K} + \vect{q}$, with $\abs{(\vect{q})}\ll\abs{(\vect{K})}$, one gets:

\begin{equation}
    E_{\pm}(\vect{q}) = \epsilon_p \pm \frac{3at}{2}\abs{\vect{q}}
\end{equation}
%
This remarkable result tells us that the dispersion close to the Fermi level is linear in \vect{q}. For intrinsic graphene, at $T= \SI{0}{\kelvin}$  $E_F = \epsilon_p$. The Fermi velocity is defined by the usual relation $v_F = \frac{1}{\hbar}\qty(\dv{E}{\vect{k}})$,
and in graphene $v_F\simeq1\times 10^6~\textrm{ms}^{-1}$ $= 0.66~\textrm{eV}\times\textrm{nm}\times\hbar^{-1}$.

An important consequence of this result is that whilst for a two-dimensional electron gas the \gls{DOS} is constant, in graphene close to the Fermi level the \gls{DOS} reads:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:grafene:density_of_states}
    \rho(E)=\frac{2A_\textrm{cell}}{\pi}\frac{\abs{E}}{v_F^2}
\end{equation}
%
If graphene is charge-neutral, there are two Van Hove singularities in the DOS at $\rho(\omega_\textrm{VH}) = \pm 1$ \si{\electronvolt}~\cite{bena2005quasiparticle}. This behavior stands in stark contrast to the \gls{DOS} of
carbon nanotubes and graphene nanoribbons as they exhibit $1/\sqrt{E}$ singularities due to their 1D structure, which imposes a quantization perpendicular to the longitudinal direction of the ribbon or tube.

Using the Einstein-Smoluchowski relation $D = k_BT/\eta = \mu k_BT$, which links an average friction coefficient $\eta$ experienced when a flow of semiclassical charge carriers diffuse in a medium with coefficient $D$, the semiclassical conductivity may be computed from the \gls{DOS} as:

\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:semiclassical_conductivity}
    \sigma_{\textrm{sc}}=e^{2} \rho(E) v(E) \ell_{\mathrm{el}}
\end{equation}
%
where $v(E)$ is the carrier velocity, $\ell_{\mathrm{el}}$ the elastic mean free path, and $e$ the elementary charge. From Eq.~\ref{ch1:eq:semiclassical_conductivity} and the charge density $n(E)$, one can calculate the charge mobility, which, at zero temperature, reads:
\begin{equation}
    \mu(E)=\frac{\sigma_\mathrm{sc}}{e n}
\end{equation}
%
Close to the Dirac points, the experimental conductivities lie within $\simeq 2-5~e^{2}/ h$ range, although several experiments have demonstrated a variation of more than an order of magnitude, owing to doping from the electrical contancts and/or the substrate~\cite{jiang2007quantum, ozyilmaz2007electronic}.

Initial interest for using graphene as a channel material in CMOS logic may have been fueled by its remarkable mobility~\cite{schwierz2010nanometer}. In a standard CMOS gate, carriers are driven by a longitudinal electric field $\CMcal{E}_\textrm{SD}$ from the the source to the drain, and their motion is often limited by various scattering sources, such as collisions between electrons and vibrational modes of the crystal lattice from which the gate is made. In their first graphene-based \gls{FET} prototype, Novoselov and colleagues measured an impressive intrinsic mobility $\mu_e^{\textrm{in}}$ at \SI{290}{\kelvin} close to $\mu_e^\textrm{in}\simeq10,000$ cm$^{2}$V$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$~\cite{novoselov2004electric} using a single layer of graphene on a Si/\ce{SiO2} substrate serving as a back-gate. These results were explained by first assuming that graphene possesses conical rather than parabolic electron energy bands close to the Fermi energy, with electrons travelling at speeds close to the speed of light and, thus, with negligible effective mass. And second, by recognising that the honeycomb lattice symmetry implies a two-component wave function, which has the practical effect of cancelling direct carrier backscattering. Although it was believed that charged impurities~\cite{hwang2007carrier} or microscopic ripples~\cite{schedin2007detection} scatterings were predominant at low temperatures, it is now widely accepted that the very little electron--phonon scattering contribution even at room temperature is responsible for mobility values $\mu^{\text{in}}\simeq200,000$ cm$^{2}$V$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$~\cite{morozov2008giant}.

In order to successufully realise a field-effect transistor, however, a suitable material has to be able to switch on and off, as these are mapped onto the two logic levels high-voltage/low-voltage, and been able to perpertuate this states against voltage variation. The figure of merit associated with this requirement is the so called $I_\text{on}/I_\text{off}$. On-off ratio quantifies the ability of any circuit component in electrical contact with the transistor to distinguish between its on and off states in the absence of gate voltages. A high on-off ratio corresponds to a low leakage current, resulting in an enhanced device performance.

% short-channel effects, the off-current of a \glspl{MOSFET} increases as the channel length scales down. While the scaling of a graphene-based transistor could be, in principle, be robust against this effect, Klein tunnelling often represents the major hurdle when graphene is employed~\cite{schwierz2013graphene}.

While the scaling of a graphene-based transistor could be, in principle, be robust against off-state-current increases when the channel length is scaled down~\cite{schwierz2013graphene}.
Klein tunnelling in the setting of graphene p--n junction results from the absence of backscattering for electrons belonging to the same Dirac cone and the conservation of momentum parallel to a straight p--n interface~\cite{young2009quantum,katsnelson2006chiral}. Since incident electrons must conserve their transverse momentum, $k_y=0$ and are barred from scattering backwards, they will tunnel through symmetric potential barriers with transmission probability of one.

Within the thermoionic emission model for the drain current, when graphene is placed on top of a semiconductor structure, the two work functions align accordingly based on the doping and carrier concentration levels (see a pictorial representation in Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:graphene_device}). By assuming that the two work functions are identical, the height of the Schottky barrier between graphene and the semiconductor may be written as $\phi_B = E_g/2$, where $E_g$ is the bandgap energy of the semiconductor, and the on-off ratio may be estimated from the simple relation ~\cite{kim2011role}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bandgap_current_ratio}
    \frac{I_\textrm{on}}{I_\textrm{off}}\propto\exp{\frac{E_g}{2k_BT}},
\end{equation}
%
where the thermal energy $k_BT$ and the bandgap are both expressed in meV. Several researchers have seen bandgap values between 300 and 500 meV to yield the ideal rations for \Gls{CMOS} applications~\cite{schwierz2010graphene, reddy2011graphene}.


Despite considerable efforts, the metallic nature of graphene has hindered CMOS architectures from reaching an off state with a high resistance, deeming them unsuitable for logic applications. In contrast, graphene-based electronics have found broad use in applications requiring high frequencies. For analogue applications, properties such as ballistic transport, linear current-voltage characteristics, high transconductance, and exceptionally high sustained currents ($> 10^8$ \si{\ampere\per\square\centi\metre}) have proven to be optimal~\cite{meric2008rf, meric2008current}.

The assessment of two transistor characteristic frequencies, namely cutoff $f_\textrm{T}$ and maximum of oscillations $f_\textrm{max}$, is the primary tool to determine frequency response. The former indicates how quickly a signal may travel from the gate to the drain of the device and is often measured when the small-signal current gain approaches unity, whilst the latter is the highest possible frequency at which there is a non-vanishing power-gain~\cite{schwierz2010graphene}.
The frequency response is inversely related to the length of the gate and proportional to the transconductance, which is approximately proportional to mobility\footnote[2]{This is strictly valid is the mobilities are described within the diffusive regime. Carriers are close to ballistic values, then the transconductance is constant.}. Therefore, to achieve a high cut-off frequencies in a transistor, high carrier mobility must be maintained while the channel length is scaled down~\cite{schwierz2013graphene}. In this regard, numerous research groups have shown gapless graphene channel \gls{RF} capabilities.

Late in 2008, it was claimed that the first device with a cutoff frequency in the gigahertz region was fabricated using exfoliated graphene placed on a high-resistivity silicon substrate, with a 30-nm-thick top-gate dielectric made of \ce{HfO2} and a 500-nm-long gate~\cite{meric2008rf}. Despite a reported cutoff frequency of about $f_\textrm{T}\simeq\SI{14.7}{\giga\hertz}$ it was stated that excessive contact resistances and gate length were the primary causes of low ($f_\textrm{max}<\SI{1}{\giga\hertz}$) maximum frequency performance. The following year, Pd/Au contacts and a 12-nm-thick aluminum oxide were implemented in a 350-nm-long graphene gate, and although the cutoff frequency was increased to \SI{50}{\giga\hertz} the maximum frequency was increased only to \SI{2}{\giga\hertz}~\cite{lin2009development}. This poor performance was attributed to the organic contaminations on the graphene surface resulting from the use of poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) as a supporting layer during the transfer process, as well as the poor development of gate dielectrics~\cite{jang2014improved}.

After two years, a one-order-of-magnitude rise in the cutoff and the highest frequency of oscillation was demonstrated~\cite{lin2010100}. The devices are realised with an epitaxially grown silicon-carbide graphene MOSFET with a \SI{240}{\nano\metre} gate length, Ti/Pd/Au contacts, and hafnium oxide 10 nm thick as top gate. Local gating is frequently preferred as gate-source, and gate-drain capacitances are inversely proportional to the channel length and minimised in this geometric configuration, hence improving the cut-off frequency that can be reached~\cite{liao2012graphene}. In addition to $f_\textrm{T}\simeq\SI{100}{\giga\hertz}$, the highest maximum frequency ever measured was $f_\textrm{max}\simeq\SI{100}{\giga\hertz}$, beyond the capabilities of any technology at the time. It has been noticed that since the maximum oscillation frequency is significantly associated to device layout and parasitic resistances arising from the metallic contacts, partial passivation of the metal contacts and a multi-finger gate layout would be viable strategies to increase this parameter~\cite{xu2016contacts}.

Later the same year, a 144-nm-long gate device with $f_\textrm{T}\simeq\SI{300}{\giga\hertz}$ was developed using mechanically exfoliated graphene and \ce{Co2Si}–\ce{Al2O3} nanowire as self-aligned multi top-gates~\cite{liao2010high}. This result was also confirmed by comparing it to the predicted value based on the relationship\footnote[2]{$f_\textrm{T} = g_\textrm{m}/(2\pi C_\textrm{g})$~\cite{sze2021physics}} between dc transconductance, gate capacitance, and the cutoff frequency in the case where total input parasitic capacitance is negligible. The authors did not report the power $G^{1/2}_\textrm{max}$, only the current gain $\abs{h_{21}}$, making it difficult to assess the $f_\textrm{max}$ of the device. A rough estimate might be extracted by looking at the results reported by Cheng and colleagues on a similar device where the gate was realised through a stack of Au/Ti/\ce{Al2O3}~\cite{cheng2012high}. The achieved enhancements in the cutoff frequency response ($f_\textrm{T}\simeq\SI{427}{\giga\hertz}$ for a channel length of 67 nm) compared with CVD graphene-based device with similar gate length were, however, eclipsed by minor improvements to the device's maximum frequency response up to \SI{8}{\giga\hertz} when the gate length was lowered to 46 nm. As for the cutoff frequency, it is vital to minimise all parasitic resistances and capacitance in order to optimise $f_\textrm{max}$. In addition, the output conductance must be decreased in order to obtain the highest feasible operating speeds, which is often accomplished by operating the transistor in the drain current saturation regime.

IBM did also some research on the saturation behavior of short channel graphene transistors leveraging CVD graphene transferred to diamond-like and SiC substrates in an attempt to improve the power gain~\cite{wu2012state}. These are particularly attractive possibilities since the substrates are commercially accessible on high-quality wafers, allowing the scaling up of graphene arrays quite straightforward. Since the work function of graphene ($\phi^\textrm{g} = \SI{4.5}{\electronvolt}$) is greater than that of silicon carbide ($\phi^{\ce{4H}-\ce{SiC}} = \SI{3.7}{\electronvolt}$), negative charges accumulate on the graphene side of the contact, leaving graphene n-doped~\cite{sonde2009electrical}. The authors compensated this unwanted doping by using an innovative technique involving plasma-enhanced CVD silicon nitride as an intrinsic p-type gate dielectric. Positive performances at high speed were assessed not only by good cutoff frequencies ($f_\textrm{T}\simeq \SI{350}{\giga\hertz}$), but also by a twofold increase in maximum oscillation frequency $f_\textrm{max}$ $\simeq\SI{40}{\giga\hertz}$. According to my knowledge, the current graphene transistor power gain record of approximately \SI{70}{\giga\hertz} was achieved by using a (000-1) carbon-terminated surface of silicon carbide instead of the conventional (0001) Si, indicating that a low contact resistance between the gold contacts and graphene is the primary factor responsible for the high maximum frequency~\cite{guo2013record}.


In terms of cutoff frequencies, the performance of graphene-based devices is comparable to that of the leading solid-state competitors, such as GaAs pHEMT, InP HEMT, and Si MOSFET. The performance in terms of maximum frequencies, however, is one or two orders of magnitude below the required values for a competing technology~\cite{tan1990InGaAs, post2006RF,lai2007sub}.

In the scientific literature, one of the most commonly cited underlying factors for this behaviour is the weak drain saturation current in graphene, which ultimately depends on the lack of a bandgap in the channel. Because of their intrinsic semiconductivity, graphene-derived materials such as nanoribbons and carbon nanotube MOSFET could be a viable solution to this problem. In the following sections, I will investigate some important aspects of the electronic response at the device level for each class of materials.



\section{Carbon Nanotube}\label{intro:sec:cnt}

\co{Introduction: define your aims and objectives}
The carbon nanotube literature is vast and well-documented. In the part that follows, my primary aims are two. First, I will provide a comprehensive review of \glspl{CNTFET}, highlighting crucial elements that have, in my opinion, played a significant role in enhancing the competitiveness of nanotube-based transistors. The second objective is to quantify the device's performance in order to compare it to \gls{GNR}-based transistors.

In contrast to graphene, \gls{CNT} have an electrical bandgap that varies according to the nanotube's chirality and diameter~\cite{dresselhaus2000carbon}. Any nanotube can be defined in the real space by defining two unit vectors $\vect{a_1}$ and $\vect{a_2}$ and any linear combination of these with integer coefficients, typically labelled $m$ and $n$. Depending on their values, a nanotube may be metallic, quasi-metallic, or semiconducting; to this simplified picture, one may add exceptions induced by the strong curvature (which leads to a certain kind of spin-orbit interactions) when the diameter of the tube is extremely small~\cite{laird2015quantum}.

%new
As depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:cnt_structure}, states resulting from quantization in the circumferential direction may or may not pass through one of the $\vect{K}$ points in the first Brillouin zone of graphene. The resulting nanotube will be metallic if it crosses a $\vect{K}$ point; else, it will be semiconducting. This feature of the crystal structure was essential for the development of the first prototype \gls{CNTFET}.

1998 saw the discovery of charge-carrier modulations within a nanotube produced by a gate-induced field from the group of Cees Dekkar~\cite{tans1998room}. These early \glspl{FET} were manufactured using a doped silicon substrate as the gate to regulate the energy bands, with thermally-grown silicon dioxide acting as the dielectric between the nanotube and the substrate, and Au contacts to electrically contacting  the nanotube.

Since then, other transistor designs have been proven and can be classified fundamentally as devices with or without a spacer. The length of the spacer $L_\textrm{spr}$ is the gap between the source/drain metal contacts and the channel. Given that a certain class of nanotube is intrinsically semiconducting, in spacer-less devices the motion of charge carriers is not induced by the electric field $\CMcal{E}_\textrm{SD}$ in the channel, but are instead injected from the contacts. The role of the gate is to lower the thermal barrier in the channel, thereby permitting tunnelling through the \gls{SB} at the source. In this case, the height of the metal's work function $\phi^\textrm{M}$ plays a crucial part in the transistor's performance.

In contrast, \glspl{CNTFET} with spacers are less constrained by injection at the source since the electric field created by the gate between source and drain controls the carriers. It has been demonstrated that a device with spacers is more resistant to ambipolar conduction due to the substantial carrier barrier injection at the drain~\cite{torres2014introduction}; Tab.~\ref{tab:CNFET} provides an overview of gated-spacer (top and bottom) devices.

\co{Contacts and $n$-type Nanotubes, to rephrase}
%new
Another critical issue for nanotube transistors is to control the conduction type of devices: $p$-type transfer characteristics have been reported for the vast majority of tubes. This is partly due to $p$-type of contaminations following the lithograpic process that are involved in the conduction~\cite{nosho2005n} even though $n$-type devices are indispensable for nanotube integrated circuits with low-power dissipation, similar to Si-based CMOS devices.

An interesting solution tested, for example, was doping the nanotube-channel surface with element of the first two groups of the periodic table (ions K+, Mg2+, etc) to fabricate $n$-type nanotube FETs~\cite{bockrath2000chemical, zhou2000modulated}. Precise control of the doping density per nanotube unit length is necessary, which is problematic. Additionally, ionized impurities are often a source of uncontrolled electron transport due to scattering events with the charge carriers. Moreover, instability in the air, and temperature fluctuations are other factors which have discoureged the chemical doping route.

The operation mechanism of the nanotube FETs has been widely accepted to be satisfactorily explained by Schottky barrier modulation model, in which the gating action is dominated by the Schottky barrier formed at the contact between the nanotube and the source metal~\cite{bachtold2001logic}. Solid-state semiconductors have dangling bonds at the surface, resulting in Fermi level pinning, which means that the variation of the band bending is independent of the work function of the metal, even when it is significant~\cite{sankey1984si}. Since the ideal tube structure has no open bonds, there is no pinning of the metal Fermi level to mid-gap states, which justifies the study of various work functions for improving performance and tuning polarity.
deviceNormalized by the tube width.
Numerous metals have been examined for nanotube interaction. It has been established that palladium ($\phi^\textrm{Pd}\simeq$ \SIrange{5.2}{5.6}{\electronvolt}) creates a nearly perfect $p$-channel contact with the valence band of \gls{CNT}. The reason for this phenomena was explained within the Schottky barrier framework in which the larger work function makes the Schottky barrier high for holes lower, thus reducing the overall contact resistance between the metal and the nanotube~\cite{javey2003ballistic}. This behaviour, however, is not universal as demonstrated by other metals such as platinum ($\phi^\textrm{Pt}\simeq5.65$ eV) which produces weaker contacts despite having a larger work function than Pd due to the physical wetting of the metal on the nanotube~\cite{lim2009contact}.

Taking these reports into account, if we use materials with a small work function as contact electrodes of nanotube FETs, the Schottky barrier height for electrons is low. In this case, it is expected that electrons are injected from the contact electrode into the nanotube and $n$-type nanotube FETs will be realized without any doping into the nanotube channel. The other problem associated with using low work function metals as contacts is their increased propensity to oxidise. Using metals with a high work function to cap the contact metals is a simple and effective solution for the contact surface's tendency to destabilise in air. High-performance $n$-type devices were demonstrated by using Sc-and Y-contacted \glspl{CNTFET}, delivering high on state currents (250 micro ampere), mobility of 5100 \SI{5100}{\cm\square\per\V\per\second}, and \gls{DIBL} of 105 \si{\mV\per\V}~\cite{ding2009contacted}. These devices reported an impressive performance for the subthreshold swing $SS\simeq\SI{73}{\milli\volt}$~dec$^{-1}$.

The subthreshold swing measures the rate at which the gate voltage switches off the transistor, and it is the gate-voltage shift necessary to cause a one-order-of-magnitude change in drain current. It is a transistor' figure of merit often indirectly linked to heat dissipation, as this represents of the primary obstacles to miniaturising \gls{CMOS} technology. Since the carriers are thermally injected, it is challenging to reduce the operating voltage while improving switching (operational) speed and maintaining a low off-state power dissipation. Since the off-state power is proportional to the off-state current, $P_\textrm{off} = RI_\textrm{off}^2$, the objective is to achieve a low off-state current without sacrificing the on-state output current. The process of charge carrier transfer by thermal means imposes a limit on the subthreshold slope, which for the \gls{MOSFET} technology is approximately $2.3 k_BT/q = \SI{60}{\milli\volt}$~dec$^{-1}$ at room temperature.

One of the most promising approaches to overcoming the Boltzmann limit proposes a different source--carrier injection mechanism based on Zener tunneling rather than thermal means. When the transmission probability does not depend on the energy gap between the metal and the nantoube work functions, but instead occurs through interband tunneling, the transmission probability becomes temperature independent. Several architecture proposals have envisioned carbon nanotubes and graphene nanoribbons transistors operating on this basis~\cite{ionescu2011tunnel, hammam2018sub, hwang2019room}.

\subsection{Scaling}
%new
Si technology's ability to function below 10-nm channel length was questioned for a long time, but recent scientific advances have demonstrated its viability and fostered a plethora of commercial uses (currently, modern laptops employ the 5-nm technology). Nonetheless, Si scalability has always been a significant starting point for investigating methods and materials that might overcome short-channel effects (threshold voltage roll-off, off-state leakage current, and channel length modulation are just a few of the problematics).

In carbon nanotube transistors, the difference between two key lengths for scaling, namely the contact $L_\textrm{c}$ and channel $L_\textrm{ch}$ lengths, is crucial yet often underestimated. Aggressive scaling for the channel length typically results in the loss of control over the band bending across the gate: intuitively, as the channel length is reduced, the electric field created by the source contact will be felt by the charge carriers more and more. The thesis presented here is that carbon nanotubes provide a suitable platform for such scaling due to their ultrathin hollow nature. Alternatively, this could be a double-edged sword. Due to a higher tunnelling probability, the lower effective carrier mass of nanotubes\footnote[2]{$m^*_\textrm{CNT} = (0.228 \pm 0.129)m_0$~\cite{marulanda2008carrier}} may increase the tunnelling current from source to drain; typically, this effect has implications such as an increase in the subthreshold swing and leakage currents in the off state.


Franklin and coworkers conducted one of the earliest systematic studies on channel length scaling effects in 2010 via a series of experiments on nanotubes using hafnium-based oxide-coated local bottom gates~\cite{franklin2010length}. When the channel length was reduced by more than two orders of magnitude (from \SI{3}{\si{\micro\metre}} down to \SI{15}{\si{\nano\metre}}), a consistent trend was found in both the threshold voltage ($V_\textrm{Th}\simeq\SI[separate-uncertainty = true]{-0.5(0.2)}{V}$) and subthreshold swing ($SS\simeq\SI{90}{\milli\volt}$~dec$^{-1}$) values.

The threshold voltage is the gate voltage required to fully invert the semiconductor. It is typically measured by applying a slight drain bias and measuring the drain current as a function of the gate voltage, provided that the drain current is at least one order of magnitude smaller than the gate voltages applied, scaled accordingly by the gate capacitance.

In addition, it was predicted that scaling had little to none effect on the transconductance $g_m$ of the device. The transconductance of a conventional \gls{MOSFET} is the change in drain current divided by the amount that the gate-to-source voltage varies at a constant bias. Typical values lies in the range \qtyrange[range-units = bracket]{1}{30}{\milli\siemens\per\micro\metre} for a small-signal field-effect transistor.

For an $n$-channel MOSFET with a certain threshold voltage, the drain current at a given gate in the saturation regime ($V_\textrm{Th} > V_\textrm{G} - V_\textrm{SD}$) is defined as follows:
\begin{equation}
    I_\textrm{SD} = \frac{1}{2}\mu_n C_\textrm{ox}\frac{W}{L}\qty(V_\textrm{G} - V_\textrm{Th})^2, \label{eq:transistor-drain-current}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mu_\textrm{n}$ is the electron mobility, $C_\textrm{ox}$ is the gate oxide capacitance, $W$ and $L$ are the gate width and length, respectively.

It is possible to work out the value of $g_m$ by using the Shichman--Hodges model and by assuming that the threshold voltage $V_\textrm{Th}$ is a device constant, such that $V_\textrm{ov} = V_\textrm{G} - V_\textrm{Th}$:
\begin{equation}
    g_\textrm{m} = \pdv{I_\text{SD}}{V_\text{GS}} =\frac{2~I_\textrm{SD}}{V_\textrm{ov}},\label{eq:schiman-hodges-model}
\end{equation}
%

This quantity $V_\textrm{ov}$ is the so-called overdrive voltage, which physically corresponds to a value just above the threshold voltage, and it is typically defined as $V_\textrm{ov} = V_\textrm{G} - V_\textrm{Th}$. This parameter is frequently employed during the design phase of a transistor because it conveniently ties the drain current with the applied bias: in fact, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:transistor-drain-current} and identifying $k_n = \mu_n C_\textrm{ox}\frac{W}{L}$, one obtains:
\begin{equation}
    I_\textrm{SD} = \frac{1}{2}k_n V_\textrm{ov}^2 \implies V_\textrm{ov} = \sqrt{\frac{2I_\textrm{SD}}{k_n}}
\end{equation}

The advantage of this equation is that the relationship written in terms of overdrive voltage rather than threshold voltage holds true for both $n$- and $p$-types of doping. To provide some background, the overdrive voltage for \SI{65}{\nano\metre} technology is commonly set between 70 and \SI{200}{\milli\volt}. Franklin and his colleagues produced transconductance values as high as \SI{40}{\micro\siemens\per\micro\metre} for a \gls{CNTFET} with a channel length of \SI{15}{\nano\metre}, which corresponds to transconductance values between approximately \qtyrange[range-units = bracket]{91}{110}{\milli\siemens\per\micro\metre}, thereby meeting the requirements for compatibility with Si technology.

\subsection{Contact Length Scaling}
MOSFET devices, when shrunk down to the nanoscale, need to consider not only $L_\textrm{ch}$ but also the lenght of the nanotube with the source and drain, $L_\textrm{c}$. In particular, computational studies have shown that higher contact transparency is expected when the metal-nanotube coupling is weak~\cite{fediai2015electron, teichert2019improved, fediai2016towards}. Especially for very long contacts, one conclusion which could be drawn from these studies is that in order to achieve high contact transparency one should minimize coupling and maximize the contact length. In the specific case of Pd and Ti contacts, the weaker nanotube-metal coupling in the case of Pd is a good explanation for the superiority of Pd-based nanotube contacts~\cite{liu2017scaling}.

By using the transfer length model, the contact length was estimated to be inversely dependent on the contact resistance between the nanotube and the metallic pads~\cite{purewal2007scaling, franklin2010length}. These experimental observations were initially explained by assuming dipole moments built up at the \gls{CNT}--metal interface~\cite{leonard2000role}. The presence of dipoles might be caused by interface states at the junctions formed by charges which provide a potential that decays within a few nanometers from the junction. A high density of dipole charge translates to a high density of interface states, which is expected to pin the Fermi level. Since in a planar contact, the dipole in a metal-CNT contact is localised in all directions, the overall effect of these interface states is to shift the bands within a few nanometers of the junction, generating a narrow barrier, whilst the energy of the bands deeper within the CNT remain the same as for a contact without interface states~\cite{svensson2011schottky}. One conclusion one may draw from this is that by having a electrical contact which has a work function closer to the the nanotube might protect the metal--nanotube junction to undergo to Fermi level pinning effects caused by surface states.

In addition to considering different contact geometries, more advanced methodologies were applied to clarify the role of the chemical bonds produced between the metal and carbon structures, or some combination of metal-/carbon-dependent interfacial charge characteristics. Cuniberti and coworkers found that the scaling of the contact resistance is mostly driven by diagonal elements of the self-energy matrix through a comprehensive \gls{NEGF} methodology~\cite{fediai2016towards}. The significant fluctuations of these elements for weakly interacting contact metals were not significant for contacts of infinite length, but their work demonstrates they have an important role when the contact length is finite. To quantitatively characterise the contact length scaling, they introduced the concept of an effective contact length, which is defined as a contact length for which the contact resistance is \%5 greater than the infinite contact case and depends solely on the local metal-nanotube coupling rather than the tube diameter~\cite{fediai2015electron}.

The researchers hypothesised that the most unfavourable length scaling is observed for weakly-interacting metals such as Cr, Pd, and Pt, for which the effective contact length is greater than 100 nm; on the other hand, metals such as Au, Al, and Sc were nearly insensitive to the contact length when this was decreased to 15 to 22 nm. Furthermore, the authors proposed that if the infinite length contact resistance is low, the element of the self-energy matrix must be greater than 30 meV in order to maintain a low resistance of less than 10 nm. This criterion appeared to be met by an unexpected contact material proposed by the authors, Rh, for which they projected a better scaling of the contact resistance in comparison to Au, Pt, and Pd. For long Rh-CNT connections, the scientists examined the contact resistance from Pd and Rh and found little to no difference, indicating that the scaling benefit gained by using Rh may not be worth the expenses if precise control over the nanotube anchoring location is limited.



\section{Graphene Nanoribbons}

%new
Among the all-carbon alternatives to silicon, graphene nanoribbons have garnered considerable attention for logic applications. These require two well-defined voltage states and the ability to rapidly flip between the two states while maintaining a low leakage current in the off state. As I have already mentioned at the beginning of the chapter, one of the primary objections of graphene's use in digital electronics is the lack of an energy gap, which would allow the device to remain in an off state without any leakage current when the applied potential is low or zero~\cite{schwierz2010graphene, schwierz2013graphene}.

%new
The confinement of large-area graphene carriers within a 1D channel creates an electronic bandgap dependent on the channel width and length~\cite{Han2007}. Early theoretical calculations have in fact shown that when the ribbon width approached the widhts around 2-3 nm, the resulting bandgap becomes closer to those of Ge or InN. By further reducing down the width (1-2), this should technically approach bangaps closer to those of Si or GaAs~\cite{barone2006electronic}.
In fact, early theoretical studies have demonstrated that when the ribbon width approaches widths of \qtyrange[range-units = bracket]{3}{4}{\nano\metre}, the resulting bandgap approaches those of Ge and InN. By further lowering the width to \qtyrange[range-units = bracket]{3}{4}{\nano\metre} range, this should theoretically approach the bandgaps of Si or GaAs~\cite{barone2006electronic}.

%new
Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:gnr-bandgap_schwierz} shows various ribbon widths and their corresponding bandgaps, as reported by theoretical and experimental studies. Moreover, this graph illustrates the paucity of ribbons with suitable widths when optimal bandgap range for \gls{CMOS} applications is considered to be in the \qtyrange[range-units = bracket]{0.1}{1}{\electronvolt} range~\cite{geng2017graphene, schwierz2013graphene}. One of the key reasons for the scarcity of appropriate \glspl{GNR} is that the bottom-up process for the synthesis of graphene ribbons was still in its infancy at the time this graph was created.

%new
The \textit{bottom-up} methodology is a technique for synthesising graphene ribbons in which the ribbon structure is created from molecular precursors reacting using reaction patterns commonly inherited from polymer chemistry and pericyclic reactions. Pioneered by two seminal papers by Hongjie Dai~\cite{Li2008} and Klaus M{\"u}llen~\cite{cai2010atomically}, it is now deemed the ideal technique to produce high-quality graphene nanoribbons.

With these class of techniques, hundred nanometer-long ribbons with widths less than \SI{5}{\nano\metre} have been synthesised~\cite{narita2015bottom}, which may be appropriate for interconnection applications requiring high current density to be maintained without causing voltage drop. Secondly, important nano-and- optoelectronics properties such as electronic bandgap, charge-carrier mobility, and optical absorption have become tunable by either varying \glspl{GNR} chemical structures or by introducing opportune decorations along the ribbon edges~\cite{Liu2015, hu2018bandgap}. Fig.\ref{fig:intro:gnr-canvas} gives a glimpse on the vast variety of edge structures generated by bottom-up techniques, suggesting the effectiveness and versatility of this approach over early \textit{top-down} approaches. These were typically accomplished by either patterning ribbons through lithography with \gls{EBL}~\cite{RN345, Han2007} or by longitudinal unzipping of single-walled nanotubes~\cite{kosynkin2009longitudinal}, or by cutting graphene sheets with metallic nanoparticles~\cite{RN346}. These methods, however, frequently produced wide widths and uncontrollable structural defects~\cite{han2010electron, Evaldsson2008}. Finally, bottom-up technology has made it possible to synthetically control the kind of defects injected into the carbon lattice, and this technological superiority is considered by many to be a substantial advantage compared to graphene and carbon nanotubes~\cite{lombardi2021synthetic,lombardi2019quantum}.

\subsection{Crystal and Band Structure}
Armchair and zigzag ribbons were historically the first two edge structures studied. The variation in the edge connectivity has a significant impact on the respective band structure. In general, the energy spectrum of a \gls{GNR} with length $L$ and width $w_\text{GNR}$ may be written as:

\begin{equation}
    E_{n,m} = \pm\hbar v_F^*~\sqrt{\qty(\frac{n\pi}{L})^2 + \qty(\frac{m\pi}{w_\text{gnr}})^2}
    \label{eq:gnr_energy}
\end{equation}
%
where $v_F^*$ is the group velocity for an energy band indexed by the pair of quantum numbers $n,m$~\cite{baringhaus2014exceptional}.

Steven Louie's group was one of the first to characterise the electrical properties of armchair ribbon based on the number of carbon dimer lines $N$ across the ribbon width using \gls{TB} calculations~\cite{son2006energy}. The authors of this seminal study discovered that when $N$ is a multiple of three of an integer $p$, the resulting ribbons feature large bandgaps that scale inversely with the width of the ribbon\footnote[2]{The $E_g \propto 1/w_\text{gnr}$ relation holds when $15~\text{nm} \le w_\text{gnr} \le 90~\text{nm}$~\cite{yang2007quasiparticle}} (Tab.~\ref{tab:agnr-tb}), even in the case the criterion $N=3p+1$ is met, this trend persists qualitatively. In contrast, $N=3p+2$ indicated that the ribbon family would be metallic.~\cite{nakada1996edge}.

When the width of the ribbon falls below \SI{10}{\nano\metre}, however, substantial electron-electron interactions induced by the system's one-dimensionality lead to significant, non-negligible self-energy correction terms that have been satisfactorily captured by many-body theories~\cite{yang2007quasiparticle} - Tab.\ref{tab:agnr-gw} summarises the estimates for these corrective terms. A few years later, STM tests on surface-synthesised 5-atom wide AGNRs supported these predictions, reporting bangaps of $\simeq\SI{100}{\milli\electronvolt}$~\cite{kimouche2015ultra}. Their findings also shed some light on the implication that finite-size effects must be considered when determining the scaling behaviour of the energy gap with ribbon length. The authors determined the orbital energies with respect to the Fermi level by measuring the differential conductance for ribbons of various lengths. As anticipated, the gap between the \gls{HOMO} and \gls{LUMO} rapidly reduces as ribbon length increases. What they also report in their work is that while the energy gap scales quadratically with ribbon length $E_g \propto 1/L^2$, the energy spacing between electronic states that belong to the same band scales inversely with ribbon length $E_g \propto 1/L$, as predicted by modelling the behaviour of these states using energy states of graphene with linear dispersion. In addition, between 4- and 5-monomer long ribbons, the \gls{HOMO} level passes the Fermi level, causing the ribbon to become positively charged. There is no rapid change in the orbital energies due to the ribbons' charge, indicating that the charging energy is negligible and may be disregarded~\cite{swart2011single}.

Z\glspl{GNR}'s electronic properties are qualitatively and quantitatively distinct from those of AGNR. In order to define the unit cell of a ZGNR, just two atoms per unit cell are required, as opposed to four for an AGNR. TThe locations of these two atoms within the unit cell and the translation symmetry of the lattice give rise to a symmetry element--a $2_1$ screw axis--present along the direction of width extension. Most notably, the presence of glide planes and screw axes in nonsymmorphic space groups\footnote[3]{Nonsymmorphic groups are space groups that are not uniquely defined by the lattice translations' repeating of a point-group motif. Te and $\alpha$-quartz are examples of nonsymmorphic groups.} (as for the wave vector group) causes energy bands to converge along certain high symmetry points and axes in the Brillouin zone~\cite{dresselhaus2007group}. This \textit{flat band} region is positioned at wave vectors $2\pi/3 \le k \le \pi$, and the electronic states in this region have been interpreted as localised states along the zigzag edge by looking at charge distribution maps~\cite{wakabayashi2010electronic}. In one-dimensional systems, these electronic states are known as \textit{edge states}, and their contribution to the entire wavefunction becomes minimal when their widths approach the micrometre range, other than exhibiting an exponential decay of their \gls{DOS} with distance from the ribbon's centre~\cite{kobayashi2006edge,son2006energy}.

When the wave vector matches the ratio between $\pi$ and the unit cell distance $d_z$, i.e. $k = \pi/d_z$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:zgnr_bandgap_edge_state_delocalisation}\textbf{c}), it is possible to induce an energy gap between energy valleys. It has been proved by spin-polarized ab-initio calculations that two distinct magnetic orderings are distinguishable in the ground state.: a ferromagnetic ($\uparrow\uparrow$, FM) configuration for spins aligned along a single edge and an antiferromagnetic ($\downarrow\downarrow$, AFM) condition for spins belonging to two distinct edges~\cite{son2006half}. \textit{Half metallicity} is the property of experiencing the phase transition from an AFM configuration (semiconducting) to an FM state (metallic), and it has been established that this property should be theoretically accessible if a magnetic field is induced parallel to the ribbon extension plane~\cite{dutta2009intrinsic}.

\subsection{Edge States}
A few years later, the chiral \gls{DOS} for the electronic states at the ribbon edges had to be incorporated into the interpretation of the previous mentioned localised states and the exotic features that followed these~\cite{yazyev2011theory}. Chirality is a remarkable phenomenon that arises when the charge carriers' time-reversal symmetry is broken, and it is possible to discriminate between carrier wavefunctions evolving in time with a clockwise phase and those evolving with a counterclockwise phase. In the molecular domain, geometrical chirality refers to any molecule belonging to a symmetry group without improper rotations, i.e. the spatial symmetry is such that the rotamer cannot be superimposed on its mirror image~\cite{lazzeretti2017chiral}.

Similary to carbon nanotubes, graphene nanoribbons can have chiral edges. Typically, chirality may be described by a chiral angle $\theta$ which may be defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \arcsin\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}\qty(\frac{m^2}{n^2+nm+m^2})}
\end{equation}
%
where the nanoribbon's edge orientation is determined by translation vector $(n,m)$ of the graphene lattice. Zigzag and armchair are characterised by $\theta=0^\circ$ and $\theta=30^\circ$, respectively, while chiral edges have $\theta$ in the range between.
Steven Louie and co workers employed a mean-field Hubbard Hamiltonian for modelling the \gls{GNR} dispersion relation, assuming the on-site Coulomb repulsion $U$ comparable to the hopping integral $t$ between two adjacent sites ($U/t \sim 1$):

\begin{equation}\label{eq:gnr_chiral_hubbard_hamiltonian}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{H}_{\textrm{MF}} = &- t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle,~\sigma}\qty(a^\dagger_{\sigma,i}a_{\sigma,j} + \textrm{H.c.}) \\
        &+ U\sum_{i}\qty(n_{i\uparrow}\langle n_{i\downarrow}\rangle + \langle n_{i\uparrow}\rangle n_{i\downarrow} - \langle n_{i\uparrow}\rangle \langle n_{i\downarrow}\rangle),
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
%
whereby the second summation is performed on spin-polarized densities $n_{i\sigma} = a^\dagger_{i\sigma}a_{i\sigma}$. This Hamiltonian preserves electron-hole symmetry, which means that the density of states is symmetric with respect to the Fermi level.

The experimental evidence was accurately characterised by the wavefunction solutions of the associated eigenvalue problem, which predicted two pairs of peaks divided in the \gls{DOS} and linked to two distinct magnetic orderings. One peak split was caused by the AFM correlation between opposite edges, whereas the second, greater peak split was caused by FM ordering along a single edge (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro:zgnr_bandgap_edge_state_delocalisation}). The energy difference between the two magnetic orderings was estimated to be in the order of tens of meV per edge atom and to decrease as the ribbon width increases, eventually becoming negligible when the width is significantly larger than the exponential decay length of spin-polarized states~\cite{lee2005magnetic}. In theory, it should then be possible to probe this energy difference by either study the tunnelling density of states or by using high-frequency resonante techniques.

These theoretical predictions have been supported by experimental data after many years of effort. Using electron-spin resonance techniques at a frequency of \SI{94}{\giga\hertz}, Slota and colleagues elucidated magnetic orderings that were localised along the edges~\cite{slota2018magnetic}. Their ribbon structure was distinguished by the presence of stable nitroxyl radicals as spin injectors along the ribbon's edges, permitting the separation of the magnetic edge fingerprint from the signal of the radical. In addition, their study shown that the edge state has a longer coherence time than the majority of $\pi$-extended organic systems, even at room temperature and in powder, which makes graphene nanoribbons particularly interesting for spintronics applications. Therefore, it would be desirable to investigate the tunability of magnetic characteristics by electrical control in single zigzag graphene nanoribbons, leveraging experimental techniques borrowed from molecular electronics, such as tunnel break junctions, for instance. In fact, this was one of the present thesis's primary objectives.

The Dai group developed one of the first bottom-up \gls{GNR} employing sonochemistry and Pd for ribbon electrical contact. As discussed previously, the large work function of Pd should reduce the Schottky barrier height for minority carrier injection in doped substrates~\cite{wang2008room}. The authors examined the impact of ribbon widths as small as $w_\textrm{GNR}\simeq$ \SI[multi-part-units = single]{2.0(5)}{\nano\metre} on the electrical performance of the material while keeping ribbon lengths roughly constant $l_\textrm{GNR}\simeq 236$. Although a few impressive transistor key figures,  were reported, degraded carrier mobility within the $\simeq$ 50--200 cm$^{2}$V$^{-1}$s$^{-1}$ range were also seen for narrower \glspl{GNR}.

The wider bandgaps, the superior quality of edge structures, a thin gate oxide, and a short \gls{GNR} channel were among the characteristics cited by the author as essential for achieving increases of approximately $10^5$ for the $I_\textrm{on}/I_\textrm{off}$ at room temperature, as well as high current density and transconductance. The team also compared the performance of their device to that of Pd-contacted \gls{CNFET} on \SI{10}{\nano\metre} \ce{SiO2} with comparable channel lengths and variable tube diameters.

They discovered that the tube diameter was crucial in determining the current density delivered in the ON state and the leakage current in the OFF state, arguing that the negatively correlated role of a large positive \gls{SB} and a short mean free path of the carriers limited by acoustic phonons and defects seemed to be the main responsible factors~\cite{pennington2007deformation, ishiyama2015influence}.

Despite a few remarkable transistor key numbers such as $I_\textrm{on}\simeq$ 2000 \si{\micro\ampere\per\micro\metre}, $I_\textrm{on}/I_\textrm{off}\simeq 10^6$, $SS\simeq\SI{210}{\milli\volt}$~dec$^{-1}$, and $g_\textrm{m} = 900$ \si{\micro\siemens\per\micro\metre}, reduced carrier mobility within the $\mu\simeq$ \SIrange{50}{200}{\centi\metre\squared\per\volt\per\second} range was also observed for narrower ribbon. The rationale for this unexpected outcome is twofold. To begin, it is well documented in traditional solid-state semiconductors that diminishing mobilities lead to increased bandgaps~\cite{peter2010fundamentals}. Second, the authors suggested that edge effects dominate carrier transport in narrow ribbons. Wang and co workers proposed a phenomenological model in which edges, defects, and phonons were regarded as independent factors in order to quantify the contribution of edges to the overall carrier mean free path $\ell$~\cite{wang2008room}:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\ell} = \frac{1}{\ell_\textrm{edge}} + \frac{1}{\ell_\textrm{phonon}} + \frac{1}{\ell_\textrm{defect}}
\end{equation}
%
The likelihood of a carrier scattering event occuring is then estimated to be inversely proportional to the free mean path:

\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:mfp_edge}
    \ell_\textrm{edge} = \frac{k_\parallel w_\textrm{gnr}}{P k_\perp}
\end{equation}
%
As we saw in the previous section, for ribbon widths less than 20 nanometers, it is possible to approximate the ribbon's energy gap as inversely proportional to the ribbon's width, provided electron-electron correlations are neglected~\cite{stampfer2008tunable}:

\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:bandgap_width}
    E_g\simeq\frac{\hbar v_F}{w_\textrm{gnr}}
\end{equation}
%
By inserting Eq.~\ref{ch1:eq:bandgap_width} and the dispersion relation $E_k$ one into Eq.~\ref{ch1:eq:mfp_edge}, an expression for the dependence on the width of the mean free path may be written as:

\begin{equation}\label{ch1:eq:mfp_edge_backscattering}
    \ell_\textrm{edge} = \frac{w}{P}\sqrt{\qty(1 + \frac{E_k}{E_g})^2 - 1}
\end{equation}
%
According to Eq.~\ref{ch1:eq:mfp_edge_backscattering}, an increase in width and/or an improvement in edge quality should lead to a rise in the mean free path. These hypotheses were proven experimentally, as wider sub-10 nm ribbons with greater mobilities were measured~\cite{wang2008room, geng2017graphene}.

\co{Energy Band-gap Engieering}
The experimental validation of the theoretically predicted relationship between ribbon width and energy gap occurred years. In 2007, Philip Kim's team demonstrated that the theoretical description lacked a few characteristics that were apparent in experiments by using micrometer-long, sub-100-nanometer-wide graphene nanoribbons produced by lithograpic means~\cite{Han2007}.

The experiments they perfomed suggested that even if the transport could--in principle--be ballistic disorder effects may produce charge localisations, and this effect becomes greater as the ribbon's length increases~\cite{areshkin2007ballistic, martin2009transport, chen2007graphene}. This hypothesis was later confirmed by a detailed analysis conducted by the Manchester group, which demonstrated that the electron--electron interactions between electrons within the dots are not negligible when the ribbon's width is reduced and that the conductance was suppressed due to Coulomb blockade, which also explained the deviation of larger energy gaps for small ribbons compared to theoretical studies~\cite{martin2009transport, ponomarenko2008chaotic}.

In an attempt to empirically resolve this conundrum, Kim's group suggests that the explanation may be found in a combination between those works. Hopping charge transfer mechanism through localised states of comparable size to the ribbon's width would result in a transport gap as opposed to a true energy gap~\cite{han2010electron}. The empirically measured bandgap, i.e. the gate voltage region $\Delta V_\text{G}$ where conductance is suppressed, may then be related to the single-particle energy gap via~\cite{castroneto2009}:
\begin{equation}
    E_g = \hbar v_F\sqrt{2\pi C_\textrm{G} \Delta V_\text{G}/\abs{e}}
\end{equation}
%
where $C_\textrm{G}$ is the capacitive coupling between the ribbon and the gate.

In addition, the authors measured the temperature dependence of the conductance and reported a strong suppression of the conductance minimum $G_\textrm{min}$, which suggests a thermally activated process is involved in the formation of the energy barrier between the valence and conduction bands, contrary to what would be predicted according to the Coulomb blockade model~\cite{beenakker1991theory}. Even with different ribbon widths, the scientists discovered a linear scaling of the conductance $G = \sigma(W - W_0)/L$ when this was normalised across an active transport region $(W - W_0)$, being $\sigma\simeq \SI{1.7}{\milli\siemens}$ the sheet conductivity. Comparing similar trends reported for \gls{SB}-restricted junctions led to the conclusion that the graphene nanoribbons-metal connection may have been limited by the formation of a Schottky barrier that dominates the charge injection~\cite{DiBartolomeo2016}.


Utilizing pure carbon nanotubes and compressing them under high pressure to make graphene nanoribbons has recently yielded excellent device performance~\cite{chen2021sub}. The nanotube samples were loaded and sealed in a sample chamber positioned in the centre of a pre-indented tungsten gasket, which was then compressed between two diamond anvils from ambient pressure to \SI{22.8}{\giga\pascal} while the structural evolution was monitored by in-situ Raman spectroscopy through the diamond window. The authors observed a resonant radial breathing mode peak at 116 cm$^{-1}$, corresponding to a nanotube with a diameter  $\simeq\SI{2.1}{\nano\metre}$, dropping in peak intensity as the pressure climbed, and finally becoming practically invisible when the pressure surpassed 5.5 GPa. The peak position shift and absence of the radial breathing mode was attributed to an irreversible structural transition in nanotubes that occurs at high pressure and affects their sub-band energy gaps.

The authors found a remarkable on/off ratio of approximately four orders of magnitude at extremely low bias. This was criticised by the scientific community, as ratios in this range for such modest biases have only previously been reported for carbon nanotube transistors and never for graphene nanoribbon devices~\ref{tab:CNFET}. In addition to this criticism, the authors said that they did not observe any radial-breathing-like mode. Previous research on seven- and nine-atom-wide armchair GNRs generated by on-surface synthesis indeed revealed a low-frequency radial-breathing-like mode in the Raman spectra using 532 and 785 nm excitation lasers, respectively.~\cite{talirz2017surface}.

The device they fabricated was a standard back-gated silicon dioxide type of the device which showed p-type on state conductivity of about:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma = \frac{G_\textrm{sd}L_\textrm{ch}}{w_\textrm{gnr}} = 7.42~\text{mS}
\end{equation}
%
for a channel of width of (w$_\textrm{gnr}\simeq \SI{2.8}{\nano\metre}$). They further test the relationship between the carrier mobilities for increasingly wider ribbon's widths up to 10 nm. The experiments reported hole mobilities\footnote[2]{Calculated according to standard FET theory $\mu = \frac{g_\textrm{m}L_\textrm{ch}}{C_\textrm{G}V_\textrm{SD}}$~\cite{sze2021physics}} of about $\mu\simeq$ \SI{2,443}{\centi\metre\squared\per\volt\per\second} for the \SI{2.8}{\nano\metre} wide and $\simeq$ \SI{3.776}{\centi\metre\squared\per\volt\per\second} for the \SI{10}{\nano\metre} wide, which are currently among the highest values observed, despite possible uncontrolled scattering events of the carriers with the non-spherical bulb-like ends cross at the two sides.


\co{Bottom-up GNRFET}

Another issue in creating reproducible devices is transferring ribbon onto a substrate to measure its electrical characteristics. Because stamping methods that rely on mechanical contact of the ribbon growth and transfer substrate frequently result in poor-performing or irreproducible \glspl{GNRFET}~\cite{cai2010atomically}, Michael Crommie's group developed a robust transfer employing a stack of PMMA/\gls{GNR}/Au/Mica~\cite{Bennett2013}. Poly-methyl methacrylate (PMMA) was first spun-cast onto \glspl{GNR} and baked to generate a PMMA/GNR/Au/Mica stack, which was then floated on concentrated HF (40\%), causing the mica substrate to delaminate from the Au growth layer. The leftover PMMA/GNR/Au film stack was then rinsed twice in water before being put to Au etchant. Finally, the PMMA/GNR film was washed and drawn onto a silicon substrate before being baked to eliminate any remaining water. Finally, acetone was used to remove PMMA, leaving GNRs on an insulating surface.


At $V_\textrm{SD}=\SI{1}{\volt}$, the resultant transistor displayed gate-modulated conductance with on-currents ranging from tens of pA to a few nA. The authors reported that the electrical transport was mostly dominated by a significant Schottky barrier $\phi_\textrm{B}\simeq\SI{1.25}{\electronvolt}$, which was caused by adopting a much narrower ribbon with a wider bandgap. They also reported that, despite the fact that the contact between the ribbons and Pd must have been only a few to perhaps ten nanometers long, they were able to measure $I_\textrm{on}/I_\textrm{off}\simeq 10^3$ at $V_\textrm{SD} = \SI{1}{V}$, even if experiment on nanotube transistors have demonstrated resistance increases as contact length is reduced past the electron mean free path\footnote[2]{In graphene and \glspl{CNT} this value is often referenced at $\ell\simeq\SI{200}{\metre}$~\cite{xia2011origins, franklin2010length}. See also Sec.~\ref{intro:sec:cnt}}. The off-state leakage was attributed to the holes tunneling through the drain barrier, and therefore being largely temperature independent but strongly sensitive on the bias, as larger ones will significantly shrink the width of the Schottky barrier.

The authors also suggested several points to ameliorate their device's criticalities. Lowering the contact work function would reduce the source conduction band barrier height and correspondingly increase the drain valence band barrier, resulting in both improved on- and off-state performance. Further improvement in band alignment should also arise through the use of wider GNRs with bangaps around $\simeq \SI{1.4}{\electronvolt}$, as lower effective mass and Schottky barriers are expected for smaller bandgaps. Lastly, the researchers envisioned that longer \glspl{GNR}~\cite{wang2017precision} might also reduce the contact resistance by increasing $L_\textrm{c}$.

The authors also proposed a certain number of measures to mitigate the device's shortcomings. Reducing the contact work function ($\phi^\textrm{Pd}\simeq$ \SIrange{5.2}{5.6}{\electronvolt}) would decrease the barrier height of the source conduction band and increase the barrier height of the drain valence band, resulting in enhanced on- and off-state performance. As effective mass and Schottky barriers are projected to be lower for smaller bandgaps, the usage of wider GNRs with bandgaps $E_g \simeq \SI{1.4}{\electronvolt}$ could also result in a further enhancement of band alignment. In conclusion, the researchers hypothesised that longer GNRs could also lower contact resistance by increasing $L_\textrm{c}$.

Using high-k dielectrics is another viable method for enhancing the properties of transistors. The usage of such dielectrics is necessitated by the fact that, when scaling down the geometrical dimensions of transistors, the oxide thickness cannot be scaled in the same manner as the other geometrical parameters, as this would result in leakage currents through the oxide. This phenomenon could also result in a field that exceeds the breakdown field of silicon dioxide. By raising the permittivity of the dielectric, one can afford a sicthicker dielectric while still achieving the same capacitance as with a thinner silicon dioxide dielectric.

Llinas and colleagues investigated the output characteristics of \glspl{GNRFET} with widths of nine and thirteen atoms that were fabricated using two distinct gate dielectrics~\cite{llinas2017short}. They compared the output response of a typical \SI{50}{\nano\metre}-thick back-gated silicon dioxide substrate to that of a high-$\kappa$ dielectric local gate (\ce{HfO2}, \gls{EOT}$ \simeq $\SI{1.5}{\nano\metre}). For the latter device at room temperature, current densities $I_\textrm{on}>\SI{1}{\micro\ampere}$ measured at $V_\textrm{SD}=$\SI{-1}{\volt} with the narrower ribbon were amongst the highest ever reported, combined with an excellent $I_\textrm{on}/I_\textrm{off}\simeq 10^5$.

Through a temperature-dependent experiment, however, the authors noted that Schottky barriers generated at the contacts again dominated transport. While the high-$\kappa$ dielectric lowers the carrier tunneling, the Au/Pd--\gls{GNR} junction leads to the formation of Schottky junctions partly due to the mismatch between the work functions of the metal and the ribbon. In order to lessen the mismatch and prevent the Schottky-dominated transport regime, adopting graphene as a contacting material could be one feasible approach~\cite{braun2021optimized, el2020controlled}.

\section{Concluding Remarks}

Graphene nanoribbons exhibit intriguing physical and chemical properties not only for technological advancement in areas where graphene and carbon nanotubes lack performance, but also for the physics they can reveal. At this time, bottom-up graphene nanoribbons have demonstrated remarkable transport and exotic properties, but these have been studied solely on the synthesis substrate. Attempting to investigate these properties at the device level will be one of the next major obstacles in the nanoribbon field. At this time, these evidences are largely unavailable and unclear.

There are three roles to which graphene nanoribbons may technologically contribute. The first application is that of sensors. Given the ability to functionalize the edges with a variety of chemical substituents, a large number of studies in various gas atmospheres have demonstrated excellent selectivities and good flexibility. A second papable field could be the integration in low-power devices, where by reducing required supply voltages, ribbons with lower bandgaps and consequently higher mobilities could be produced. A third and more enthusiastic application would be the development of dechorence-tolerant quantum hardware utilising their topological properties.
