\chapter{\label{ch:conclusions}Conclusions and Perspectives}

\minitoc

Especially over the last decades, the integration of carbon nanostructures into electronic devices has enjoyed a wide breadth of experiments, given than to find suitable materials for reducing the power consumption of silicon integrated circuits represent one of the most urgent issue of modern electronics.

To this end, I first explored the possibility of embedding a molecularly-defined graphene nanoribbon in \gls{SET}. The first motivation is \glspl{SET} are amongst the electronic devices with the lowest power consumption. In numbers, the power dissipated by a \gls{CMOS} inverter is approximately \SI{7e-5}{\watt}, whereas for a \gls{SET} is approximately \SI{13e-12}{\watt}~\cite{mishra2021power}. The second reason is that, historically, \glspl{SET} have been a very successful device architecture for studying quantum properties of \glspl{CNT}, and (2) understanding their fundamental issues, which have led to the realisation of the first 16-bit \gls{CNT} microprocessor~\cite{hills2019modern}.

Using the temperature protocol developed in Ch.~\ref{ch:instrumentation_development}, I have presented the first experimental evidence of a molecular \gls{SET} that can work up to room temperature based on a molecularly-defined graphene nanoribbons. The charging energy $E_C$ overcomes the thermal fluctuations even at $T=\SI{290}{\kelvin}$, leading to the observation of Coulomb oscillations. I then revelead three major transport regimes for low-energy excitations.

At cryogenic temperatures, Coulomb-oscillation heights are essentially invariants, and I believe this is due to Coulomb blockade. Power-law scaling in $T$ are found from \SI{1}{\kelvin} to approximately \SI{80}{\kelvin}, while Coulomb-oscillation widths are proportional to $T$. Similar scalings have been reported in the literature, and explained with the presence of finite-range Coulomb interactions in 1D systems. Because I did not observe an exponential decay of the conductance $G\sim T^{-\beta\phi}$, I exclude thermally-activated processes~\cite{sols2007coulomb}.

I interpreted the power-law scaling as the tunneling event through a Tomonaga--Luttinger liquid phase within the correlated-sequential-tuneling framework. Plasmon modes can be also excited during the tunneling event, and this intuition is used to qualitatively rationalise the different power-law exponents measured for each charge transistion. To test this hypothesis, I then estimated a mean tunneling exponent from the impedance of an ideal transmission line associated with the corresponding plasmon oscillations, and recovered a value very close to what observed experimentally, i.e. $\alpha_\textrm{end}\simeq 0.5$. At high temperatures, scattering due to graphene flexural phonons become dominant, destroying the Tomonaga--Luttinger liquid phase. These results are in overall agreement with a non-linear dispersion of the low-energy excitations, in contrast to metallic carbon nanotubes.

In an attempt to search for an explanation of the ultra-clean features observed, I then tested two \glspl{MGNR} where different functionalisations were grafted onto the edges for improving the dispersion in solution. The ribbons decorated with alkyl chains displayed p-doped semiconducting behaviour, whereas the \glspl{MGNR} carrying sterically-hindred groups were systematically observed associated with \gls{SET} devices. Albeit I do not have the statistical power to assess the validity this result, I provide evidences why I the crucial role of these bulky groups is quite plausible.

Firstly, I was able to observe a strong suppression of the tunneling current at low bias, in combination with vibron-assisted excitations, using \si{\milli\kelvin} transport spectroscopy, which was explained within the Franck--Condon conductance blockade model. This also elucidated the small steady increase of the Coulomb oscillations at very low temperatures. This evidence was qualitatively supported by an \textit{in-silico} experiment, where the phonon dispersion of a \gls{MGNR} with and without maleimide groups was calculated. The results suggest that the vibrational modes in \gls{MGNR} are more strongly coupled to the electric charge than was previously reported for a certain class of devices built with  suspended carbon nanotubes. I therefore suggest that the understanding of mechanical and quantum vibrations is crucial when frequencies in the range \si{\mega\hertz}--\si{\giga\hertz} are sent to excite and manipulate spins~\cite{vincent2012electronic, gaita2019molecular, escalera2018spin}, and that a rational design of the lattice vibrations by modification of the \gls{MGNR} topology could, in principle, increase the spin--phonon coupling, leading to predicted long coherence times~\cite{palyi2012spin}.

Lastly, I have attempted to fabricate another fundamental electronic component, namely a p--n junction. Carbon nanotubes have firstly paved the way by demonstrating the possibility of doping its hallow structure. However, the extreme difficulty of realising n-type of doping, and seprating semiconducting nanotubes from those metallic have slowed down the process have always been critical for the \gls{CNT} technology. I then addressed the feasibility of this task, by simulating the engineering of the electronic band structure using \gls{DFT} when functional decorations acting as donors or acceptors of electrons are placed onto the edges.

I found that, at the level of accuracy I simulated, the energy shifts obtained with the introduction of the dopant atoms seems to have a weak effect on the bandgap. I did not test how its shrinkage is affected by changing the ribbon width but fixing the chemical group, and as such I recommend to explore more about it in the future. I expect to see a negligible effects of the dopant atom for smaller ribbon width, but my predictions could be confuted by increasingly strong many-body correlations.

The weak effect of the dopant atoms onto the bandgap was, partially, confirmed. I testes the effect of the edge-doping by measuring \glspl{MGNR} predicted to have the biggest bandgap differences compared with the base case. The experiments showed that the device performance do not change, but that, statistically, it is more likely to observe a drastic decrease of the conductance for more positive gate voltages when methoxy groups are introduced, a confirming p-type doping effects. At this stage is not clear if this effect can be truly assigned to the functional groups and more tests, perhaps in different atmospheres, are required.

The difficulty of carrying out edge-doping is addressed by measuring another class of very promising emerging materials, i.e.\ \glspl{pGNR}, where the dopant atoms is introduced thorugh a porphyrin directly into the honeycomb lattice of the \gls{MGNR}. The motivations come from the fact that (1) fused porphyrin oligomers (also called \textit{tape}) have displayed an increase in the conductance for longer tapes, an observation rationalised with the bandgap shrinkage~\cite{leary2018bias}, and (2) that magnetic porphyrins attached to\glspl{GNR} are able to retain their magnetic fingerprint~\cite{he2017fusing}, not compromising those topological states intensively sought-after for the creation of robust molecular qubits. The results are encouraging: the porphyrin embedded in the \gls{MGNR} topology results in \gls{FET} where both n and p-doping can be induced, fast switching behaviour can be observed, and field-effect mobilities are in line with what expected from \glspl{FET} at ambient conditions.

In conclusion, we have simultaneously developed several lines of research focused on the study of electron transport in \glspl{MGNR}. With this approach, the experimental results have offered (1) a direction to the synthetic design of the materials towards novel compounds with improved properties, such as the addition of 3D groups grafted onto the ribbon edges; (2) the development of new instrumentation and new measurement protocols to deepen our knowledge of the fundamental  properties of molecularly-made systems; (3) the realisation of two first-of-their-kind electronic devices. I believe these findings can be used for investigating further quantum effects such as spin states, topological effects, and strongly-correlated phases in molecular materials. These findings provide a picture of the transport physics, as well as the first instructions for overcoming long-standing hurdles that have hampered the development of carbon-based electrical devices, and will have an appreciable influece on the development of the first nanoribbon-based microprocessors.
